# 169 WORDS THAT MEAN THE SAME — near-words grouped by what they do
A reader who knows only letters cannot see that cold and chilly are
one subject, or that a warm pan and a searing pan are not. This file
holds the groups themselves: what the members of each group share,
where they disagree, and the whole company listed on one line so it
can be carried in one grip.

ESSENCE: heat words are one ladder — warm at the bottom, scalding at
  the top — and cold words are the same ladder read downward. The
  pick is not flavor but a claim about how much: a warm bath and a
  scalding bath are two different injuries.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / near-words grip one spot at different
  amounts.
CANNOT: no swapping up or down a ladder without changing the report
  — "the pan is warm" and "the pan is searing" send a hand to two
  different places.
THREAD: cooking (02: doneness is a temperature ladder with names),
  medicine (06: a fever is graded, never merely present).
MEANS-SAME: hot warm heated scalding boiling searing roasting sweltering baking | cold cool chilly freezing frigid icy frosty bitter
ASKED-AS: hot warm cold cool temperature words scalding freezing chilly another word for

ESSENCE: wet words say how much water and how far it went — damp is
  a surface, soaked is all the way through, drenched adds that it
  happened fast and from outside. Dry words run the same road
  backwards, from moist through parched.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / one condition, several handles, each
  gripping a different depth.
CANNOT: no wet word without a claim about depth — a damp shirt and a
  soaked shirt are two different decisions about changing.
THREAD: building (15: damp in a wall is not the same job as a
  flood), cooking (02: a moist crumb and a soggy crumb are a pass and
  a fail).
MEANS-SAME: wet damp moist soggy soaked drenched sodden saturated dripping waterlogged | dry arid parched dehydrated crisp withered dessicated
ASKED-AS: wet damp soaked drenched dry parched moist soggy how much water

ESSENCE: hard words say a thing keeps its shape under a push — firm
  gives a little, rigid gives nothing, brittle gives nothing and then
  breaks. Soft words say the opposite: it takes the shape of whatever
  presses it and gives the shape back or does not.
ROOT: physics (01) / a material's answer to a load is its identity.
CANNOT: no hardness without a push to resist — a thing alone on a
  table is neither hard nor soft until something presses it.
THREAD: building materials (15: stiff and strong are different
  properties), cooking (02: a set custard and a runny one).
MEANS-SAME: hard firm solid rigid stiff dense tough brittle unyielding | soft yielding pliable supple springy limp spongy mushy tender
ASKED-AS: hard soft firm rigid stiff brittle squashy tender words for texture

ESSENCE: speed words split on two different questions — how much
  ground per hour (quick, swift, rapid) and how soon it began
  (prompt, sudden, immediate). Sluggish says slow and blames it,
  steady says slow and praises it.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / a near-word can carry a verdict the
  plain word does not.
CANNOT: no speed word without a distance or a delay behind it — a
  sudden thing may be slow once it starts, and a swift thing may
  have taken all day to begin.
THREAD: sport (08: reaction time and top speed are trained apart),
  logistics (39: transit time is not the same as lead time).
MEANS-SAME: fast quick rapid swift speedy brisk hasty sudden immediate prompt | slow sluggish gradual leisurely plodding dawdling delayed unhurried
ASKED-AS: fast quick slow rapid swift sudden sluggish gradual speed words

ESSENCE: size words are always measured against a standard that is
  never spoken — a big mouse is smaller than a small horse. Huge and
  vast differ in shape, huge in bulk and vast in spread; tiny and
  slight differ in what is small, the whole thing or its weight.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / a word is aimed by the company it is
  standing in.
CANNOT: no size word without a standard beside it — "large" alone
  names no size at all, only a comparison with something unsaid.
THREAD: measurement (36: a number needs a unit for the same reason),
  design (28: scale is read against the body of the viewer).
MEANS-SAME: big large huge enormous vast immense massive giant bulky colossal | small little tiny minute slight petite miniature compact
ASKED-AS: big large small huge tiny enormous vast size words compared to what

ESSENCE: strength words are all about resisting, and they differ by
  what is resisted — a strong rope resists pulling, a strong wall
  resists pushing, a strong argument resists a reply, a strong drink
  resists dilution. Weak words are the same list read as failure.
ROOT: physics (01) / strength is a load carried without failing, so
  the load has to be named.
CANNOT: no strength without a named load — strong in tension can be
  weak in bending, and a beam does not know it is being praised.
THREAD: engineering (04: a rated part is rated for one kind of
  force), persuasion (22: a strong case is one a counter cannot bend).
MEANS-SAME: strong sturdy robust powerful mighty tough hardy durable resilient | weak feeble frail flimsy fragile brittle faint failing
ASKED-AS: strong weak sturdy robust powerful fragile flimsy tough words for strength

ESSENCE: clean words say what was removed and dirty words say what
  arrived. Wiped removes loose dirt, scrubbed removes stuck dirt,
  sterile removes life itself — and grubby, filthy and contaminated
  are three quite different amounts of trouble.
ROOT: cleaning (18) / clean is not a state but the absence of one
  named thing.
CANNOT: no clean without a named thing removed — clean of dust is
  not clean of germs, and a wiped bench can still poison a meal.
THREAD: medicine (06: sterile field is a legal standard, not a
  compliment), chemistry (03: pure means pure of a stated impurity).
MEANS-SAME: clean spotless washed scrubbed sterile pure pristine hygienic rinsed | dirty filthy grubby soiled stained grimy contaminated foul
ASKED-AS: clean dirty spotless filthy sterile grubby contaminated words for cleanliness levels

ESSENCE: light words say how much reaches the eye — dim, glowing,
  bright, dazzling, blinding form one ladder, and dark words are the
  same ladder downward. Gloomy and murky carry a mood the plain
  words do not, and shadowy says light is present but blocked.
ROOT: light and seeing (34) / brightness is a quantity, and the eye
  reports ratios rather than amounts.
CANNOT: no darkness without something to fail to see — a sealed box
  is not dark to anything except an eye placed inside it.
THREAD: photography (34: exposure is this ladder, in numbers),
  design (28: a value scale is the same ladder in paint).
MEANS-SAME: bright light lit glowing radiant dazzling blinding luminous glaring | dark dim dusky shadowy gloomy murky unlit pitch
ASKED-AS: bright dark dim glowing dazzling gloomy shadowy words for light levels

ESSENCE: loudness words carry two things at once — how much sound
  and what kind. A roar, a blare and a crack are all loud and none
  is the same noise. On the quiet side, hushed says someone is
  keeping it down; silent says there is nothing there.
ROOT: music (10) / loudness and timbre are separate properties that
  ordinary words fuse into one.
CANNOT: no silence in a place with a listener and a moving thing —
  quiet is a small amount of sound, never none.
THREAD: physics (01: sound is pressure, and pressure has no zero in
  air), medicine (06: a murmur is heard by kind before by loudness).
MEANS-SAME: loud noisy deafening thunderous booming blaring piercing shrill raucous | quiet soft hushed muted faint muffled silent still
ASKED-AS: loud quiet noisy deafening hushed silent faint muffled words for volume

ESSENCE: smooth and rough are about what a finger or a wheel meets on
  the way across — polished and glassy say the bumps are gone, silky
  says they are small and even, and coarse, jagged and craggy say
  how big and how sharp the bumps that remain are.
ROOT: craft (29) / a surface is finished to a size of remaining bump,
  and the name of the finish is that size.
CANNOT: no smoothness at every scale — sand a board to glass and it
  is still mountains to a microscope.
THREAD: engineering (04: friction and tolerance are surface
  roughness in numbers), the deep earth (33: erosion is roughness
  being spent).
MEANS-SAME: smooth even polished sleek glassy silky level slick burnished | rough coarse jagged bumpy uneven gritty rugged craggy abrasive
ASKED-AS: smooth rough polished coarse jagged bumpy gritty surface texture words

ESSENCE: sharp means the same push is put through a smaller place.
  Keen and honed say more of that, blunt and dull say less — which
  is why one word cuts and the other bruises, using the identical
  arm behind it.
ROOT: physics (01) / pressure is force divided by area.
CANNOT: no cutting without a small area to press through — a blunt
  edge does not cut weakly, it does something else entirely.
THREAD: cooking (02: a dull knife crushes a tomato it should slice),
  medicine (06: sharp injury and blunt injury are two casualties).
MEANS-SAME: sharp keen pointed edged honed piercing serrated barbed | blunt dull rounded worn chipped flattened unsharpened
ASKED-AS: sharp blunt dull keen pointed edge words honed serrated knife

ESSENCE: weight words are about what the carrying costs — heavy,
  hefty and leaden add the strain of holding; light, airy and
  buoyant take it away. The same words are lent to work, to news and
  to music, and the loan carries the strain along with it.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / a word carried to new ground brings
  the old feeling with it.
CANNOT: no weight without a pull to be weighed against — the same
  mass is heavy on the ground and weightless in orbit, unchanged.
THREAD: physics (01: mass is a property, weight is a relationship),
  story (27: heavy news and light news are told differently).
MEANS-SAME: heavy weighty hefty leaden dense laden burdensome ponderous | light weightless airy buoyant slight portable feathery
ASKED-AS: heavy light weighty hefty leaden airy buoyant words for weight

ESSENCE: tight and loose are about how much travel is left before a
  thing takes the load. Snug is contact with no strain, taut is
  strain with no travel, and slack, wobbly and rattling are three
  sizes of gap that has not closed yet.
ROOT: engineering (04) / play is the free motion before a joint
  begins to carry.
CANNOT: no tightening without a load taken up — turning a nut past
  contact does not add grip, it starts breaking the thread.
THREAD: sewing (29: ease is deliberate looseness), music (10: string
  tension is pitch).
MEANS-SAME: tight snug taut firm secure clamped fastened constricting | loose slack baggy free wobbly undone rattling flapping
ASKED-AS: tight loose snug taut slack wobbly clamped fastened words for fit

ESSENCE: full and empty are both said about containers, and both
  hide the question "of what". Brimming and crammed differ in what
  is being filled — brimming is a liquid at the rim, crammed is
  solids that have been pushed. Empty, bare and vacant differ in
  what is expected to be there.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / a word about a container carries an
  unspoken expectation about its contents.
CANNOT: no empty without an expectation to disappoint — an empty
  seat is a fact about a person who is not in it.
THREAD: logistics (39: a full truck may be full by weight or by
  volume, never both), the mind (20: a vacancy is felt as a shape).
MEANS-SAME: full filled packed crammed brimming stuffed loaded laden overflowing | empty bare hollow vacant drained depleted deserted unoccupied
ASKED-AS: full empty packed crammed brimming bare hollow vacant words for capacity

ESSENCE: old splits into three unrelated claims that share one word —
  how many years (ancient, aged), how worn (shabby, decrepit) and
  how out of use (obsolete, outdated). A new thing can be shabby and
  an ancient thing can be pristine.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / one handle serving three questions is
  cheap, and the listener sorts it by company.
CANNOT: no age from wear — a hard year and a hundred easy years leave
  the same marks and are not the same claim.
THREAD: the deep earth (33: rock is dated by layer, not by look),
  keeping knowledge (40: condition and provenance are separate fields).
MEANS-SAME: old ancient aged elderly antique vintage historic longstanding | worn shabby decrepit dilapidated battered weathered | new fresh recent modern current latest unused
ASKED-AS: old new ancient antique worn shabby obsolete modern fresh age words

ESSENCE: whole says every part is present and joined; broken says a
  join failed, and the near-words name how it failed — cracked keeps
  the pieces in place, shattered does not, snapped is clean, and torn
  followed a weakness rather than a line.
ROOT: physics (01) / a break follows the weakest path available, and
  that path leaves its signature.
CANNOT: no breaking without a flaw to start at — a break begins at
  the smallest crack, never in the middle of sound material.
THREAD: medicine (06: a fracture is named by its line), craft (29:
  a repair is judged by whether the break can be found again).
MEANS-SAME: whole intact complete unbroken entire undamaged sound | broken cracked shattered smashed split snapped fractured torn ruined
ASKED-AS: whole broken intact cracked shattered snapped torn ruined damage words

ESSENCE: rising and falling words differ in three ways — how fast
  (climb against soar, drop against plummet), whether help was
  needed (lift is done to a thing, rise is done by it), and whether
  it was expected (surge and slump both mean it was not).
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / a near-word smuggles in who acted and
  whether anybody meant it.
CANNOT: no rising without something to rise against — up is a
  direction borrowed from a pull, and in free water there is none.
THREAD: money (16: a rally and a spike are the same direction and
  different stories), weather (13: pressure words are the same set).
MEANS-SAME: rise climb ascend soar surge lift mount increase swell rally | fall drop sink descend plunge plummet decline slump collapse
ASKED-AS: rising falling climb soar plunge plummet decline slump increase drop words

ESSENCE: open and closed words differ by what is doing the blocking.
  Ajar and gaping name the size of the gap; locked, sealed and
  blocked name three different reasons a thing cannot be entered,
  and only one of them can be undone by a key.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / the near-word names the mechanism,
  and the mechanism decides what will fix it.
CANNOT: no closed without a way it could have been open — a solid
  wall is not a shut door, and calling it one hides that there is no
  way through at all.
THREAD: computing (19: closed, locked and permission denied are
  three faults), law (17: a closed hearing and a sealed record differ).
MEANS-SAME: open ajar unlocked uncovered exposed accessible parted gaping | closed shut sealed locked blocked fastened covered barred
ASKED-AS: open closed shut ajar sealed locked blocked uncovered barred words

ESSENCE: near and far are pure comparison — a distant relative and a
  distant star are the same word doing arithmetic against two very
  different rulers. Adjacent and neighbouring add touching or
  nearly touching; remote adds that getting there is hard.
ROOT: words and meaning (66) / a distance word is a ratio, and the
  ruler is left unsaid.
CANNOT: no distance word without a ruler — near in a city and near
  in a desert are an hour apart and both true.
THREAD: navigation (12: range is stated in the units of the trip),
  people together (21: a close friend is measured in contact).
MEANS-SAME: near close nearby adjacent neighbouring beside within local | far distant remote faraway outlying beyond removed
ASKED-AS: near far close distant nearby remote adjacent beyond how far words

ESSENCE: beginning and ending words differ by whether the thing was
  planned. Start and stop are neutral, launch and conclude say it was
  prepared, and halt, cease and abort say it ended before its shape
  was finished.
ROOT: story (27) / an ending is judged against the shape that was
  promised at the beginning.
CANNOT: no ending without a beginning to close — a thing that
  stopped before it started was cancelled, which is a different fact.
THREAD: engineering (04: shutdown, abort and failure are three log
  lines), music (10: a cadence is an ending that was promised).
MEANS-SAME: begin start open launch commence originate found initiate | end finish stop close conclude cease terminate halt abort
ASKED-AS: begin start end finish launch conclude stop halt cease words

ESSENCE: joining words differ by whether the join can be undone —
  glued and welded cannot, fastened and coupled can, and merged says
  the two are no longer findable. Separating words carry the same
  question backwards: unfasten is expected, sever is not.
ROOT: craft (29) / a join is defined by how it comes apart, not by
  how it went together.
CANNOT: no join without a way it fails — every fastening has a load
  at which it lets go, and the word tells you which kind of letting
  go to expect.
THREAD: chemistry (03: a bond is named by its energy to break),
  people together (21: a partnership is written for its ending).
MEANS-SAME: join attach fasten weld glue bind merge connect couple unite | separate split divide detach part sever unfasten disconnect
ASKED-AS: join attach fasten weld glue connect separate split detach sever words

ESSENCE: holding words differ in the strength and the intent —
  grip and clutch say effort, hold and keep say nothing about
  effort, support says the load is being taken. Releasing words
  differ in whether it was a choice: drop was not, relinquish was.
ROOT: martial arts (07) / a grip is a load path, and letting go is a
  decision or a failure.
CANNOT: no holding without a load being taken — a hand resting on a
  thing carries none of it, and the word for that is touching.
THREAD: engineering (04: clamping force is a number), law (17:
  custody, possession and title are three different holds).
MEANS-SAME: hold grip grasp clutch clasp keep retain carry support cradle | release let free loosen drop relinquish surrender discharge
ASKED-AS: hold grip grasp clutch keep release let go loosen drop words

ESSENCE: making words differ by how much of the shape was decided
  beforehand — build and assemble follow a plan, forge and shape are
  done in the hand, create claims nothing like it existed. Destroying
  words differ by whether the pieces survive.
ROOT: craft (29) / making is imposing a shape, and the word says who
  chose it and when.
CANNOT: no making without something to make it from — creating out
  of nothing is a claim no craft has ever supported.
THREAD: physics (01: matter is rearranged, never made), cleaning
  (18: dismantling and wrecking leave different piles).
MEANS-SAME: make build create form construct assemble produce forge shape craft | destroy wreck demolish ruin raze smash dismantle obliterate
ASKED-AS: make build create construct assemble destroy wreck demolish dismantle ruin words

ESSENCE: giving and taking are one motion described from two ends,
  and the near-words carry who consented. Donate and grant say the
  giver chose, seize and steal say the taker did, and receive says
  nothing about how it arrived.
ROOT: money (16) / a transfer has two sides, and every word for it
  is spoken from one of them.
CANNOT: no giving without a taking on the other end — a gift nobody
  accepted has not moved, whatever the giver intended.
THREAD: law (17: delivery and acceptance are separate legal acts),
  people together (21: a favour creates a debt the words hide).
MEANS-SAME: give hand offer donate grant supply provide deliver lend bestow | take seize grab remove receive accept steal collect claim
ASKED-AS: give take offer donate grant receive seize steal accept transfer words

ESSENCE: moving and resting words differ by whether anything is
  expected to change. Wait and pause say motion will resume, settle
  and idle say it may not, and remain says the thing did not move
  while others did.
ROOT: physics (01) / rest and motion are the same state seen from
  different places, so the word is about expectation.
CANNOT: no rest without a frame to be still in — a person sitting
  quietly is travelling at the speed of the planet.
THREAD: navigation (12: everything is measured against a chosen
  reference), the mind (20: stillness is noticed only against motion).
MEANS-SAME: move go travel shift stir advance proceed migrate journey | rest stay stop halt wait remain settle idle pause linger
ASKED-AS: move travel go shift rest stay wait remain pause halt words

ESSENCE: growing words differ by direction and cause — swell is
  outward pressure, spread is sideways, multiply is more of the same
  thing rather than more of one thing, develop is change of form
  rather than change of size.
ROOT: living world (31) / growth is either more cells or bigger
  cells, and the everyday words do not tell them apart.
CANNOT: no growing without a supply to grow from — every increase is
  drawn from a store, and the word never mentions the store.
THREAD: farming (14: yield is limited by whichever input runs out
  first), money (16: growth and inflation look alike in a total).
MEANS-SAME: grow expand swell increase spread multiply enlarge develop rise | shrink contract shrivel dwindle wither reduce diminish compress
ASKED-AS: grow expand swell increase spread shrink contract dwindle wither words

ESSENCE: helping and harming words differ by whether the effect was
  intended and how long it lasts. Aid and assist join work already
  happening, rescue and relieve stop a harm, and impair, spoil and
  sabotage mark the difference between accident and choice.
ROOT: right and wrong (25) / intent changes the name of the same
  outcome.
CANNOT: no help without a want being served — an unwanted help is a
  harm wearing the other word.
THREAD: medicine (06: first do no harm is a ranking of these two
  lists), law (17: negligence and malice are the same damage priced
  differently).
MEANS-SAME: help aid assist support rescue serve relieve benefit ease | harm hurt damage injure wound spoil impair sabotage worsen
ASKED-AS: help aid assist support rescue harm hurt damage injure sabotage words

ESSENCE: hiding words differ in the method — cover puts something in
  front, disguise changes the look, withhold never presents it at
  all. Showing words differ in effort: reveal removes a cover, display
  arranges for looking, demonstrate makes the thing perform.
ROOT: persuasion (22) / what is not shown is chosen as carefully as
  what is.
CANNOT: no hiding without a watcher to hide from — concealment is a
  relationship, and a thing in an empty room is merely somewhere.
THREAD: strategy (23: cover, concealment and deception are three
  distinct tasks), design (28: display is composition, not exposure).
MEANS-SAME: hide conceal bury mask cloak disguise cover screen withhold | show reveal display expose uncover disclose exhibit demonstrate
ASKED-AS: hide conceal cover disguise withhold show reveal display expose uncover

ESSENCE: asking words carry how much power the asker has — request
  and inquire assume none, demand assumes some, beg assumes none and
  says so. Answering words carry whether the answer settled anything:
  reply is any words back, confirm is agreement, retort is a fight.
ROOT: people together (21) / every question is also a move about
  standing, not only about information.
CANNOT: no answer without a question it fits — words that arrive
  after a question and do not fit it are a change of subject.
THREAD: law (17: a demand starts a clock a request does not),
  persuasion (22: the form of the ask sets the range of the reply).
MEANS-SAME: ask question query inquire request demand beg petition probe | answer reply respond retort explain state confirm acknowledge
ASKED-AS: ask question query request demand answer reply respond confirm explain words

ESSENCE: the split between knowing and guessing is not confidence but
  grounds. Know, prove and confirm say something stands behind the
  claim; guess, suspect and reckon say something does not; assume
  and believe say the question was skipped entirely.
ROOT: evidence (36) / a claim is worth its support, and the verb
  reports the support.
CANNOT: no knowing without something that could have shown it wrong
  — a claim no observation could dent is a belief in a knowing coat.
THREAD: chance (35: a probability is a stated amount of not
  knowing), law (17: suspicion, belief and proof are three thresholds).
MEANS-SAME: know understand grasp realise recognise prove confirm establish verify | guess suppose suspect assume reckon estimate speculate believe
ASKED-AS: know guess suppose suspect assume believe prove confirm certain unsure words

ESSENCE: wanting words differ by how badly and how respectably —
  wish is idle, need claims necessity, crave admits the wanting is
  running things. Refusing words differ by what is being refused:
  decline is the offer, deny is the claim, resist is the force.
ROOT: the mind (20) / a want is a pull with a name attached
  afterwards.
CANNOT: no refusal without an offer or a claim in front of it —
  refusing needs something already on the table.
THREAD: persuasion (22: a no to the offer is not a no to the goal),
  right and wrong (25: need is the word that ends arguments, so it
  is the word most often borrowed).
MEANS-SAME: want wish desire crave long need seek covet fancy | refuse decline reject deny renounce resist spurn rebuff
ASKED-AS: want wish need desire crave refuse decline reject deny resist words

ESSENCE: keeping and losing words differ by whether anyone chose.
  Save and preserve are deliberate, hoard says too much of it,
  misplace is an accident, forfeit was a rule, and squander was a
  choice the speaker disapproves of.
ROOT: keeping knowledge (40) / what survives is what someone decided
  to keep, and the words record the deciding.
CANNOT: no keeping without a cost to keep it — every store is paid
  for in space, care or spoilage, and the word never names the bill.
THREAD: money (16: holding has a carrying cost), farming (14: a
  harvest kept badly is a harvest lost slowly).
MEANS-SAME: keep hold store save preserve retain hoard maintain stockpile | lose drop misplace forfeit waste squander shed mislay
ASKED-AS: keep save store preserve hoard lose misplace waste squander forfeit words

ESSENCE: one trade has two names, and which word is used says which
  side of the counter is speaking. Purchase and procure add
  formality, bid says the price is not yet fixed, auction says the
  seller is letting buyers set it, and offload says the seller
  wanted out.
ROOT: money (16) / every trade is one event recorded twice, once as
  a buy and once as a sell.
CANNOT: no buying without a selling on the other side — a purchase
  with no seller is a taking, and the words for that are elsewhere.
THREAD: law (17: offer and acceptance are the two halves), keeping
  knowledge (40: double entry exists because of this doubling).
MEANS-SAME: buy purchase acquire procure order pay bid | sell vend market auction trade supply offload liquidate
ASKED-AS: buy sell purchase acquire order sell auction trade price words

ESSENCE: eating and drinking words are graded by speed and manners —
  nibble, eat and devour are one ladder, sip, drink and guzzle
  another, and graze and feast are about the pattern over time
  rather than the mouthful.
ROOT: the human journey (37) / eating is watched by others, so its
  words carry judgement more than measurement.
CANNOT: no eating word without a manner attached — there is no
  neutral verb for it in ordinary speech, only degrees of approval.
THREAD: medicine (06: intake is measured where language judges),
  people together (21: table manners are enforced by these words).
MEANS-SAME: eat chew bite swallow devour nibble gobble graze feast consume | drink sip gulp swig quaff guzzle slurp swallow
ASKED-AS: eat drink chew swallow devour nibble sip gulp guzzle feast words

ESSENCE: cooking words split into two lists that share a kitchen —
  the ways of applying heat, which differ by the carrier (air, fat,
  water, steam) and its temperature; and the ways of combining,
  which differ by how much air is wanted in the result.
ROOT: cooking (02) / a method is a carrier of heat plus a
  temperature, and everything else is timing.
CANNOT: no boiling above the boiling point — water pins the
  temperature until it has all left, which is why boiled and roasted
  can never taste alike.
THREAD: chemistry (03: browning needs a temperature water forbids),
  physics (01: heat moves faster through fat and water than air).
MEANS-SAME: cook bake roast fry sear grill broil boil simmer poach steam braise stew | mix stir whisk beat fold knead blend cream
ASKED-AS: cook bake roast fry boil simmer steam braise whisk fold stir words

ESSENCE: cutting words differ by the tool and by what the material
  did. Slice, chop and dice are a blade and a size; saw is repeated
  small cuts; tear, rip and split are the material failing along its
  own weakness with no edge involved.
ROOT: craft (29) / a cut is a controlled failure, and the word names
  how much control there was.
CANNOT: no clean cut in a material with a grain — wood, meat and
  cloth decide the line unless the edge is sharp enough to overrule
  them.
THREAD: cooking (02: against the grain is the whole instruction),
  surgery (06: incision and laceration are the same wound, made
  differently).
MEANS-SAME: cut slice chop dice mince shred carve saw slit sever trim | tear rip split cleave snap break shatter
ASKED-AS: cut slice chop dice shred carve saw tear rip split words

ESSENCE: cleaning words are sorted by what carries the dirt away —
  wipe and dust move it, wash and rinse dissolve and float it, scrub
  and scour break its grip first, and polish removes the surface
  rather than the dirt.
ROOT: cleaning (18) / dirt is matter in the wrong place, and every
  method is a way of giving it somewhere else to be.
CANNOT: no cleaning without moving dirt somewhere else — a clean
  room is a dirty cloth, a used tank or a full bin.
THREAD: chemistry (03: a detergent works by letting water hold oil),
  physics (01: order here is paid for by disorder there).
MEANS-SAME: clean wash rinse scrub wipe mop sweep dust polish scour launder | tidy clear sort arrange straighten declutter
ASKED-AS: clean wash rinse scrub wipe mop sweep polish tidy clear words

ESSENCE: fixing words differ by how much of the original is left.
  Mend and patch keep the part and add to it, repair restores the
  function, overhaul replaces whatever is worn whether it failed or
  not, and replace keeps nothing but the place.
ROOT: engineering (04) / maintenance is chosen against the cost of
  the failure it prevents.
CANNOT: no fixing without knowing what failed — replacing a part
  that was not the fault leaves the fault in place with a new bill.
THREAD: medicine (06: treating a symptom is patching), computing
