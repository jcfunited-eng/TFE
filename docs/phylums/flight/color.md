## THINGS
The units this subject is about. Each sits in many phylums at once;
its other homes are named so a cascade can branch.

- AIR — the ocean we stand at the bottom of: 1.2 kg in every cubic
  meter at sea level, thinning as you climb, pressing ten tonnes on
  every square meter of ground. Air and water are the same physics
  at different thickness; also: weather, earth, chemistry, the
  body (breath is pressure difference).
- THE WING — a tilted surface that bends passing air downward and
  is thrown upward in return. A sail is a wing stood upright, a
  propeller is wings on a carousel, a rotor is a wing that carries
  its own airflow; also: animals, transport, design, play (the
  kite, the paper dart).
- THE FOUR FORCES — lift against weight, thrust against drag; in
  steady flight nothing is left over. Lift is air thrown down and
  the wing thrown up; drag is the fluid's toll, the churned wake
  its receipt; thrust is fluid thrown backward — propeller, jet,
  wingbeat, and rocket differ only in how much and how fast, for
  there is no recoilless push; also: physics, transport, movement,
  animals (squid and jellyfish are jets in flesh).
- THE LIGHT GASES AND HOT AIR — hydrogen, helium, heated air: the
  lifters that need no speed at all, priced per cubic meter in
  SCIENCE; also: chemistry, physics.
- THE BALLOON AND THE AIRSHIP — flight by weighing less than the
  air pushed aside; also: weather (they ride the wind and cannot
  argue with it), history.
- THE HELICOPTER — hover bought at enormous power; the machine
  chosen for access, not efficiency; also: transport, survival
  (rescue), engineering.
- THE CONTROLS — ailerons roll, elevator pitches, rudder yaws:
  three axes, coupled, so flying is a coordination and not three
  levers; also: mathematics, movement.
- BIRDS, BATS, AND INSECTS — the original fliers and the original
  ledger: every trick in this phylum, they found first; also:
  animals, life.
- THE ATMOSPHERE'S THINNING — density falls roughly by half every
  5,500 m; less than a third remains at airliner height; also:
  weather, earth, the body (thin air and blood).
- THE RUNWAY — prepared ground long enough to reach flying speed
  and to stop again if something fails; the runway, not the
  aircraft, sets what can operate where; also: building, transport.
- THE KITE — the oldest flying machine: lift and drag balanced
  against a tether; also: play, history.

## CLAIMS
What holds, written to be tested by a crossing query. Worth is
applicability to the query at hand, never absolute.

- Lift is deflection: a wing meets air at a slight tilt and bends
  the flow downward; the air, thrown down, throws the wing up. The
  pressure difference across the wing IS that throw written in
  fluid — one force, two descriptions, the same ledger read twice.
- The honest Bernoulli: in smooth flow, faster means lower
  pressure — because the fluid sped up only where pressure behind
  exceeded pressure ahead; the low reading is that spent
  difference. Pressure is stored push, speed is spent push; their
  sum along the stream holds.
- A wing stalls at an angle, not at a speed — past roughly 15° of
  tilt the air cannot follow the upper curve, tears away into
  churn, and lift collapses in a heartbeat. Fast, slow, level, or
  diving. The speed people quote is only the speed at which LEVEL
  flight needs that angle.
- Drag grows with the square of speed and the power to push
  through it with the cube: double the speed, four times the push,
  eight times the engine.
- A climb is not extra lift — it is extra thrust, spent on gaining
  height. This is why a heavy aeroplane on a hot day cannot climb
  rather than cannot fly.
- How far a flying thing goes is decided by lift-per-drag: glide
  angle with the engines off, fuel per mile with them on, the
  shape of the wings. An albatross gets ~20 m forward per meter of
  fall, a sparrow ~4, a sailplane 40 and more.
- Everything that flies moves by throwing air the other way — and
  for the same push, throwing a LOT of air gently wastes far less
  than a little thrown violently (worked in SCIENCE; it is why
  airliners wear enormous fans).
- Bigger fliers must fly faster: weight grows with the cube of
  size, wing area only with the square, so load per square meter
  rises with size and speed with its square root. The sparrow
  leaps into the air; the swan runs across the water.
- Hovering is the most expensive way to fly — thrust from still
  air costs the most power per newton (worked in SCIENCE); the
  hummingbird pays by eating roughly its weight in nectar a
  day (~).
- Aircraft climb because the air thins: less drag for the
  airframe, less breath for the engine; the cruising altitude is
  where those two curves cross for that aircraft.
- Fuel and payload share one weight limit: a machine that carries
  its own energy carries it as load, so range and cargo are one
  currency and every route is a point on that line.
- The medium shapes the machine: water is dense and slow, so ships
  are enormous and unhurried; air is thin and fast, so aircraft
  are light and quick and cannot pause (the mirror holds the
  machines that tried to serve two media).
- Ice on a wing is shape, not weight: a layer thinner than a coin
  changes the curve the air must follow, so the wing stalls far
  below the angle the pilot expects — and it forms in conditions
  that look mild.
- Near the ground a wing works better: the ground interrupts the
  shed vortex, drag from making lift falls, a landing aeroplane
  floats — and an overloaded one can rise in ground effect and be
  unable to climb out of it.
- Near the speed of sound the air stops getting out of the way
  smoothly: waves pile up ahead, drag rises steeply, controls
  change character. That is why fast wings are swept.
- Where the weight sits is decisive: too far forward and the
  aircraft cannot rotate; too far back and it is unstable in the
  way that cannot be recovered. Loading is a calculation, never
  putting things where they fit.
- Stability is a bought trade: a machine that returns to level
  when nudged is docile and resists its pilot; one that holds
  whatever it is pointed at is agile and must be flown every
  second — fighters are the second, kept flyable by computers.
- Navigation away from landmarks is a chain of substitutions:
  speed, heading, and time give a position that drifts; a star,
  beacon, or satellite resets it. The art is knowing how far the
  estimate has decayed since the last fix.

## SCIENCE
The math and physics of flight, held — not mentioned. Each piece is
a LAW a query can compute with, a RULE it can follow, and where it
earns it, a WORKED case run to a real answer.

### THE AIR ITSELF

LAW: air's density at sea level ≈ 1.2 kg/m³ — about 1/800 of
  water's 1,000. Density falls roughly by half every 5,500 m;
  less than a third is left at 11,000 m.
WORKED: a room 5 m × 4 m × 2.5 m holds 50 m³ of air:
  50 × 1.2 = 60 kg — a grown person's weight of air in an
  ordinary room. The medium only looks like nothing.

LAW: pressure is the weight of fluid above, shared as squeeze in
  all directions: sea-level air ~101,000 N per square meter — ten
  tonnes per square meter; every 10 m of sea water adds one more
  atmosphere. A barometer is a scale that weighs the sky.
RULE: to read height from pressure — falling pressure is climbing
  altitude; the altimeter is a barometer with its dial relabeled.

LAW: fluid moves only because pressure differs — every wind is
  the sky falling sideways from high squeeze to low. Flight
  happens inside a medium that is itself moving.

LAW: buoyancy — a fluid pushes up on anything in it by the weight
  of fluid displaced, because pressure grows with depth: the push
  from below beats the push from above by exactly that weight.

### LIFT AS AIR SHOVED DOWN — one force, two ledgers

LAW (the throw ledger): lift = mass of air worked per second ×
  downward speed given to it. The air a wing works is roughly the
  tube its span sweeps: mass per second = density × tube area ×
  flight speed.
WORKED: a 1,000-kg light plane, span 10 m, at 45 m/s. Tube area
  = π × 10² ÷ 4 ≈ 78.5 m². Air worked per second:
  1.2 × 78.5 × 45 = 4,239 kg — over four tonnes of air every
  second. Weight to hold: 1,000 × 9.8 = 9,800 N. Downward speed
  needed: 9,800 ÷ 4,239 ≈ 2.3 m/s. A huge river of air, nudged
  gently down — that is all level flight is.

LAW (the pressure ledger, same force): lift = ½ × density ×
  speed² × wing area × lift coefficient (the tilt's dial, ~0.3 at
  fast cruise up to ~1.6 with everything hanging out).
RULE: for level flight set lift = weight and solve for whichever
  quantity the question asks.
WORKED: same plane, wing area 16 m², coefficient 0.5:
  ½ × 1.2 × v² × 16 × 0.5 = 4.8 v² = 9,800 N → v² = 2,042,
  v ≈ 45 m/s (about 160 km/h). Both ledgers land on the same
  flight.

LAW: the stall floor — the coefficient tops out (~1.6) at the
  critical angle (~15°); below the speed where level flight needs
  that much, the wing cannot carry the weight at any tilt.
WORKED: same plane at coefficient 1.6:
  v² = 2 × 9,800 ÷ (1.2 × 16 × 1.6) = 19,600 ÷ 30.72 ≈ 638,
  v ≈ 25 m/s. Cruises at 45, stalls at 25 — every knot of that
  margin is the pilot's working room.

### THE SIZE LAW — why bigger birds must fly faster

LAW: weight grows with the cube of size, wing area with the
  square, so load per square meter grows in step with size — and
  since lift buys weight with speed squared, flight speed grows
  with the square root of the loading.
WORKED: a sparrow, 30 g on 0.010 m² of wing: load = 0.294 ÷
  0.010 = 29.4 N/m²; speed to carry it (coefficient 1):
  v² = 2 × 29.4 ÷ 1.2 = 49, v = 7 m/s. A swan, 10 kg on 0.6 m²:
  load = 98 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 163 N/m²; v² = 2 × 163 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 272,
  v ≈ 16.5 m/s. The swan must fly more than twice as fast just
  to stay up — why it runs across the water to take off, why it
  cannot hover, and why there is a size above which flapping
  stops being payable at all (the mirror holds who found that
  ceiling the hard way).

### DRAG'S TOLL AND THE POWER BILL

LAW: drag = ½ × density × speed² × frontal area × shape number
  (streamlined ~0.05-0.3, a flat plate ~1.2). Power = drag ×
  speed, so power grows with speed CUBED.
RULE: to re-price a speed change — square the ratio for force,
  cube it for power. Double the speed: 4× the push, 8× the
  engine.
WORKED (terminal fall): falling stops accelerating when drag
  equals weight. Skydiver, 80 kg, belly-down (area × shape
  ≈ 0.7): 784 = 0.6 × v² × 0.7 → v² = 784 ÷ 0.42 ≈ 1,867,
  v ≈ 43 m/s — about 155 km/h. Open the parachute (area × shape
  ≈ 25): v² = 784 ÷ 15 ≈ 52, v ≈ 7.2 m/s — the arithmetic of
  jumping a 2.7-m wall (52 ÷ 19.6). The parachute does not
  defeat the fall; it re-prices it.

LAW: the power to stay up = weight × speed ÷ lift-per-drag.
WORKED: the light plane at lift-per-drag 10: drag = 9,800 ÷ 10 =
  980 N; at 45 m/s the bill is 980 × 45 ≈ 44,000 W — a modest
  car engine's worth to keep a tonne aloft. An airliner:
  70,000 kg = 686,000 N, lift-per-drag 17: drag ≈ 40,400 N; at
  250 m/s ≈ 10,100,000 W — ten megawatts, every joule from the
  tanks.
RULE (the glide, same law backwards): engines off, height is the
  fuel; distance = height × lift-per-drag.
WORKED: a sailplane at 40, released at 1,000 m: 40 km of reach.
  An airliner at 17 from 11,000 m: ~190 km — a jet with both
  engines out is in trouble, not doomed.

### HOVERING — the price of standing still on air

LAW: thrust from still air = 2 × density × disc area × (induced
  speed)²; ideal power = thrust × induced speed. Small disc → the
  air must be thrown hard → the power soars. This one law is the
  helicopter's whole tragedy and the jetpack's.
WORKED: a 2,000-kg helicopter, rotor 10 m across: disc = π × 25
  ≈ 78.5 m². Thrust needed 19,600 N. Induced speed: v² =
  19,600 ÷ (2 × 1.2 × 78.5) = 19,600 ÷ 188.4 ≈ 104, v ≈ 10.2
  m/s. Ideal power = 19,600 × 10.2 ≈ 200,000 W — 200 kW before
  losses; real machines pay ~300. The same weight cruising as
  fixed-wing costs ~90 kW — hovering is roughly triple.
WORKED (why jetpacks last half a minute): 110 kg of man-plus-pack
  on ~1 m² of effective disc: v² = 1,078 ÷ 2.4 ≈ 449, v ≈ 21
  m/s; power = 1,078 × 21 ≈ 23,000 W ideal — a small car's
  engine, carried on the back, with its fuel. The law does not
  forbid it; it prices it out of usefulness.

LAW: forward flight rescues the rotor — moving feeds the disc air
  it did not have to accelerate, so power falls; then the
  advancing blade meets air at rotor speed PLUS flight speed and
  the retreating one at rotor speed MINUS it, and that asymmetry
  caps the helicopter's top speed.

### LIGHTER-THAN-AIR — the buoyancy arithmetic

LAW: lifting force = (density of air − density of filling) ×
  volume. Near 20°C, in kg/m³: air 1.20 · hydrogen 0.08 · helium
  0.17 · air heated to 100°C ≈ 0.94 (density falls with absolute
  temperature: 1.20 × 293 ÷ 373). Lift per cubic meter: hydrogen
  1.12 kg · helium 1.03 kg · hot air 0.26 kg.
RULE: to size a balloon — divide the whole load (people, basket,
  burner, AND the envelope itself) by the lift per cubic meter of
  the chosen filling.
WORKED: a 250-kg load. Hot air: 250 ÷ 0.26 ≈ 960 m³ — a sphere
  about 12 m across; this is why hot-air balloons are enormous.
  Helium: 250 ÷ 1.03 ≈ 243 m³ — a sphere under 8 m. Hydrogen:
  250 ÷ 1.12 ≈ 223 m³ — less than a tenth smaller than helium's,
  and it burns; that tenth is what the Hindenburg paid for (the
  mirror holds the receipt). Hot air lifts a quarter of helium's
  — and costs nothing but burner fuel, which is why it owns the
  sport.
RULE (the ceiling): a balloon rises until the thinning air's
  density advantage exactly equals its load — break-even IS the
  ceiling, and dropped ballast raises it.

### THRUST — throwing air backward, gently or hard

LAW: thrust = mass thrown per second × speed given to it; but the
  energy left in the wake = ½ × mass per second × that speed
  SQUARED. Same thrust two ways, very different bills.
WORKED: 100,000 N of thrust. Throw 100 kg/s at 1,000 m/s: wake
  energy = ½ × 100 × 1,000² = 50,000,000 W wasted. Throw
  1,000 kg/s at 100 m/s: ½ × 1,000 × 100² = 5,000,000 W. Same
  push, one-tenth the waste — the whole reason airliners wear
  giant fans, propellers beat jets when slow, and a whale's slow
  flukes beat a fast tail. Fighters take the first deal anyway: a
  huge fan cannot go fast.

### CLIMB, RUNWAY, AND CROSSWIND — the operating arithmetic

LAW: climb rate = excess power ÷ weight.
WORKED: the light plane needs 44 kW to hold level; the engine
  delivers ~67 kW of useful push: excess 23 kW; climb = 23,000 ÷
  9,800 ≈ 2.3 m/s. A hot high day steals ~10 kW: climb ≈ 1.3
  m/s. Add 200 kg of baggage and the excess thins toward zero —
  the aeroplane still flies; it just cannot climb.

LAW: distance to reach a speed grows with the speed SQUARED
  (distance = v² ÷ (2 × acceleration)).
WORKED: takeoff at 70 m/s under 2 m/s² of push: 4,900 ÷ 4 =
  1,225 m of runway. Heat, altitude, and weight raise the needed
  speed ~10%: 77² ÷ 4 ≈ 1,482 m — 21% more ground from 10% more
  speed, before counting the weaker engine. Hot, high, heavy:
  each word lengthens the runway by the square.

LAW: motion through a moving medium adds as arrows — path over
  ground = motion through the air + the air's own motion.
WORKED: 100 km/h of airspeed across a 20 km/h crosswind: point
  sin⁻¹(20/100) ≈ 11.5° into the wind and make good
  √(100² − 20²) ≈ 98 km/h along the intended line. Aim off or
  arrive downwind — the only choices.

LAW: air's slight stickiness is why flow follows a surface at all
  — a thin boundary layer clings and bends with the wing. It is
  the layer a stall tears loose, roughness trips early, and ice
  re-sculpts; keep it attached and everything above works.

## METHODS
How flying is done — each with what governs it, when it applies,
and what breaks it.

- FIXED-WING FLIGHT (speed rented for lift): keep speed above the
  stall floor, trade the four forces deliberately — thrust for
  height, height for speed. Breaks when: the angle passes the
  critical (recover by LOWERING the nose, the opposite of
  instinct), ice re-shapes the wing, or the loading leaves the
  weight envelope.
- GLIDING AND SOARING (borrow the sky's power): height is the
  purse, lift-per-drag the exchange rate; rising air — thermals,
  ridge wind — refills the purse. Birds invented it; the
  albatross commutes on it. Breaks when the sky stops paying and
  only the purse remains.
- BALLOONING (buoyancy steered by altitude): heat or valve to go
  up and down; for sideways, choose the wind by choosing the
  height. Breaks when weather arrives — a balloon rides the sky
  and cannot argue with it.
- ROTOR FLIGHT (a wing carrying its own airflow): hover for
  access no runway gives, paid at triple rate; engine failure is
  an exchange, not a fall — autorotation stores the descent in
  the blades and spends it in one flare. Breaks when asked for
  speed (the retreating-blade cap), or flown low and slow with
  nothing stored to spend.
- FLAPPING (the animals' method): wings that are engine and lift
  at once, riding unsteady swirls the smooth-flow law does not
  price; glorious at small size, paid worse with every doubling,
  stopping near condor size. At human size see the mirror — its
  flagship entry.
- PROPULSION BY THROW: choose by speed — big slow throw when slow
  (propeller), smaller hotter throw when fast (jet), the giant
  fan as the airliner's compromise. Breaks when propeller tips
  approach the speed of sound and the smooth air breaks first.
- NAVIGATION (dead reckoning plus fixes): run speed × heading ×
  time, admit the drift, reset on any external truth; crab into
  crosswinds by the computed triangle. The method IS the honesty
  about decay.
- FLYING THE MARGINS (the safety method): every flight is flown
  inside envelopes computed before wheels move — weight and its
  position, runway against the day's heat, fuel against the worst
  hour. The calculation is the method; the flight is its receipt.
- SEPARATION (the traffic method): keep aircraft apart in three
  dimensions and time; respect the wake, which persists minutes
  and sinks slowly. Capacity is how close is safe, nothing else.

## MEANS
What the methods run on, with the numbers that matter.

- WINGS BY SHAPE: long and slender = high lift-per-drag, the
  glider's and albatross's choice; short and stubby = agile and
  strong, the fighter's and sparrow's; swept = the transonic
  compromise. Area sets the stall floor; span sets the tube of
  air worked.
- ENGINES BY POWER-PER-KILOGRAM — the number that decides what
  can fly at all: muscle ~3 W/kg sustained (~), early petrol
  engines ~150 W/kg, modern turbofans thousands. Steam never
  crossed the line (the mirror holds it). Every era of flight is
  the era of its engine.
- FUELS BY ENERGY-PER-KILOGRAM: a flying machine carries its
  energy as load, so this number is destiny. Kerosene ~43 MJ/kg;
  today's batteries ~1 MJ/kg — even crediting the motor's better
  efficiency, ~20× behind per kilogram carried (~): why electric
  flight is short-range and the long haul still burns.
- THE LIFTING FILLINGS: hydrogen 1.12 kg of lift per m³ (burns),
  helium 1.03 (rare, inert), hot air 0.26 (free, refillable).
- THE HONEST INSTRUMENTS: airspeed from pressure difference, the
  altimeter a relabeled barometer, the gyroscope holding a
  horizon when the real one is gone, the angle vane reading the
  only number the stall cares about. Feel lies in cloud (the
  mirror holds the graveyard spiral).
- THE WIND TUNNEL — measurement's gift: the sky brought indoors,
  wings priced before anyone bets a life. The first successful
  fliers built one before they built the aeroplane.
- THE PARACHUTE: drag worn as a garment; re-prices a fall from
  155 km/h to a 2.7-m wall jump.

## PURPOSE
The wants this subject answers — the doors a query enters by.

- CROSS WHAT CANNOT BE WALKED: oceans, ranges, ice — hours where
  the surface asks weeks. The purpose that built the airliner.
- GO FAST: the speed only thin air affords — the fastest
  practical travel there is, at the steepest energy price per
  tonne (economy holds the ranking).
- SEE FROM ABOVE: survey, weather, search, war — the high ground
  made portable.
- REACH THE UNREACHABLE: the helicopter's purpose — the rooftop,
  the reef, the mountainside; access valued above efficiency.
- CARRY WHAT CANNOT WAIT: mail, organs, people in a hurry —
  flight wins exactly where the clock outranks the bill.
- PLAY AND WONDER: the kite, the paper dart, the view from the
  window seat — a want older than any cargo, born in the watching
  of birds.

## HISTORY
How the knowledge arose, in what order, at what cost — the order
carries why.

- The kite first, by centuries: flight on a string — proof that
  air can carry, long before anyone could ride it.
- The balloon next (1783), because its arithmetic is easiest:
  buoyancy asks no speed, no control, no engine — only heat and a
  bag. Lighter-than-air flew first by skipping every hard problem
  at once; flight didn't stop at balloons because a balloon
  cannot argue with the wind.
- The gliders (1800s): lift and control learned separately from
  power, on hillsides, cheaply — except Lilienthal, who paid with
  his life for the stall lesson (the mirror holds the entry). The
  tower jumpers' centuries had already taught, to anyone who
  would count, that arms are the wrong engine (the mirror's
  flagship).
- 1903, powered flight — the year the engine's watts-per-kilogram
  crossed the line AND control was solved together, by bicycle
  mechanics who built a wind tunnel first: measurement before
  machinery, the deepest lesson in the phylum.
- The airship's rise and fall: hydrogen's extra tenth of lift
  bought with fire (1937 the receipt); the airliner ended the
  argument on speed alone.
- The jet, then the fan: throw air harder, then learn to throw
  MORE air more gently — the bypass arithmetic arrived as fuel
  bills, and every airliner engine since has grown a bigger fan.
- The sound barrier (1947): a steep hill mislabeled as a wall,
  crossed with swept wings and better measurement; the label
  survives in speech (the mirror files it).
- The helicopter late, though dreamed earliest: hover's power
  bill had to wait for engines light enough to pay it.
- Human-powered flight at last (1977-79): the oldest want in the
  phylum, cashed NOT by stronger arms but by moving the problem —
  legs on a propeller, a 30-m wing, ~300 W, across the Channel in
  under three hours. The want was never wrong; the method was.
  The mirror's flagship holds the full recompute.

## RELATIONS

what runs along it.

- PHYSICS: the whole subject is its branch — action-reaction,
  pressure, energy bookkeeping, the square and cube laws, waves
  piling up near their own speed. Air and water: one physics,
  two thicknesses.
- WEATHER: flight lives inside its subject — wind as the moving
  medium, thermals as free fuel, icing, density altitude; and
  turbulence, the shared open wound (the mirror holds it).
- ANIMALS: birds, bats, and insects hold the original patents —
  soaring, flapping's vortex tricks, the size law obeyed to the
  gram; the albatross as glider, the hummingbird as helicopter.

  tonne-kilometre and winning wherever time outranks cost; the

  the worst hour, stability as a placed choice, fail toward

- THE BODY: thin air at altitude, ears popping on descent, the
