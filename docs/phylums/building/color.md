## THINGS
The units this subject is about; other homes named so a cascade can
branch.

- STONE, BRICK, CONCRETE — the squeeze family: mighty against
  crushing, a tenth of that against stretch; every old building is
  shaped around that one-sidedness; also: earth, chemistry, craft.
- STEEL — both jobs at full strength, drawn to wire six times
  harder in stretch; it freed building from the vault; also:
  engineering, chemistry (rust), economy.
- WOOD — a bundle of straws: mighty along the grain, feeble
  across; splits along, snaps across, swells across with moisture
  and hardly along; also: plants, farming (a tree is a pump — its
  tubes dictate the timber), cooking (muscle is straws too).
- REINFORCED CONCRETE — a marriage of opposites: concrete takes
  the squeeze, steel bars laid where the stretch runs take the
  stretch, held by one piece of luck — both swell with heat at
  nearly the same rate, so the seasons don't divorce them; also:
  the body (bone: stiff mineral wed to stretchy collagen),
  engineering (fiberglass, carbon fiber — same marriage).
- GLASS — the one-way heat valve: sunlight passes in, the warmed
  room radiates back at a wavelength glass won't pass — every
  window a small unregulated heater; also: physics, farming (the
  greenhouse is this aimed at plants).
- WATER — the patient enemy: rusts, rots, dissolves, freezes and
  wedges, travels up pores and as vapor into every seam;
  buildings die wet; also: chemistry, weather, earth.
- THE GROUND — every building's true base is dirt: weak,
  variable, only ever sampled; also: earth, farming.
- THE JOINT — where materials meet: where water enters, cost
  gathers, sound leaks, things break; the material is rarely the
  problem, the joins are; also: craft, engineering.
- THE FRAME AND THE CORE — columns, beams, the vertical shafts;
  the core is the one thing that can never move afterwards; also:
  the body (the spine), computing (an early structure outliving
  every feature built on it).
- STAIR, DOOR, RAMP — the building measured against the body; the
  ruler an onlooker reads the whole building with; also: the
  body, movement, measurement.
- THE STREET — a room with a floor, two walls, and an end you can
  see, asked to move traffic through AND serve its buildings;
  also: transport, people, economy (the kerb — a city's most
  fought-over three meters).
- THE PIPES AND WIRES — water in, waste out, current steady,
  words through: what a city lives on and never notices until it
  stops; also: engineering, economy, medicine.
- THE CROWD — people on foot are a fluid with a capacity curve:
  free flow, shuffle, crush; also: people, movement, survival.
- THE SUN — one half of the sky only, high in summer, low in
  winter; its geometry is known before the building is drawn and
  holds for its whole life; also: astronomy, weather, farming.

## CLAIMS
What holds, written to be tested by a crossing query.

- A structure hands out exactly two jobs — squeeze and stretch;
  everything built carries one, the other, or both (a bent beam:
  squeezed above, stretched below). Every pound aloft passes hand
  to hand to the ground.
- The triangle is the only pinned-stick shape that cannot change
  form without changing a stick's length; every other polygon
  folds. One diagonal brace cures a wobbly frame.
- The arch turns a gap into a squeeze: each stone leans on the
  next, the load flows around the hole and lands as outward-and-
  down thrust at the feet — asking only the job stone can do. A
  dome is an arch spun in a circle (eggshells, skulls, igloos).
- Materials remember two ways: a little pull springs back
  (elastic); past the yield point the change is permanent
  (plastic). A crumple zone spends the crash as bend on purpose;
  a sprain is a ligament past yield.
- A crack is a force magnifier: load crowds at a sharp tip, tens
  of times over, and each advance sharpens the tip — cracks feed
  on themselves. Round the corner and the magnifier is disarmed.
- Water comes in at the changes — roof to wall, window, parapet,
  chimney, pipe — almost never through the middle. The wall that
  lasts lets what gets in run down a gap and out at the bottom: a
  raincoat with a drain, never a sealed bag.
- Everything breathes with temperature, and restrained movement
  becomes force enough to buckle steel (numbers in SCIENCE).
- Before a building is a style it is a machine for making one
  climate inside another; which way it faces decides more about
  warmth, light, and running cost than any material chosen after.
- Mass and insulation solve different problems: mass banks the
  day's heat for the night (helps only where day and night differ
  sharply); insulation slows heat leaving (the long winter's
  need). Swap them and you built the wrong building carefully.
- Daylight is a small fierce sun plus an enormous soft sky, and
  the sky is what lights a room well; a window reaches about
  twice its head height, then gives up. Shade works outside the
  glass or barely at all — an indoor blind stops the light after
  the heat already entered.
- Felt warmth is four things — air, surrounding surfaces, air
  movement, dampness — and the thermostat measures one. Air moves
  by wind, warm air rising, or a fan; no fourth way. A sealed
  building must be given air deliberately: the leaks that lost
  heat were also carrying away moisture.
- A tall building is a chimney nobody ordered: warm air escapes
  above and hauls cold air in at the lobby — why the doors fight
  you and the top floor runs warm.
- Cost gathers at corners, junctions, changes of plane; a compact
  shape is cheap before any material is chosen, and the same
  surface-per-volume sum drives the heating bill.
- The column grid serves the structure, room sizes the people,
  and the two rarely agree — settling that is most of the plan.
  The core is permanent; skin, walls, uses all change. Buildings
  outlive their purposes: the survivors have plain structure,
  decent heights, adaptable rooms. A building is layers aging at
  different speeds — site forever, structure a century, skin
  decades, pipes twenty years, layout ten — and most trouble is a
  fast layer tied to a slow one.
- Where the wanted path and the planned path differ, people win:
  the worn track, the propped door, the back entrance. Rooms
  outlive their labels; people navigate by landmarks, not signs;
  occupy edges, not middles; meet a building in its first three
  meters at eye level; are drawn to a seat with a view from
  cover; and want each room as private as its use, reached as a
  sequence, not a single door.
- Fire safety buys minutes, not prevention: two ways out from
  everywhere, walls that hold a room's fire an hour, stairs kept
  clear of smoke — and in a tunnel the danger IS the smoke,
  channeled at whoever stands downwind.
- A room's sound is how long a noise takes to die there, and the
  right length depends on use — no room serves organ and lecture
  both. Keeping sound out takes mass, separation, completeness —
  one hole undoes the other two. Footsteps are not voices: impact
  enters the structure and bypasses every airborne defense.
- A city is its infrastructure. Water is the first limit on its
  size (heavy, needed daily, no substitute); everything is sized
  for the worst hour, not the average; a sewer moves waste, never
  destroys it; the street mains are sized for the fire brigade,
  not for drinking; the grid stands because any single thing may
  fail unnoticed; and a network loses a slice (a fifth of the
  water, often) cheaper to tolerate than to find.
- Infrastructure systems lean on each other — power runs the
  pumps, water cools the generators, crews need fuel and working
  signals — so one failure spreads sideways into systems that
  were fine. The spare that would absorb it is idle by
  definition, so every efficiency drive finds and removes it.
- Deferring maintenance is borrowing at a terrible rate: the
  saving is visible now, the cost compounds on someone later. A
  city built quickly gets its replacement bill all at once — a
  cohort installed together comes out together.
- Junctions set a road's capacity, not width; more road makes
  more driving until it jams at a larger size; density is what
  pays for the bus, shop, and school; a rider's waiting minute
  weighs about double a moving one.
- Infrastructure is defined by being unnoticed: its successes are
  invisible, so it is underfunded until a failure makes it
  briefly famous.

## SCIENCE
The math, physics, and chemistry of building, held — not
mentioned: LAW to compute with, RULE to follow, WORKED where it
earns it.

### PHYSICS — the ledger of materials (two jobs, with numbers)

LAW: stress = force ÷ area (N/mm²); a material carries until
  stress meets strength, and strength differs by job:
  squeeze — concrete ~30 · brick ~20-40 (the wall held to less by
  its mortar) · granite ~100-250 · wood along the grain ~40 · steel
  250. stretch — concrete ~3 · stone ~5-10 · wood along the grain
  ~80 clear (knots halve the working figure) · steel 250, drawn
  bridge wire ~1,600 · spider silk ~1,000 at a sixth the weight.
  Across its straws wood does a tenth of its along-grain work.
RULE: give each material only its strong job — stone the squeeze,
  wire the stretch, steel either.
WORKED: a concrete prism 100 × 100 mm (10,000 mm²): squeeze 30 ×
  10,000 = 300,000 N ≈ 30 tons — a loaded truck; stretch 3 ×
  10,000 = 30,000 N ≈ 3 tons. Tenfold gap in one block — THAT is
  why steel bars lie where the stretch runs. One 20 mm bar (area
  π × 10² ≈ 314 mm²) hangs 314 × 250 = 78,500 N ≈ 8 tons alone.

### PHYSICS — the load path as proof (worked in steel)

LAW: a structure stands when every load has an unbroken path to
  the ground and every member keeps stress under strength ÷
  safety factor.
RULE: to prove a column — sum every load above, divide by the
  strength, multiply by the factor: that is the steel area
  needed.
WORKED: a column under a 6 × 6 m patch of floor = 36 m² at
  7.5 kN/m² (the floor's own weight plus people and things): 36 ×
  7.5 = 270 kN a story; ten stories = 2,700 kN = 2,700,000 N. At
  250 N/mm²: 2,700,000 ÷ 250 = 10,800 mm² bare; times 1.5,
  16,200 mm² — a solid bar ~127 mm square (127² ≈ 16,129) holds
  ten floors. Real columns spread that area into wide flanges
  because a slender bar bows before it crushes: AREA answers
  crushing, SHAPE answers bowing.
LAW: the safety factor = capacity ÷ demand — because loads are
  guessed, materials vary, builders err. Buildings ~1.5-2 (more
  where inspection can't reach); elevator ropes ~10; aircraft
  ~1.5, since every spare pound must fly — flight pays weight for
  margin, a building pays money.

### PHYSICS — the beam and the span

LAW: sag grows with span to the FOURTH power and shrinks with
  depth CUBED; width only adds proportionally.
RULE: buy span with depth, never width — double the span and sag
  is 2⁴ = 16×; double the depth instead and sag falls 8-fold.
WORKED: a 25 mm shelf over 60 cm sags a hair; over 120 cm, 16
  hairs, and looks it. As rule of thumb, floor beams want ~span ÷
  20 of depth: 6 m wants ~300 mm, 12 m ~600 — why column-free
  floor is bought, not assumed, and a car park's bay size can set
  a whole building's frame.

### PHYSICS — the arch, squeeze made clever

LAW: an arch under a spread load shoves sideways at each foot
  H ≈ w · L² ÷ (8 · f) — load per meter, span, rise. The flatter,
  the harder the shove.
RULE: provide for H — mass (buttress), a tie rod across the feet,
  or a neighbor arch shoving back.
WORKED: span 10 m, rise 2 m, 10 kN/m (100 kN total): H = 1,000 ÷
  16 = 62.5 kN sideways at each foot besides 50 kN down. Halve
  the rise: 1,000 ÷ 8 = 125 kN — the shove DOUBLED. That line is
  the buttress, the tie rod in the flat brick arch, and why Roman
  bridges stand two thousand years into the argument: every stone
  only ever squeezed.

### MATHEMATICS — the square-cube law (the size ceiling)

LAW: scale a shape by s: area grows s², weight s³ — strength per
  weight falls as 1/s. Standing stress at a column's base is
  density × g × height, regardless of thickness.
RULE: a material's self-crush ceiling = strength ÷ (density × g).
WORKED: granite at its cautious end, 100 MPa, 2,700 kg/m³:
  100,000,000 ÷ (2,700 × 9.8) = 100,000,000 ÷ 26,460 ≈ 3,780 m —
  a solid granite column ~3.8 km tall crushes its own base;
  shape only spreads the answer (mountains reach ~9 km as cones
  on a floating crust — the earth phylum's story).
WORKED, downward: scale an ant up 100×: weight ×1,000,000, leg
  cross-section ×10,000 — legs 100× too weak; the ant that
  carries ~50 bodies, scaled up, could lift only half of one. The
  elephant stands its legs straight beneath like columns because
  its margin is thin. Ants scale; elephants do not; towers meet
  the same ledger (the mirror files the tower to heaven).
WORKED, the heating corollary: double a building every way — 8×
  volume, 4× skin — half the skin, heat loss, and wall cost per
  room inside. Compactness is this arithmetic; so is the corner's
  price.

### PHYSICS — the ground (pressure run backwards)

LAW: pressure = force ÷ area; a foundation runs it in reverse —
  grow the area, tame the pressure. Soil bears little: soft clay
  ~50 kN/m², firm ground ~150-300, rock thousands.
RULE: footing area = building weight ÷ what the soil bears.
WORKED: a 100-ton house (~1,000 kN) on soft clay: 1,000 ÷ 50 =
  20 m² of footing — a 0.5 m strip along 40 m of wall. Portable:
  80 kg on boot soles (~0.03 m²) presses ~26,000 Pa and punches
  through snow bearing ~5,000; on 0.4 m² of snowshoe, 784 ÷ 0.4 ≈
  2,000 Pa — walks on top. Camel feet, tank treads, and a tree's
  root plate run the same law.

### PHYSICS — heat, air, and water in the shell

LAW: length changes ΔL = α · L · ΔT; steel's α = 12 × 10⁻⁶ per °C
  (concrete nearly the same — the marriage's luck). Restrained,
  it builds stress = E · α · ΔT, E = 200,000 N/mm² for steel.
RULE: long structures get gaps (the toothed joints tires thump);
  what cannot gap must be strong enough to fight itself.
WORKED: a 500 m bridge through a 40°C year: 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 500 × 40
  = 0.24 m of breathing. Welded rigid: 200,000 × 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 40 =
  96 N/mm² — over a third of the steel's strength spent on
  weather. That is the rail buckled in a heat wave and the summer
  sag of power lines.
LAW: warm air is lighter; a warm column in a cold day pushes
  Δp ≈ 1.29 × 9.8 × height × ΔT ÷ 273 pascals.
WORKED: 100 m building, 20°C warmer inside: 1.29 × 9.8 × 100 × 20
  ÷ 273 ≈ 93 Pa — on a 2 m² lobby door ~185 N, a 19-kg child
  pulling the handle against you all winter. The same push drives
  every chimney and hurries smoke up a stairwell faster than
  people walk.
LAW: cold air holds less water; cool damp air far enough and the
  water lands as drops on whatever surface is there — inside a
  wall, at whichever layer sits at the wrong temperature.
RULE: put the wet point where it can dry, not where it can rot —
  and watch whatever crosses the insulation (steel beam, balcony,
  metal frame): small shares of wall, large losses, announced as
  the cold stripes where mold grows first.
WORKED, daylight: window head at 2.2 m lights ~4.4 m of room; lit
  from both sides plus a middle, a building deeper than ~13 m is
  an electric building by construction — why old buildings are
  narrow and courtyards exist.

### PHYSICS — water and energy in the city

LAW: standing water presses ρ · g · h — every 10 m of height is
  ~98,000 Pa, call it one atmosphere.
RULE: cut a hilly city into pressure zones along its contours;
  one zone cannot serve both ends of a tall hill.
WORKED: a 70 m hill spans 1,000 × 9.8 × 70 = 686,000 Pa ≈ 7
  atmospheres — the bottom's pipes near bursting while hilltop
  taps dribble. The roof tank is the same law at one address.
WORKED, the pumping bill: lifting 1 m³ by 50 m costs 1,000 × 9.8
  × 50 = 490,000 J = 0.136 kWh. A city of a million at ~300 L
  each moves 300,000 m³ a day: 300,000 × 0.136 ≈ 41,000 kWh —
  ~41 MWh of pure lifting daily, ~58 at real pump efficiency,
  before treating. A water bill is an energy bill wearing a
  different name.
LAW: a network is sized for peak simultaneous demand; the average
  says nothing about simultaneity.
WORKED: a household averages ~400 L/day = 0.28 L/min; one shower
  draws 8 L/min — ~29× the average, and whole streets shower at
  once. Larger: 50 houses average 0.23 L/s; the fire brigade
  needs ~30 L/s from the hydrant — ~130× the street's average.
  The mains are sized for the fire, run oversized and slow every
  ordinary day — and the stale far end of the network is where
  the quality trouble lives.

### MATHEMATICS — flow, crowds, and chance

LAW: throughput = density × speed, and speed falls as density
  climbs — flow peaks in the middle, then collapses. A jammed
  road and an empty one both carry nothing.
WORKED: at 100 km/h (27.8 m/s) with 2 s gaps each car occupies
  27.8 × 2 + 4 ≈ 60 m: the lane passes 27.8 ÷ 60 × 3,600 ≈ 1,670
  cars an hour; real lanes peak near ~2,000 just below the jam.
  Feet obey the same curve: peak flow ~1.3 persons per meter of
  width per second near ~2 persons/m²; past ~4/m² a shuffle; past
  ~6/m² the pressure of those behind injures those in front.
RULE: size exits from the crowd's law — 1,000 people through 2 m
  of doors at 1.3 × 2 = 2.6 persons/s takes 1,000 ÷ 2.6 ≈ 385 s ≈
  6.4 minutes. Fire's minutes are bought or lost in this sum.
LAW: a "hundred-year" flood is a 1-in-100 chance EACH year, and
  independent years cluster.
WORKED: chance across a 30-year mortgage = 1 − 0.99³⁰ = 1 − 0.740
  = 26% — one in four; across a century ≈ 63%. Two floods in
  consecutive years falsify nothing — the average says nothing
  about the spacing.
LAW: a rider's waiting minute weighs ~2× a moving one.
WORKED: a slow bus every 10 minutes (wait 5, weighted 10) beats a
  fast one every 40 (wait 20, weighted 40) before the wheels
  turn — frequency IS transit's speed. The bus's own clock is
  spent at the doors: boarding seconds × stops × trips is why
  level floors and off-vehicle fares beat any engine.

### PHYSICS — sound in rooms and walls

LAW: reverberation time ≈ 0.161 × volume ÷ absorption (s, m³, m²
  open-window-equivalent). Speech wants ≤ ~1.0 s; organ music
  thrives at 2-4.
WORKED: a hall 20 × 15 × 10 m = 3,000 m³ with 300 m² absorption:
  0.161 × 3,000 ÷ 300 = 1.61 s — grand for a choir, mush for a
  lecture. To reach 1.0: 483 ÷ 1.0 = 483 m², so add ~180 m² of
  soft surface. The restaurant got loud the year the tablecloths
  left.
RULE: to keep sound out — mass, separation, completeness, and the
  last governs: one unsealed socket passes more than a meter of
  concrete was stopping. Impact enters the structure and bypasses
  mass; the cures are softness at the contact or a floating
  floor.

### CHEMISTRY — the slow enemies

REACTION: iron + water + air → rust, always; rust occupies
  several times the iron's volume, so a rusting rebar SWELLS and
  bursts its cover off from inside (the rust-stained ruins where
  water won). Paint is the argument continued — bridges are
  painted forever.
REACTION: freezing water swells ~9% and, confined, presses tens
  of megapascals — several times stone's stretch strength of
  ~5-10. Each winter deepens the crack it made: frost carves
  mountains and farms potholes with one chisel.
REACTION: rot is hired-out digestion — fungus needs wood, water,
  air, warmth together; deny any one and wood lasts (piles under
  old sea-cities: always drowned, never rotted).
LAW: a disinfectant is consumed as it works, falling with time
  and distance down the pipe — treatment leaves a residual on
  purpose, and where it has faded the quality problems live.

## METHODS
How things are done here — what governs each, what breaks it.

- SITING AND FACING: read the sun's fixed band first (winter gift
  on one face, west-afternoon punishment, no overhang stops a
  horizontal sun); corridors and stairs on the noisy side,
  bedrooms on the quiet — nearly free in layout, expensive and
  half-working attempted later with glazing.
- SPREADING THE LOAD: dig to ground that bears, widen the footing
  by pressure ÷ area, then build up. Breaks when soil is assumed
  instead of sampled — settling cracks are the ground answering a
  question nobody asked.
- FRAMING: triangulate what must not fold; carry every load down
  a continuous path; buy span with depth; fix the grid and core
  FIRST — the core is one decision taken on every floor at once
  and can never move. Keep floor heights generous: room, beam,
  ducts, and lights compete for the same inches — tall old
  warehouses became flats, mean-storied offices became rubble.
- CLOSING THE SHELL: shed water at every join (flashing), drain
  the wall (cavity, weep holes), put the dew point where it
  dries, break the thermal bridges; shade outside the glass;
  light rooms from two sides; choose mass or insulation by the
  climate's swing. Breaks when sealed like a bag — water that
  always gets in cannot leave.
- GIVING AIR: use the three movers deliberately — cross-breeze,
  the stack's high and low mouths, or fans; a tight building gets
  ducted air on purpose. Each mover fails in its own weather:
  still days starve the breeze, mild days kill the stack.
- BUYING MINUTES (fire): two ways out from everywhere; hour
  walls; stairs pressurized against smoke; doors opening outward
  under a crowd's push; in tunnels, fans driving smoke one way to
  hold a clear path the other — chosen in seconds. Breaks at the
  propped door and the boxes in the stairwell: completeness fails
  first, as with sound.
- SHAPING SOUND: absorption matched to use (the Sabine sum); mass
  + separation + completeness against neighbors; softness or
  floating floors against footsteps. Breaks on one hole.
- SIZING FOR THE WORST HOUR: peak, not average, sizes the pipe,
  cable, exit; N-1 sizes the grid — not "nothing breaks" but "one
  thing may break at a time"; a reservoir is judged by the rate
  it sustains through the worst drought on record, not the volume
  it holds.
- MAKING STREETS: sort movement from access — trunks that move,
  branches that serve, conflict pushed to junctions; time the
  lights as one corridor (every green second is taken from
  someone; each change wastes a few clearing); keep blocks
  small — routes multiply with junctions, shops live on corners;
  spend the first three meters of frontage on doors and life.
  Breaks when one road does both jobs — it does neither.
- RUNNING THE CITY'S BLOOD: pressure zones by contour; residual
  disinfectant down the pipe; sewage in three ordered jobs —
  settle the solids, let bacteria eat the dissolved, polish and
  disinfect — each failing if the one before did (and the mixed
  flow read as the city's daily blood test, days ahead of any
  clinic); gas mains packed overnight as their own tank; district
  heat only where dense AND cold, since heat leaks per meter of
  pipe. Breaks when rain and sewage share one pipe and the sky
  delivers the peak: the overflow to the river is a design, not
  an accident — separating means digging every street.
- MOVING PEOPLE: frequency before speed; level boarding and
  off-vehicle fares against dwell; density within a short walk of
  the stop or the service spirals (fewer riders → cuts → fewer
  riders); judge fares knowing most cost is fixed and much
  benefit lands on people not aboard. Breaks when managed to the
  farebox — the system shrinks itself toward nothing.
- KEEPING IT STANDING (upkeep as method): pay the small bill on
  time — deferral compounds, ~1 of sealing skipped becoming ~4-5
  of rebuilding; renew cohorts before they fail together; dig
  once and share the hole; shake down every new system — failures
  crowd the first months, fall to a floor, climb again at end of
  life; where a structure has no second load path, a person walks
  the metal forever — redundancy wasn't built in, so inspection
  substitutes. Breaks when the budget argues against visible
  things — prevention emits no signal.
- BUILDING FOR THE UNKNOWN TENANT: plain structure, generous
  heights, separable layers (never bury a twenty-year pipe in a
  hundred-year slab), rooms sized for several uses — the future
  use is unknown at the drawing, so fitting the present exactly
  is a bet that the present will last.

## MEANS
What the methods run on, with the numbers that matter.

- THE MATERIALS' LEDGER: the strength table in SCIENCE is the
  shopping list — squeeze cheap in stone and concrete, stretch in
  steel, both in wood along its straws (its cure for across:
  plywood, grains crossed on purpose).
- THE DRAWINGS: the plan says where things are; the SECTION says
  what the place is like — height, light, what you see standing
  there — and most unfixable mistakes are section mistakes made
  on plans. A physical model ends an argument in ten seconds.
- THE INSTRUMENTS: plumb line and level (gravity as reference);
  the borehole — the ground's only honest word, a sampled column
  bet on a whole field; the frequency meter — every generator on
  a network turns in step, so their common speed is supply and
  demand balanced: the whole system's books, read in real time.
- THE MACHINES: crane, pump, digger — and the rule that the
  expensive part of anything buried is the hole, not the pipe.
  Underground, hitting something is normal, not careless — the
  register was never really kept.
- THE CODES AND RECORDS: standards are compressed disasters —
  every clause written after the failure that produced it; the
  as-built that differs from the as-drawn; the black start plan —
  a grid needs power to make power, so recovery begins at the few
  plants kept able to start unaided, building outward for hours.
- THE PEOPLE: the long-serving operator who knows which valve
  sticks and which alarm lies — the real manual, written nowhere,
  retiring when they do; the fire brigade whose hose sized the
  mains; the gritting crew betting salt on a forecast before the
  ice forms, judged afterwards with certainty.

## PURPOSE
The wants this subject answers — the doors a query enters by.

- HOLD OFF THE WEATHER: one climate made inside another —
  deliberate obstacles in the way of things that were going to
  happen anyway.
- STAND: carry every load — live, dead, wind, crowd — down an
  unbroken path to the ground, for a century, with margin.
- GATHER: put people near enough that the bus, shop, school,
  pipe, and wire can exist at all — nearly every shared thing
  needs a quorum within reach, and density pays for it; its costs
  (noise, shade, other people) are answered by building well, not
  by spreading out.
- MOVE AND CARRY: water in, waste out, current steady, streets
  flowing, words through — the arrangements a settlement IS.
- PROTECT: minutes in a fire, mass against noise, height against
  the flood, margin against the crush.
- LAST AND ADAPT: outlive the purpose, take the next tenant, hand
  the structure on — a hundred-year bet against an unknown
  future.

## HISTORY
How the knowledge arose, in what order, at what cost.

- The squeeze era, most of building's life: stone knew one job,
  so every great thing was thick wall, arch, vault, dome — form
  as the workaround for the missing half of the ledger. Its
  masterpieces still stand: squeeze does not fatigue.
- Wood's parallel reign: the material that could stretch was
  grown, not made — roofs, bridges, towns of straws, triangulated

- The paid lesson of water and waste: cities drank their own

  broken — the first great sewers poured into the drinking river

- Iron, steel, then the elevator: stretch became purchasable by

- Reinforced concrete's marriage, then its lesson: the couple
  agreed on heat but not on water — rust swelled and burst the

- The crack's tuition at altitude: the first jetliners tore at

- The car's remaking of the city: separation by rule made travel
  compulsory, parking spread the destinations, wider roads filled

  filed in the mirror.
- The sealing lesson: tight buildings saved the heat and kept the
  moisture; mold taught that the leaks had been doing two jobs.
- The screen's arrival: pumps and substations run from miles
  away — one operator, a hundred sites, and a way in nobody
  designed. Efficiency and exposure came in one upgrade; that
  ledger is still open.
