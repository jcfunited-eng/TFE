## THINGS
The units this subject is about; each names its other homes so a
cascade can branch.

- THE OPPONENT — what makes this subject unlike every other: a learner
  who updates on your every move, so each law is played against a mind,
  not a lock; also: mind, play, economy, governance.
- FORCE — organized capacity to compel: people, weapons, the training
  welding them; decides only where gathered; also: physics (pressure is
  force over area), governance, law.
- SUPPLY — the daily arriving tons a force eats and burns: food, water,
  fuel, ammunition, parts. An army is a machine for eating and burning,
  bounded by what arrives; also: transport, economy, farming, upkeep.
- GROUND — terrain doing soldiers' work: the hill, river, narrow street
  and wood that make one defender worth several attackers; also: earth,
  weather, building (a wall is terrain made on purpose).
- INFORMATION AND ITS SHADOW — what each side knows, guesses, and is
  fed; the fog is not a failure of the medium, it IS the medium; also:
  mind, measurement, story.
- MORALE — the belief that staying is survivable and worth it; the real
  currency: most contests end when one side stops believing, well
  before it stops being able; also: mind, people, body, economy (a
  panic is morale collapsing while wearing money).
- THE PLAN — a walked map of possibilities, not a script; it dies on
  first contact and still pays, because the planner improvises from a
  map instead of a fog; also: mind, teaching, design.
- THE RESERVE — force deliberately held back; once battle starts, the
  commander's entire remaining ability to affect the day; also: economy
  (slack as responsiveness), medicine.
- WALLS AND WEAPONS, IN PAIRS — every wall answers a particular weapon
  and every weapon a particular wall; neither ever stays ahead; also:
  engineering, building, craft.
- THE SMALL GROUP — the eight people around a fighter; in the moment
  people fight for them, not for causes, and a unit rebuilt from
  strangers does not fight; also: people, teaching, culture.
- THE POPULATION — in irregular war, the actual board: food, shelter,
  recruits, information and silence flow from whichever side a village
  finds safer to help; also: people, governance.
- THE THREAT — a promise of cost doing its work inside the other side's
  head; deterrence's instrument; also: mind, law (a penalty deters only
  if expected), language.
- THE SETTLEMENT — the agreement the fighting was for; livable by the
  loser, or a truce with a date on it; also: law, governance.
- TIME AND PATIENCE — a weapon outright: sieges, blockades and
  insurgencies are bets on who can keep consuming, or merely existing,
  longer; also: economy, survival.

## CLAIMS
What holds, written to be tested by a crossing query. Worth is
applicability to the query, never advocacy.

- War is a means, never a thing in itself: it starts because somebody
  wants a decision they cannot get by asking, and stops when one side
  changes its mind about the price. The argument is the war.
- Force destroys armies and cannot remove disagreements — what the war
  was about still sits there when the guns go quiet, and a settlement
  that skips it is the next war wearing a later date.
- Over time, supply beats valor: the side that stays fed, armed and
  rested outlasts the side that merely fights well (arithmetic in
  SCIENCE, without exceptions long enough to matter).
- Most soldiers are not fighting — they drive, cook, mend, carry, treat
  and dig; the shooters are a minority, and the ratio is the price of
  keeping that minority effective, not waste.
- The weakest part of an army is the road behind it: cutting supply
  needs a bridge, a depot or a convoy, not a victory — a small force in
  the right place stops a large one without a battle.
- Battles are mostly decided before they are fought — by who chose the
  ground, the timing, and whether to fight at all. Refusing a contest
  already lost is the strongest move there is.
- The defender holds the cheaper hand: dug in, knowing the ranges,
  needing only not-to-lose while the attacker crosses open ground and
  must win — the working rule is ~3 attackers per defender AT THE POINT
  of assault. The advantage flips when the attacker picks the point and
  arrives first: the entire purpose of mobility and surprise.
- Strength spread is strength spent: force decides only where gathered,
  so the art is accepting weakness almost everywhere to be strong at
  the one point that matters (priced by the square law in SCIENCE).
- Surprise is enormous and brief: an advantage made of the other side's
  ignorance ends when they learn, and using it is how they learn. Spend
  it in one go; a plan needing a second surprise has a hole in it.
- Deception cannot make an enemy believe nothing — only something else;
  the workable lie confirms what they already expect, and the most
  valuable secret is what you know they think. The defense is the
  mirror: weigh what the other side CAN do, never its apparent intent.
- Intelligence rarely fails by missing information: the warning is
  usually in the files — buried, contradicted, or refused for not
  fitting the standing belief. The failure is in the reading.
- Every plan dies on first contact, and planning still pays; friction
  is the medium — everything arrives late, wrong, and misreported, not
  from incompetence but because many tired, frightened people are
  coordinating in the dark.
- What breaks in battle is a belief, not a body count: units stop being
  useful at a casualty threshold well under half — not destroyed,
  switched off — and the decision spreads faster than any order.
- Under fire nobody rises to the occasion; everyone falls to the level
  trained to. Drill makes people do together, without deciding, what
  none would choose alone — terror stops the thinking part, and a
  skill practiced past thought still runs.
- Most rounds hit nobody, by design: fire that keeps heads down removes
  the ability to shoot, move and look — freedom of movement is the
  product. And most battle casualties come from fragments covering an
  area, not aimed shots: digging is a soldier's most valuable act.
- For most of history disease killed more soldiers than the enemy
  (crowding plus bad water); even now illness, accidents and exhaustion
  outdraw combat — real strength is present-and-fit, not on-paper.
- The wounded live or die mostly in the hours before surgery: stop the
  bleeding where they fell, then a fast trip to someone who can
  operate. The achievement is a transport system.
- Two ways to win, needing different armies: break the enemy's
  coherence faster than it reacts (cheaper, riskier), or consume its
  substance faster than it replaces (slower, surer). The choice mostly
  reflects who has more to spend.
- Every advance weakens with each mile: the attacker's supply lengthens
  while the defender falls back onto its depots; an offensive is
  strongest in the middle, most dangerous to itself at the end (the
  crossing curves are worked in SCIENCE).
- Escalation is a ratchet: each answer must slightly exceed the last or
  read as surrender. Nobody picks the top of the ladder; everybody
  picks one more rung.
- A threat deters only when all three hold: you can carry it out, they
  believe you will, and they know exactly what triggers it. Fail one
  and it is noise — deterrence is mostly communication (SCIENCE).
- Where both sides can destroy each other AFTER being struck, striking
  first buys nothing: stability from being UNABLE to defend — a peace
  of hostages kept in plain view, maintained on purpose.
- Arms races are a trap of structure, not judgment: each side's best
  individual move is to build, both end poorer, the balance ends where
  it started (the worked matrix in SCIENCE).
- Starting a war takes one decision; ending it takes two. Costs already
  paid demand redemption, so terms acceptable at the start turn
  humiliating by the end, and wars outrun both sides' wish to stop.
- A guerrilla needs only to keep existing while the government must
  protect everything and be seen to work — different victory conditions
  on one board, and time favors whoever needs less. The insurgency
  lives on the population, so the fight is over which side people find
  it safer to help — and a raid that kills a family recruits more
  fighters than it removes.
- Taking a country is the cheap part; holding it means feeding,
  policing and administering it without the legitimacy that makes
  ruling cheap — troops who cannot go home, with no victory available.
- Aircraft see everything, reach anywhere, hold nothing: ground is
  governed by whoever stands on it. At sea, control (your ships move,
  theirs do not) takes a fleet; denial (nobody moves safely) takes a
  few submarines or mines. Stopping is cheaper than guaranteeing,
  everywhere.
- Blockade works slowly and lands unfairly: scarcity is rationed by
  power, so the shortage reaches the weakest first and the army last —
  and keeps working long after anyone watches.
- A sponsor arming somebody else's war is served by continuation, not
  resolution: the losing side keeps getting enough to continue, never
  enough to win.
- Allies pull two ways at once: each wants the others to fight, each
  fears abandonment if it hangs back — free-riding and entanglement
  grown from the same fear.
- Soldiers rarely surrender alone: a group decides together, leaning on
  whether surrender looks survivable — a reputation for killing
  prisoners makes the enemy fight to the last. Cruelty raises its own
  price.
- For most of history the killing came AFTER the decision: a formation
  that holds loses a fraction; one that runs is cut down from behind.
  Between defeat and catastrophe stands withdrawing together.
- Civil wars run longer, dirtier and end harder: no border, no exit,
  the enemy in the same street, and no going home — home is the thing
  being fought over.
- In nearly every war most of the dead are not soldiers: they die of
  displacement, hunger, cold and disease — broken water, food and
  shelter — for years after the shooting stops.
- Almost every war is entered by BOTH sides expecting a quick result;
  long wars are two short-war assumptions colliding.
- The famous battle is usually the moment somebody noticed, not the
  moment it was decided; supply, production and exhaustion set the
  result earlier, and the battle made it visible enough to name.
- Casualty figures reported during a war are moves in it, not
  measurements: who counts as a combatant, whether hunger counts, which
  period — each definition moves the number before counting begins.
- Doctrine is a vocabulary, not a rulebook: shared meaning lets
  thousands who cannot talk act as one. And the one on the spot knows
  what is happening while headquarters knows what it is for — giving
  purpose and letting the spot pick the method beats waiting for orders
  whose situation has gone.
- Armies learn expensively from the last war and meet a different one:
  the better an institution learns, the more precisely it fits what is
  over.

## SCIENCE
The math of opposed wills, held — not mentioned. Each piece is a LAW a
query can compute with, a RULE to follow, and a WORKED case run to a
checked answer.

### THE MATRIX — choosing under an opponent

LAW: when two sides choose at once, each outcome pays each side a
  number; a side compares its own payoffs holding the other's choice
  fixed. An outcome is stable when neither gains by switching alone —
  and the stable cell can be worse for BOTH than one they both prefer.
  The trap is in the structure, not the players.
RULE: write the payoff table, mark each side's best answer to each move
  of the other; the cell where both marks land is where it goes.
WORKED (the arms race, and the security dilemma priced): each side
  picks BUILD or HOLD. Building costs 2; a lead is worth 4.
  · both HOLD: 0 and 0
  · I BUILD, they HOLD: −2 + 4 = +2 for me; −4 for them
  · both BUILD: leads cancel, −2 and −2.
  If they HOLD, my build pays +2 > 0; if they BUILD, my build pays
  −2 > −4. Building wins WHATEVER they do, the table is symmetric, so
  both build and both land at −2 while 0 and 0 sat on the table. That
  is the security dilemma in four cells: my safety purchase reads as
  your threat, and two sensible players buy the worse world.
WORKED (the drain — one number moved): add inspection that catches a
  lone builder before the lead is real. BUILD against HOLD now pays the
  builder −2 (cost spent, lead denied), the holder 0; holding wins
  whatever the other does, and 0 and 0 becomes the stable cell. One
  checkable promise unlocked the trap — why treaties live or die on
  verification, not trust.

### THE SQUARE LAW — why concentration wins

LAW: under massed aimed fire, fighting strength grows with the SQUARE
  of numbers (each fighter both shoots and soaks fire): N against a
  smaller M leaves roughly √(N² − M²) standing.
RULE: square both sides, subtract, take the root; to price a divided
  force, run the first fight's survivors into the second.
WORKED: 500 meet 400. 250,000 − 160,000 = 90,000; √90,000 = 300.
  Outnumbering by a quarter, the larger side loses 200 while destroying
  all 400.
WORKED (defeat in detail): the 400 split into two 200s met in turn.
  First: 250,000 − 40,000 = 210,000 → ~458 survive. Second: 210,000 −
  40,000 = 170,000 → ~412. Split, the 400 cost the enemy ~88; together,
  ~200. Strength spread IS strength spent — why concentration at the
  chosen point wins battles that would be lost everywhere at once, and
  why a central position, moving reserves faster than the ring around
  it, punches above its total.

### SURPRISE — the arithmetic of not knowing where

LAW: the defender must divide across every point the attacker MIGHT
  choose; the attacker masses at the one he DID choose. Local odds =
  attacker's mass ÷ (defender's total ÷ points guarded).
RULE: count the points the defender cannot leave open, divide his force
  by that count, compare the attacker's whole force to the fraction.
WORKED: 10 battalions guard 5 passes — 2 each. An attacker with 6
  masses all at one pass: 6 against 2 is 3-to-1 at the point of contact
  — assault odds for a force outnumbered 10 to 6 overall. The bill: the
  used surprise teaches; next season the same move meets 8. Capital,
  spent in the using.

### SUPPLY — the arithmetic that bounds everything

LAW: daily need = heads × daily consumption. A pre-industrial soldier
  ate ~1.5 kg a day, a horse ~10 kg; a modern mechanized force runs
  ~100 kg per soldier per day and up, fuel and ammunition counted.
RULE: multiply heads by the daily figure; that tonnage must arrive
  EVERY day, and what cannot arrive, the force cannot do.
WORKED: 100,000 soldiers, mechanized: 100,000 × 100 kg = 10,000,000 kg
  = 10,000 tonnes a day — ~1,000 ten-tonne truckloads daily just to
  stand still. Every fighting plan is this delivery problem first.

LAW: the carriage eats its cargo. delivered = load − (team's daily
  consumption × round-trip days); at some distance delivery is zero —
  the dead radius.
RULE: set delivered to zero and solve for distance; the honest radius
  stops at half-eaten.
WORKED: a wagon hauls 1,000 kg behind a team eating 50 kg of fodder a
  day at 25 km a day, and must return: round-trip days = 2D ÷ 25,
  fodder = 50 × 2D ÷ 25 = 4D kg. Delivered = 1,000 − 4D: zero at
  250 km, half-gone at 125 km. A cart's useful reach was ~125 km — why
  pre-rail armies hugged rivers and coasts (a barge carries ~30
  wagonloads past the same mouths, stretching the radius ~30-fold), why
  empires ended where their logistics did, and why fierceness never
  moved this number one kilometer.

LAW (the advance weakens itself): deliveries per vehicle per day =
  working hours ÷ round-trip time, which grows with distance from the
  depot — while the retreating defender's distance to ITS depots
  shrinks. The curves cross; past the crossing the attacker weakens
  with every mile of success.
RULE: recompute trips per day at the new distance; scale deliveries by
  the ratio.
WORKED: trucks at 40 km/h, 8 driving hours a day. Depot 40 km back:
  round trip 80 km = 2 h → 4 loads a day. The advance wins 120 km;
  depot now 160 km back: 320 km = 8 h → 1 load a day. The same fleet
  delivers ONE QUARTER — the army quartered its own supply by winning,
  while the defender fell back onto full depots. The culminating point
  as arithmetic: why offensives stall unresisted, and why the moment of
  greatest apparent victory is the moment of greatest real danger.

### THE LONG WAR — production against fierceness

LAW: force next month = force this month − losses + replacements.
  Whoever's replacement rate exceeds its loss rate GROWS while
  fighting, and no front-line skill closes a large enough output gap.
RULE: take each side's monthly net (built minus lost), project the
  lines; where they cross, the material verdict is in.
WORKED: A starts with 3,000 tanks, loses 200 a month, builds 100 — net
  −100. B starts with 2,000, fights WORSE — loses 250 — but builds 400:
  net +150. Crossing: 3,000 − 100t = 2,000 + 150t → 1,000 = 250t → t =
  4 months (both at 2,600). Month 12: A = 1,800, B = 3,800 — more than
  double. B loses every exchange and wins the war by arithmetic. This
  computation is the grave of "victory by fierceness alone" (receipts
  in the mirror).

### THE SIEGE — two larders and a clock

LAW: days of holding = stores ÷ daily consumption; the ration is the
  lever. Both sides consume continuously — the contest is between two
  larders, plus disease compounding in a crowded camp with bad water.
RULE: compute each side's days of supply, including the besieger's haul
  from ITS base, then discount the camp by disease's compounding.
WORKED: a town of 30,000 eating 1 kg a head holds 2,700 tonnes:
  2,700,000 ÷ 30,000 = 90 days; halve the ration → 180. The besieging
  20,000 eat ~30 tonnes a day, every kilogram hauled (the wagon law
  prices it), and camp sickness at ~2% a week compounds: 0.98²⁶ ≈ 0.59
  — ~40% of the besiegers gone in 180 days without one assault. Why the
  record so often shows the besieging army starving first, and why
  patience is listed among MEANS.

### DETERRENCE — cost inside the other head

LAW: a threat deters when (believed cost) × (believed chance it is
  carried out) exceeds the prize. All three live in the OTHER side's
  head; truth enters only through belief.
RULE: price the prize as they price it; multiply the threatened cost by
  the chance THEY assign; compare. To repair a deterrent, work on
  belief and the trigger's clarity, not the warhead.
WORKED: prize worth 10; threatened cost 100 believed 1 time in 20:
  100 × 0.05 = 5 < 10 — they come. Belief raised to 1 in 5: 100 × 0.20
  = 20 > 10 — they stay home. Nothing physical changed; the
  communication did. The mutual case: where each side keeps the certain
  ability to answer AFTER being struck, the first blow buys ruin at
  near-certainty — so the stability rests, backwards from instinct, on
  both staying unable to defend.

## METHODS
How things are done here — with what governs each and what breaks it.
Described, never advocated.

- CHOOSING THE GROUND, THE TIME, AND WHETHER: odds cannot be beaten,
  only chosen, and the choosing happens before contact. Breaks when
  pride, sunk cost or an ally's dragging makes refusal unavailable.
- CONCENTRATION (the square law made deliberate): accept weakness
  almost everywhere; be strong at the point that matters. Breaks when
  the enemy learns the point, or the stripped places were unsafe.
- DEFENSE AND FORTIFICATION (the cheaper hand, stored): dig, range,
  wait; make ground do soldiers' work. Breaks when mobility lets the
  attacker pick an unwatched point, or the wall answers the LAST war's
  weapon (the mirror holds that graveyard).
- TAKING APART VS GRINDING DOWN: break coherence faster than the enemy
  reacts, or consume substance faster than it replaces — governed by
  the production law. Breaks when a coherence-breaker runs long, or a
  grinder is out-produced.
- COMBINED ARMS: pair weapons whose weaknesses differ — armor blind in
  towns, infantry naked in the open, artillery unable to hold — so
  every response exposes the enemy to the partner. Breaks when one arm
  outruns the others and meets its counter alone.
- DECEPTION (expectation management): feed the story they already
  prefer; spend the surprise in one go. Breaks when repeated — the
  trick that wins Sunday is dead film by Monday.
- DRILL AND DOCTRINE: rehearse past thought (under fire only the
  rehearsed exists); teach a shared vocabulary so thousands who cannot
  talk act as one. Breaks when it hardens into fitting the last war.
- PURPOSE-DOWN COMMAND: tell the one on the spot what it is FOR and let
  them pick the method — the order's situation is gone before the order
  arrives. Breaks when subordinates were trained to comply, not judge.
- THE RESERVE: hold force back on purpose; commit at the moment that
  decides. Governed by slack as the only responsiveness. Breaks when
  everything is committed early — the commander can then only watch.
- ATTACKING THE ROAD, NOT THE ARMY: the bridge, the depot, the convoy —
  a chain fails at its cheapest link. Breaks when routes and stores are
  redundant enough to reroute.
- SIEGE AND BLOCKADE (patience as the weapon): outlast the other
  larder; scarcity does the fighting. Breaks when your own larder,
  health or patience runs out first — as it historically often did; and
  blockade's cost lands on the weakest first, part of its true price,
  filed honestly.
- DENIAL (the weaker side's purchase): make the thing unusable rather
  than owned — mines, submarines, raids. Breaks when the object was
  control all along; denial never governs a street.
- DETERRENCE: capability, believed will, a clear trigger — all three,
  communicated. Breaks when any leg fails, the trigger blurs, or the
  answer is believed not to survive the first blow.
- INSURGENCY AND ITS COUNTER (the population as the board): the
  insurgent needs only to exist and show the government cannot deliver;
  the counter is protection that makes helping the government the safer
  bet, village by village. Breaks when raids kill families (each
  recruits more than it removes) or kill counts are read as the score.
- ENDING IT (the hardest method): a settlement the loser can live with,
  or a loser genuinely unable to try again — one or the other,
  honestly. Governed by: starting takes one decision, ending takes two,
  and sunk cost hardens both. Breaks when terms are harsh enough to
  enrage and mild enough to permit — the standard recipe for the
  sequel.

## MEANS
What the methods run on, with the numbers that matter.

- THE TRAIN — wagons, barges, rails, trucks: the wagon's ~125 km honest
  radius, the barge's ~30-fold stretch, the fleet's trips-per-day law.
  A force's reach is a property of these, not of its courage.
- ROADS, BRIDGES, PORTS, DEPOTS — the chain's links, each a target
  worth more than a battle; redundancy is supply's armor.
- GROUND ITSELF — the free force-multiplier; and the spade, the
  cheapest protection ever devised against the fragments that cause
  most casualties.
- WALLS AND THEIR HEIRS — stored defense, always one generation from
  obsolete.
- WEAPONS IN PAIRS — each with a counter, fielded so the counters cover
  each other; a lone arm is a solved problem.
- MAPS, SCOUTS, SIGNALS, FILES — worth exactly the READING applied to
  them, which is where failures actually live.
- THE DRILL FIELD — where capability is installed in advance; what is
  rehearsed until boring is all that exists under fire.
- THE SMALL GROUP, KEPT TOGETHER — cohesion as equipment: units rotated
  as blocks fight; units patched with strangers do not.
- THE MEDICAL CHAIN — tourniquet where they fell, fast transport, then
  surgery: survival is bought in the first hours.
- THE FACTORY AND THE TREASURY — in any long war, the deciding means;
  the production law runs on them.
- THE WORD — treaty, threat, guarantee: working only while believed,
  priced by the deterrence law, transformed (as the matrix shows) by
  anything that makes it checkable.

## PURPOSE
The wants this subject answers — the doors a query enters by.

- GET A DECISION ASKING COULD NOT — the founding purpose; force as the
  costliest method of settling a disagreement.
- KEEP WHAT IS HELD WITHOUT FIGHTING — deterrence: make the cost
  legible in advance so the contest never starts.
- SURVIVE THE STRONGER — the weaker side's menu: denial, ground,
  patience, existence as victory.
- SPEND LESS OF WHAT IS LOVED — every law here that prices cost serves
  getting the decision at the least ruin.
- END IT SO IT STAYS ENDED — the settlement livable by the loser; the
  purpose most often failed, so its failures fill the mirror.
- UNDERSTAND IT TO PREVENT IT — the study's oldest justification;
  served here, answered nowhere yet (the mirror holds the open want).

## HISTORY
How the knowledge arose, in what order, at what cost.

- Ground and supply first, older than writing: every early manual that
  survives is mostly marches, stores and choosing ground — the fighting
  chapters are the short ones. The least glamorous knowledge is oldest
  because it was paid for first.
- The walls-and-weapons wheel: high stone stopped ladders and fell to
  cannon; low earth stopped cannon and cost more than castles; concrete
  stopped shells and was walked around. Each turn taught, at full
  price, that a defense answers a named threat and threats get renamed.
- Disease's long reign: the camp outkilled the enemy for most of the
  record; that drains and clean water outfight doctors arrived late,
  from public health, outside the subject.
- The rail and the timetable: mass mobilization turned armies into
  schedules — visible, hard to reverse, watchable — and crises into
  races nobody meant to start. That the SIGNAL of preparing can cause
  the war dates from this.
- The industrial verdict: the long wars of factories settled that past
  a certain length, output decides — the production law was written in
  those receipts, against every romance of fierceness.
- Air power's promise, corrected by its physics: seeing and reaching
  everything, holding nothing; "bombing alone decides" was tried
  repeatedly, and the correction is now color (the tried form is in the
  mirror).
- The inversion at the top: weapons finally terrible enough that
  stability came from being unable to defend — deterrence formalized as
  communication, the matrix written out, the strange peace of hostages
  in plain view held on purpose.
- The guerrilla lesson, learned expensively by the strong: kill counts
  measured the wrong quantity; the population was the board; legitimacy
  the object. Filed late, after much tuition.

## RELATIONS
First-class connections — the cascade's paths outward, each naming what
runs along it.

- GOVERNANCE: war as the state's costliest instrument; legitimacy as
  what makes ruling cheap and occupation ruinous; the force that
  protects a state can take it.
- LAW: the settlement as enforceable judgment; rules of war as
  mechanisms with payoffs (surrender must look survivable); a penalty
  deters only if expected — the deterrence law shared.
- ECONOMY: the production law; blockade as rationing by power;
  mercenary incentives (paying for presence buys presence); the
  arms-race matrix shared with every commons.
- MATHEMATICS: the matrix, the square law, the crossing curves,
  compounding disease and production — the arithmetic deciding what the
  stories only narrate.
- PHYSICS: force over area (concentration is cooking's knife law
  wearing armor); fragments spreading energy over area; movement
  costing energy the mover pays.
- MIND: expectation as deception's target; belief as what breaks; sunk
  cost keeping wars alive; overconfidence starting them; the reading of
  intelligence as the failure point.
- PEOPLE: the small group as the real unit of loyalty; collapse as a
  shared expectation failing all at once; the crowd's decision
  outrunning orders.
- BODY and MEDICINE: fear's removal of fine control (the reason drill
  exists); exhaustion and camp disease as casualty sources;
  transport-before-surgery; the veteran's invisible bill arriving late,
  looking like something else.
- TEACHING: drill as repetition past thought; doctrine as taught
  vocabulary; judgment versus compliance; the trap of learning the last
  war too well.
- ENGINEERING and BUILDING: walls and counters one generation apart
  forever; fortification as terrain made on purpose; the trench as the
  cheapest protection ever devised; redundancy from different failure
  modes (combined arms shares the principle).
- TRANSPORT: the whole of supply — wagon radius, barge stretch, truck
  turnaround; a fuel that must carry itself.
- EARTH and WEATHER: terrain granting sight, shot and movement
  unequally; chokepoints as targets; the central position's geometry;
  the season as a campaign's clock — mud, winter and harvest deciding
  what plans survive the calendar.
- FARMING: armies eating the countryside; harvests as strategic stores;
  blockade landing on food first.
- SURVIVAL: the besieged larder; the refugee's route — people leave in
  the direction that is open, in bursts, and return needs safety, a
  house, a job and a document, all slower than the war.
