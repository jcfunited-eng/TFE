## THINGS
The units this subject is about. Each sits in many phylums at once;
its other homes are named so a cascade can branch.

- THE OPPONENT — the thing that makes this subject different from
  every other: a learner who updates on your every move, so any
  law here is played against a mind, not a lock; also: mind
  (expectation), play (every game with two players), economy
  (competitors), governance.
- FORCE — organized capacity to compel: people, weapons, and the
  training that welds them; decides only where it is gathered;
  also: physics (pressure is force over area), governance (the
  state's monopoly on it), law.
- SUPPLY — the daily arriving tons an armed force eats and burns:
  food, water, fuel, ammunition, parts. An army is a machine for
  eating and burning, and everything it can do is bounded by what
  arrives; also: transport, economy, farming, upkeep.
- GROUND — terrain doing the work of soldiers: the hill, river,
  narrow street, and wood that make one defender worth several
  attackers; also: earth, weather, building (a wall is terrain
  made on purpose), farming.
- INFORMATION AND ITS SHADOW — what each side knows, guesses, and
  is fed; the fog is not a failure of the medium, it IS the
  medium; also: mind, measurement, computing, story.
- MORALE — the belief that staying is survivable and worth it; the
  real currency, because most contests end when one side stops
  believing, well before it stops being able; also: mind, people,
  body (what fear does), economy (a panic is morale collapsing
  while wearing money).
- THE PLAN — a walked map of possibilities, not a script; it dies
  on first contact and still pays, because the planner improvises
  from a map instead of a fog; also: mind, teaching, design.
- THE RESERVE — force deliberately held back; once battle starts
  it is the commander's entire remaining ability to affect the
  day; also: economy (slack as responsiveness), medicine (the
  crash team), upkeep.
- WALLS AND WEAPONS, IN PAIRS — every wall answers a particular
  weapon and every weapon answers a particular wall; neither side
  ever stays ahead; also: engineering, building, history-of-craft
  (not yet built as its own door; craft holds the making).
- THE SMALL GROUP — the eight people around a fighter; in the
  moment, people fight for them, not for causes; a unit constantly
  rebuilt from strangers does not fight; also: people, teaching,
  culture.
- THE POPULATION — in irregular war, the actual board: food,
  shelter, recruits, information, and silence flow from whichever
  side a village finds safer to help; also: people, governance,
  farming.
- THE THREAT — a promise of cost, doing its work inside the other
  side's head; the instrument of deterrence; also: mind, law (a
  penalty deters only if expected), language.
- THE SETTLEMENT — the agreement the fighting was for; it must be
  livable by the loser or it is a truce with a date on it; also:
  law, governance, story.
- TIME AND PATIENCE — a weapon in its own right: sieges, blockades,
  and insurgencies are all bets on who can keep consuming, or
  merely existing, longer; also: economy, survival.

## CLAIMS
What holds, written to be tested by a crossing query. Worth is
applicability to the query at hand, never advocacy.

- War is a means, never a thing in itself: it starts because
  somebody wants a decision they cannot get by asking, and stops
  when one side changes its mind about the price. The fighting is
  the method; the argument is the war.
- Force can destroy an army and cannot remove a disagreement — so
  whatever the war was about is still sitting there when the guns
  go quiet, and a settlement that does not deal with it is the
  next war wearing a later date.
- Over time, supply beats valor. The side that stays fed, armed,
  and rested outlasts the side that merely fights well; the
  arithmetic is in SCIENCE and it has no exceptions long enough
  to matter.
- Most soldiers are not fighting: they drive, cook, mend, carry,
  treat, and dig — in a modern force the shooters are a minority
  (roughly one in three or fewer). Not waste; the price of keeping
  the minority effective.
- The weakest part of an army is the road behind it. Cutting the
  supply needs a bridge, a depot, or a convoy, not a victory — so
  a much smaller force in the right place stops a much larger one
  without a battle.
- Battles are mostly decided before they are fought — by who chose
  the ground, the timing, and whether to fight at all. The
  strongest move is refusing contests already lost.
- The defender holds the cheaper hand: dug in, knowing the ranges,
  needing only not-to-lose, while the attacker crosses open
  distance and must win. The working rule of thumb is that an
  assault wants about three attackers per defender AT THE POINT of
  attack. The advantage flips when the attacker chooses the point
  and arrives before the defender can react — which is the entire
  purpose of mobility and surprise.
- Strength spread is strength spent. Force decides only where it
  is gathered, so the art is accepting weakness almost everywhere
  to be strong at the one point that matters (the square law in
  SCIENCE prices this exactly).
- Surprise is enormous and brief: an advantage made of the other
  side's ignorance ends the moment they learn, and using it is
  exactly how they learn. It must be spent in one go; a plan
  needing a second surprise is a plan with a hole in it.
- Deception cannot make an enemy believe nothing — only something
  else. The workable lie confirms what they already expect; the
  most valuable secret is not what you are doing but what you know
  they think. The defense is the mirror: weigh what the other side
  CAN do, never what it seems to intend.
- Intelligence rarely fails by missing information. The warning is
  usually in the files already — buried, contradicted, or refused
  because it did not fit the standing belief. The failure is in
  the reading, not the collecting.
- Every plan dies on first contact, and planning still pays: the
  future branches past any script, and a script with a second
  author is no longer a script.
- Friction is the medium: everything takes longer, arrives late,
  goes to the wrong place, and is reported wrongly — not from
  incompetence but because many tired, frightened people are
  coordinating in the dark.
- What breaks in a battle is a belief, not a body count. Units
  stop being useful at a casualty threshold well under half —
  they are not destroyed, they are switched off — and the
  decision to stop spreads faster than any order.
- Under fire, nobody rises to the occasion; everyone falls back to
  the level they were trained to. An army's real capability is
  whatever it rehearsed until it was boring.
- Drill exists to make people do together, without deciding, what
  no one would choose alone — because terror stops the thinking
  part, and a skill practiced past thought still runs.
- Most rounds fired hit nobody, and that is the design: fire that
  keeps heads down removes the other side's ability to shoot,
  move, and look. Hitting is a bonus; freedom of movement is the
  product.
- In industrial war, most battle casualties come from fragments
  and blast covering an area, not from aimed shots — which is why
  digging is the single most valuable thing a soldier does.
- For most of history, disease killed more soldiers than the
  enemy — crowding plus bad water in a hurried town of young men.
  Even now, illness, accidents, and exhaustion remove more people
  than combat; the true strength is present-and-fit, not on-paper.
- Whether the wounded live is decided mostly in the hours before
  surgery: stop the bleeding where they fell, then a fast trip to
  someone who can operate. The achievement is a transport system,
  not an operating theater.
- Two ways to win, needing different armies: take the enemy apart
  faster than they can react so their force stops working as a
  whole (cheaper, riskier), or destroy more than they can replace
  (slower, surer). The choice mostly reflects who has more to
  spend.
- Every advance weakens with each mile: the attacker's supply
  lengthens while the defender falls back onto its own depots. An
  offensive is strongest in the middle and most dangerous to
  itself at the end (the crossing curves are worked in SCIENCE).
- Escalation is a ratchet: each answer must slightly exceed the
  last or read as surrender, so conflicts climb by small steps
  nobody chose as a whole. Nobody picks the top of the ladder;
  everybody picks one more rung.
- A threat deters only when three things are all true: you can
  carry it out, they believe you will, and they know exactly what
  triggers it. Fail any one and the threat is noise — deterrence
  is mostly communication, not weapons (arithmetic in SCIENCE).
- Where both sides can destroy each other AFTER being struck,
  striking first buys nothing — stability comes from being unable
  to defend, not from being able. A peace built on hostages kept
  in plain view, maintained on purpose.
- Arms races are a trap of structure, not of judgment: each side's
  best individual move is to build, both end up poorer, and the
  balance finishes where it started. The spending was not stupid;
  the situation was (the worked matrix is in SCIENCE).
- Starting a war needs one decision; ending it needs two. By the
  time anyone wants out, the costs already paid demand to be
  redeemed, so terms acceptable at the start are humiliating by
  the end, and wars run past the point where either side wants
  them.
- A guerrilla does not need to win, only to keep existing; the
  government must protect everything and be seen to work. Time
  favors whoever needs less to count as succeeding.
- An insurgency lives on the population, so the fight is over
  which side people find it safer and more sensible to help —
  and a raid that kills a family recruits more fighters than it
  removes.
- Taking a country is the cheap part. Holding it means feeding,
  policing, and administering it without the legitimacy that
  normally makes ruling cheap — troops who cannot go home,
  indefinitely, with no victory available.
- Aircraft see everything, reach anywhere, and hold nothing; who
  governs the ground is settled by whoever stands on it. Denial
  from above is real; control from above is not.
- At sea there are two jobs at wildly different prices: control
  (your ships move, theirs do not — takes a fleet) and denial
  (nobody moves safely — a few submarines, mines, or missiles
  will do). Stopping is cheaper than guaranteeing, everywhere.
- Blockade works slowly and lands unfairly: scarcity is rationed
  by power, so the shortage reaches the weakest first and the
  army last — and it keeps working long after anyone watches.
- A sponsor arming somebody else's war is served by continuation,
  not resolution: the losing side keeps receiving enough to
  continue and never enough to win.
- Allies pull two ways at once: each wants the others to do the
  fighting, and each fears being abandoned if it hangs back — so
  alliances breed free-riding and entanglement from the same fear.
- Soldiers rarely surrender alone; a group decides together that
  resistance is over, and the decision leans on whether surrender
  looks survivable. A reputation for killing prisoners makes the
  enemy fight to the last — cruelty raises its own price.
- For most of history the killing happened AFTER the decision: a
  formation that holds loses a fraction; one that turns and runs
  is cut down from behind. The difference between defeat and
  catastrophe is staying together while withdrawing.
- Civil wars run longer and dirtier and end harder: no border, no
  exit, the enemy in the same street, and no going home because
  home is the thing being fought over.
- Most of the dead in nearly every war are not soldiers. They die
  of displacement, hunger, cold, and disease — the second-order
  work of broken water, food, and shelter — for years after the
  shooting stops.
- Almost every war is entered by BOTH sides expecting a quick
  result: each counts its own advantages, discounts the other's,
  and assumes the first month goes to plan. Long wars are two
  short-war assumptions colliding.
- The famous battle is usually the moment somebody noticed, not
  the moment it was decided; the result was set earlier by supply,
  production, and exhaustion, and the battle is where the
  accumulated advantage became visible enough to name.
- Casualty figures reported during a war are moves in the war, not
  measurements of it: who counts as a combatant, whether hunger
  counts, which period is measured — each definition moves the
  number before any counting begins.
- Doctrine is a vocabulary, not a rulebook: when everyone means
  the same thing by an order and a phase, thousands who cannot
  talk to each other still act as one.
- The one on the spot knows what is happening; headquarters knows
  what it is for. Telling subordinates the purpose and letting
  them pick the method beats waiting for instructions, because by
  the time an order arrives, its situation has gone.
- Armies learn expensively from the last war and then meet a
  different one — the better an institution learns, the more
  precisely it fits what is over.

## SCIENCE
The math of opposed wills, held — not mentioned. Each piece is a
LAW a query can compute with, a RULE it can follow, and a WORKED
case with the arithmetic run to an answer.

### THE MATRIX — choosing under an opponent

LAW: when two sides choose at once, each outcome pays each side a
  number, and a side compares its own payoffs DOWN its own choices
  while holding the other's choice fixed. An outcome is stable
  when neither side gains by switching alone. The stable outcome
  can be worse for both than one they both would prefer — the
  trap is in the structure, not the players.
RULE: to find where a standoff settles — write the little table of
  payoffs, circle each side's best answer to each of the other's
  moves, and look for the cell where both circles land. That cell
  is where it goes, like it or not.
WORKED (the arms race, and the security dilemma priced): each side
  chooses BUILD or HOLD. Building costs 2. Falling behind — they
  build, you hold — costs 4; a lead is worth 4 to whoever has it.
  · both HOLD: 0 and 0
  · I BUILD, they HOLD: I pay 2, gain the lead worth 4 → +2 for
    me; −4 for them
  · both BUILD: leads cancel, each is out its 2 → −2 and −2.
  Check my choices. If they HOLD: build pays +2, holding pays 0 —
  build. If they BUILD: build pays −2, holding pays −4 — build
  again. Building wins for me WHATEVER they do; the table is
  symmetric, so it wins for them too. Both build. Both land at
  −2 when 0 and 0 sat on the table. That is the security dilemma
  in four cells: my safety purchase reads as your threat, and two
  sensible players buy the worse world.
WORKED (the drain — same table, one number moved): add inspection
  that catches a lone builder before the lead is real. Now BUILD
  against HOLD pays the builder −2 (cost spent, lead denied) and
  the holder 0. If they HOLD: build −2, hold 0 — hold. If they
  BUILD: build −2, hold 0 — hold again. Holding now wins
  whatever they do, and 0 and 0 becomes the stable cell. One
  checkable promise moved one payoff and unlocked the trap —
  which is why treaties live or die on verification, not trust.

### THE SQUARE LAW — why concentration wins

LAW: under massed aimed fire, fighting strength grows with the
  SQUARE of numbers, because each fighter both shoots and soaks
  fire: N against a smaller M leaves the larger side roughly
  √(N² − M²) standing when M is gone.
RULE: to price an engagement — square both sides, subtract, take
  the square root; to price dividing a force — run the survivors
  of the first fight into the second.
WORKED: 500 meet 400. 500² = 250,000; 400² = 160,000; difference
  90,000; √90,000 = 300. The larger side finishes with ~300 —
  outnumbering by a quarter, it loses well under half while
  destroying the whole of the smaller force.
WORKED (defeat in detail — the same 400, split): the 400 divide
  into two 200s met one after the other. First fight: 250,000 −
  40,000 = 210,000 → ~458 survive. Second: 210,000 − 40,000 =
  170,000 → ~412 survive. Split, the 400 cost the enemy ~88; kept
  together they cost ~200. Strength spread IS strength spent —
  this arithmetic is why the side that concentrates at the chosen
  point wins battles it would lose everywhere at once, why a
  central position that moves reserves faster than the ring
  around it punches above its total, and why "divide the enemy"
  appears in every tradition independently.

### SURPRISE — the arithmetic of not knowing where

LAW: a defender must divide across every point the attacker MIGHT
  choose; the attacker masses at the one point he DID choose.
  Local odds = attacker's mass ÷ (defender's total ÷ points that
  must be guarded).
RULE: to price surprise — count the points the defender cannot
  leave open, divide his force by that count, and compare the
  attacker's whole force against that fraction.
WORKED: 10 battalions defend 5 passes — 2 per pass. An attacker
  with 6 total, knowing his own choice, masses all 6 at one pass:
  6 against 2 is 3-to-1 at the point of contact — assault odds —
  for a force outnumbered 10 to 6 overall. That is surprise as
  information asymmetry, in one division. And the bill: the used
  surprise teaches. Next season the defender has read the tell,
  and the same move meets 8. Capital, spent in the using.

### SUPPLY — the arithmetic that bounds everything

LAW: daily need = heads × daily consumption. A pre-industrial
  soldier ate ~1.5 kg a day and a horse ~10 kg; a modern
  mechanized force runs ~100 kg per soldier per day and up, once
  fuel, ammunition, and parts are counted.
RULE: to size a force's leash — multiply heads by the daily
  figure; that tonnage must arrive EVERY day, and whatever cannot
  arrive, the force cannot do.
WORKED: 100,000 soldiers, mechanized: 100,000 × 100 kg =
  10,000,000 kg = 10,000 tonnes a day — about 1,000 ten-tonne
  truckloads daily just to stand still. Before any plan is a
  fighting plan it is this delivery problem, solved or not.

LAW: the carriage eats its cargo. delivered = load − (crew and
  team's daily consumption × round-trip days). At some distance
  the delivery is zero — the dead radius.
RULE: to find the dead radius — set delivered to zero and solve
  for distance; to find the honest radius, stop at half-eaten.
WORKED: a wagon hauls 1,000 kg behind a team eating 50 kg of
  fodder a day, making 25 km a day, and must come back:
  round-trip days = 2D ÷ 25, fodder = 50 × 2D ÷ 25 = 4D kg.
  Delivered = 1,000 − 4D: zero at D = 250 km, half-gone at 125 km.
  A cart's useful reach from its base was ~125 km, full stop —
  which is why pre-rail armies hugged rivers and coasts (a barge
  carries ~30 wagonloads past the same mouths, stretching the
  radius ~30-fold), why empires ended where their logistics did,
  and why fierceness never moved this number one kilometer.

LAW (the advance weakens itself): deliveries per vehicle per day =
  working hours ÷ round-trip time, and round-trip time grows with
  distance from the depot — while the retreating defender's
  distance to ITS depots shrinks. The curves cross; past the
  crossing the attacker is weaker every mile it succeeds.
RULE: to find what an advance does to its own supply — recompute
  trips per day at the new distance and scale deliveries by the
  ratio.
WORKED: trucks at 40 km/h, 8 driving hours a day. Depot 40 km
  back: round trip 80 km = 2 h → 4 loads a day. The advance
  succeeds 120 km; depot now 160 km back: round trip 320 km =
  8 h → 1 load a day. The same fleet delivers ONE QUARTER — the
  army quartered its own supply by winning, while the defender
  fell back onto full depots. This is the culminating point as
  arithmetic: why offensives stall with no one resisting them,
  and why the moment of greatest apparent victory is the moment
  of greatest real danger.

### THE LONG WAR — production against fierceness

LAW: force next month = force this month − losses + replacements.
  Whoever's replacement rate exceeds its loss rate GROWS while
  fighting; the gap compounds monthly, and no skill at the front
  closes a large enough gap in output.
RULE: to call a long war — take each side's monthly net (built
  minus lost) and project the lines; where they cross, the
  material verdict is in, whatever the headlines say.
WORKED: side A starts with 3,000 tanks, loses 200 a month, builds
  100 — net −100. Side B starts with 2,000, fights WORSE — loses
  250 a month — but builds 400: net +150. Crossing: 3,000 − 100t
  = 2,000 + 150t → 1,000 = 250t → t = 4 months (both at 2,600).
  By month 12: A has 3,000 − 1,200 = 1,800; B has 2,000 + 1,800 =
  3,800 — more than double. B loses every exchange and wins the
  war by arithmetic. This single computation is the grave of
  "victory by fierceness alone" (the mirror holds the receipts).

### THE SIEGE — two larders and a clock

LAW: days of holding = stores ÷ daily consumption; the ration is
  the lever. Both sides consume continuously, so the contest is
  between two larders — plus disease, which compounds in a
  crowded camp with bad water.
RULE: to call a siege — compute each side's days of supply,
  including the besieger's haul from ITS base, then discount the
  camp by disease's compounding.
WORKED: a town of 30,000 eating 1 kg a head holds 2,700 tonnes:
  2,700,000 ÷ 30,000 = 90 days; halve the ration → 180. The
  besieging 20,000 eat ~30 tonnes a day, every kilogram hauled in
  (the wagon law above prices that), and camp sickness at a rough
  ~2% a week compounds: 0.98²⁶ ≈ 0.59 — about 40% of the
  besieging army gone in 180 days without a single assault. This
  is why the historical record shows besiegers starving and
  sickening first so often, and why patience is listed under
  MEANS as a weapon.

### DETERRENCE — cost inside the other head

LAW: a threat deters when (believed cost) × (believed chance it is
  carried out) exceeds the prize. All three parts live in the
  OTHER side's head; the true cost and the true resolve enter
  only through belief.
RULE: to test a deterrent — price the prize as the other side
  prices it, multiply the threatened cost by the chance THEY
  assign, and compare. To repair one, work on the belief and the
  clarity of the trigger, not the warhead.
WORKED: the prize is worth 10. The threatened cost is 100, but
  they believe it will be carried out 1 time in 20: 100 × 0.05 =
  5 < 10 — they come. Same weapon, belief raised to 1 in 5:
  100 × 0.20 = 20 > 10 — they stay home. Nothing physical
  changed; the communication did. And the mutual case: where each
  side keeps the certain ability to answer AFTER being struck,
  the first blow's payoff is ruin times near-certainty — striking
  buys nothing, and the stability rests, exactly backwards from
  instinct, on both sides staying UNABLE to defend.

## METHODS
How things are done here — each with what governs it and what
breaks it. Described, never advocated.

- CHOOSING THE GROUND, THE TIME, AND WHETHER (the pre-battle
  method): odds cannot be beaten, only chosen, and the choosing
  happens before contact. Refusing a lost contest is the
  strongest move in the book. Breaks when: pride, sunk cost, or
  an ally's dragging makes the refusal unavailable.
- CONCENTRATION (the square law made deliberate): accept weakness
  almost everywhere; be strong at the point that matters. Breaks
  when: the enemy learns the point (surprise spent), or the
  stripped places were not as safe as accounted.
- DEFENSE AND FORTIFICATION (the cheaper hand, stored): dig,
  range, wait; make ground do soldiers' work; a wall is stored
  defensive advantage. Breaks when: mobility lets the attacker
  pick an unwatched point, or the wall answers the LAST war's
  weapon (the mirror holds that graveyard).
- TAKING APART VS GRINDING DOWN (the two victories): break the
  enemy's coherence faster than it reacts, or consume its
  substance faster than it replaces. Governed by the production
  law. Breaks when: a coherence-breaker runs long (its supply
  arithmetic collapses), or a grinder is out-produced.
- COMBINED ARMS (no good answer left): pair weapons whose
  weaknesses differ — armor blind in towns, infantry naked in the
  open, artillery unable to hold — so every response the enemy
  picks exposes it to the partner. Breaks when: one arm outruns
  the others and meets its counter alone.
- DECEPTION (expectation management): feed the story they already
  prefer; spend the surprise all at once. Governed by: the
  matrix — and by its mirror-defense, weighing capacity, never
  apparent intent. Breaks when: repeated (the trick that wins
  Sunday is dead film by Monday).
- DRILL AND DOCTRINE (capability installed in advance): rehearse
  past thought, because under fire everyone falls to the level of
  training; teach a shared vocabulary so thousands who cannot
  talk act as one. Breaks when: it hardens into fitting the last
  war precisely.
- PURPOSE-DOWN COMMAND (intent, not instructions): tell the one
  on the spot what it is FOR and let them pick the method — the
  order's situation is gone before the order arrives. Breaks
  when: subordinates were trained to comply, not to judge.
- THE RESERVE (control kept by not spending): hold force back on
  purpose; commit it at the moment that decides. Governed by:
  slack as the only source of responsiveness. Breaks when:
  everything is committed early — then the commander can only
  watch.
- ATTACKING THE ROAD, NOT THE ARMY (the cheap lever): the bridge,
  the depot, the convoy — a chain fails at its cheapest link.
  Governed by the supply laws. Breaks when: the enemy's stores or
  routes are redundant enough to reroute.
- SIEGE AND BLOCKADE (patience as the weapon): outlast the other
  larder; scarcity does the fighting. Governed by the siege
  arithmetic. Breaks when: your own larder, health, or patience
  runs out first — as it historically often did; and blockade's
  cost lands on the weakest first, which is part of its true
  price, filed honestly.
- DENIAL (the weaker side's purchase): make the thing unusable
  rather than owned — mines, submarines, raids; stopping is
  cheaper than guaranteeing. Breaks when: the object was control
  all along — denial never governs a single street.
- DETERRENCE (cost made credible in advance): capability, believed
  will, and a clear trigger — all three, communicated. Governed
  by the deterrence law. Breaks when: any leg fails, the trigger
  blurs, or the threatened side stops believing the answer
  survives the first blow.
- INSURGENCY AND ITS COUNTER (the population as the board): the
  insurgent needs only to exist and to show the government cannot
  deliver; the counter is protection and services that make
  helping the government the safer bet, village by village.
  Breaks when: raids kill families — each recruits more than it
  removes; or when kill counts are read as the score (they
  measure the wrong quantity).
- ENDING IT (the hardest method): a settlement the loser can live
  with, or a loser genuinely unable to try again — one or the
  other, honestly. Governed by: starting takes one decision,
  ending takes two; sunk costs harden both. Breaks when: terms
  are harsh enough to enrage and mild enough to permit — the
  standard recipe for the sequel.

## MEANS
What the methods run on, with the numbers that matter.

- THE TRAIN — wagons, barges, rails, trucks, transports: the
  wagon's ~125 km honest radius, the barge's ~30-fold stretch,
  the truck fleet's trips-per-day law. The reach of every force
  is a property of these, not of its courage.
- ROADS, BRIDGES, PORTS, DEPOTS — the chain's links, each a
  target worth more than a battle; redundancy is armor for
  supply.
- GROUND ITSELF — the free force-multiplier: the hill, the river
  line, the narrow place; and the spade, the cheapest protection
  ever devised against the fragments that cause most casualties.
- WALLS AND THEIR HEIRS — palisade, stone, earth, concrete, and
  the mobile counters that answered each; stored defense, always
  one generation from obsolete.
- WEAPONS IN PAIRS — each with a counter, fielded so the counters
  cover each other; a lone arm is a solved problem.
- MAPS, SCOUTS, SIGNALS, AND FILES — the information means; worth
  exactly as much as the READING applied to them, which is where
  failures actually live.
- THE DRILL FIELD — where capability is installed in advance;
  what is rehearsed until boring is the whole of what exists
  under fire.
- THE SMALL GROUP, KEPT TOGETHER — cohesion as equipment:
  units rotated and rebuilt as blocks fight; units patched with
  strangers do not.
- THE MEDICAL CHAIN — tourniquet where they fell, fast transport,
  then surgery: survival is bought in the first hours, by a
  transport system.
- THE FACTORY AND THE TREASURY — in any long war, the deciding
  means; the production law runs on them.
- THE WORD — treaty, threat, guarantee: instruments that work
  only while believed, priced by the deterrence law, and (as the
  matrix shows) transformed by anything that makes them checkable.

## PURPOSE
The wants this subject answers — the doors a query enters by.

- GET A DECISION ASKING COULD NOT — the founding purpose; force
  as the costliest method of settling a disagreement, chosen when
  the others look slower or weaker.
- KEEP WHAT IS HELD WITHOUT FIGHTING — deterrence: make the cost
  legible in advance so the contest never starts.
- SURVIVE THE STRONGER — the weaker side's whole menu: denial,
  ground, patience, existence as victory.
- SPEND LESS OF WHAT IS LOVED — every law here that prices cost
  (supply, concentration, the reserve, the medical chain) serves
  the want of getting the decision at the least ruin.
- END IT SO IT STAYS ENDED — the settlement livable by the loser;
  the purpose most often failed, so its failures fill the mirror.
- UNDERSTAND IT TO PREVENT IT — the study's oldest justification;
  served here, answered nowhere yet (the mirror holds the open
  want).

## HISTORY
How the knowledge arose, in what order, at what cost.

- Ground and supply first, older than writing: every early manual
  that survives is mostly about marches, stores, and choosing
  ground — the fighting chapters are the short ones. The oldest
  knowledge here is the least glamorous, because it was paid for
  first.
- The walls-and-weapons wheel, turning through the whole record:
  high stone stopped ladders and fell to cannon; low thick earth
  stopped cannon and cost more than castles; concrete stopped
  shells and was walked around. Each turn taught the same lesson
  to a new generation at full price: a defense is an answer to a
  named threat, and threats get renamed.
- Disease's long reign: for most of the record, the camp killed
  more than the enemy, and the knowledge that drains and clean
  water outfight doctors arrived only when public health did —
  late, and from outside the subject.
- The rail and the timetable: mass mobilization turned armies
  into schedules — visible, hard to reverse, watchable by the
  other side — and crises into races nobody meant to start. The
  knowledge that the SIGNAL of preparing can itself cause the war
  dates from this.
- The industrial verdict: the long wars of factories settled it
  that past a certain length, output decides — the production
  law was written in those receipts, against every romance of
  fierceness (the mirror holds the romance's graves).
- Air power's promise, corrected by its physics: seeing and
  reaching everything, holding nothing; the claim that bombing
  alone decides was tried repeatedly and the correction is now
  color (see the mirror for the tried-and-failed form).
- The inversion at the top: weapons finally terrible enough that
  the stability came from being unable to defend — deterrence
  formalized as communication, the matrix written out, and the
  strange peace of hostages in plain view held on purpose.
- The guerrilla lesson, learned expensively by the strong: kill
  counts measured the wrong quantity; the population was the
  board; legitimacy was the object. Filed late, after much
  tuition was paid.

## RELATIONS
First-class connections — the cascade's paths outward. Each names
what runs along it.

- GOVERNANCE: war as the state's costliest instrument; legitimacy
  as the thing that makes ruling cheap and whose absence makes
  occupation ruinous; the force that protects a state can take
  it.
- LAW: the settlement as enforceable judgment; rules of war as
  mechanisms with practical payoffs (surrender must look
  survivable); a penalty deters only if expected — the deterrence
  law shared.
- ECONOMY: the production law; blockade as rationing by power;
  mercenary incentives (paying for presence buys presence); the
  arms race matrix shared with every commons.
- MATHEMATICS: the matrix, the square law, the crossing curves,
  compounding disease and production — the arithmetic that
  decides what the stories only narrate.
- PHYSICS: force over area (concentration is cooking's knife law
  wearing armor); fragments spreading energy over area; movement
  costing energy that the mover pays.
- MIND: expectation as the target of deception; belief as what
  actually breaks; sunk cost keeping wars alive; overconfidence
  starting them; the reading of intelligence as the failure
  point.
- PEOPLE: the small group as the real unit of loyalty; the
  collapse threshold as a shared expectation failing all at once;
  the crowd's decision spreading faster than orders.
- BODY: what fear does to fine control and thinking — the reason
  drill exists; exhaustion as a casualty source.
- MEDICINE: the transport-before-surgery law; camp disease; the
  veteran's invisible bill arriving late and looking like
  something else.
- TEACHING: drill as repetition past thought; doctrine as taught
  vocabulary; training judgment versus compliance; the trap of
  learning the last war too well.
- ENGINEERING: walls and counters, one generation apart forever;
  redundancy from different failure modes (combined arms shares
  the principle); the bottleneck that moves rather than
  disappears.
- BUILDING: fortification as terrain made on purpose; the trench
  as the cheapest protection ever devised.
- TRANSPORT: the whole of supply — the wagon radius, the barge
  stretch, the truck turnaround; a fuel that must carry itself.
- EARTH: terrain granting sight, shot, and movement unequally;
  chokepoints readable as targets; the central position's
  geometry.
- WEATHER: the season as a campaign's clock; mud, winter, and
  harvest deciding what plans survive contact with the calendar.
- FARMING: armies eating the countryside; harvests as strategic
  stores; blockade landing on food first.
- SURVIVAL: the besieged larder, the refugee's route (people
  leave in the direction that is open, in bursts, and return
  needs safety, a house, a job, and a document — all slower than
  the war).
- STORY: the decisive-battle narrative as hindsight's convenience
  (the mirror holds where it broke); casualty figures as moves;
  war memory as the culture's inheritance of scarcity survived.
- MEASUREMENT: definitions deciding numbers before counting
  begins; present-and-fit versus on-paper strength; the metric
  that measures the wrong quantity losing the war it was
  supposed to score.
- LANGUAGE: doctrine as shared meaning; the threat as an act of
  communication; orders as compressed intent.
- PLAY: the matrix shared with every two-player game; sport as
  the same asymmetries rehearsed for stakes of nothing.
- CULTURE: why units cohere, what face costs, which settlements
  can be sold at home; the feud kept alive by nobody being able
  to be the one who stopped.
- ETHICS: described-never-advocated is itself an ethical stance;
  the second-order dead as the true bill; cruelty raising its own
  price as a fact about the world, not merely a judgment.
- BELIEF: causes worth dying for as morale's deep reservoir — and
  the finding that in the moment, the eight nearby outweigh the
  abstraction.
- FLIGHT: air power's real gifts and real limits — denial from
  above, control never.
- UPKEEP: availability versus inventory as the true strength;
  repair and maintenance as the quiet majority of capability.
