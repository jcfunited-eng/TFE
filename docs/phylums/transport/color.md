## THINGS
The units this subject is about; other homes named so a cascade can branch.

- THE LOAD — freight and the passenger: the thing that must be somewhere
  else, priced by mass, bulk, clock (perishables), and address; also:
  economy, farming, people.
- THE VEHICLE — an argument with a medium: ship, train, lorry, aircraft;
  the medium decides its shape before any designer does; also:
  engineering, design, physics.
- THE PATH — prepared (rail, canal, runway) or free (road, sea, sky):
  bought once and fed with traffic, or straight to the door; also:
  building, economy, earth.
- THE CONTAINER — the standard box: not steel but an agreement, sealed at
  the factory, untouched to the shop, one shape every crane, ship, train,
  and lorry is built around; also: law (a standard is a treaty),
  computing (data moves in fixed parcels), craft.
- THE JOIN — port, terminal, hub, border, doorstep: where a journey
  changes hands or modes; nearly all the waiting and damage live here;
  also: building, law, body (the heart as hub).
- THE QUEUE — vehicles or goods waiting at one busy resource; made by
  variability, not shortage; also: mathematics, computing, medicine.
- THE TIMETABLE — a solved puzzle, not a plan: every vehicle, crew, and
  slot constraining all the others, padded on purpose so one delay dies
  instead of cascading; also: mathematics, teaching, engineering.
- THE FUEL ABOARD — a vehicle carries its own energy, so fuel is cargo
  displacing cargo; range and payload spend one weight budget; also:
  physics, flight, survival (carrying your own water).
- THE BUFFER — warehouse and stock: the shelf between journeys, a paid
  bet against demand, delay, and defect; also: economy, farming (the
  granary), body (fat stores).
- THE PAPER — manifest, customs code, the named handover point where risk
  changes owners; stops goods as hard as a broken axle; also: law,
  governance, economy.
- THE CREW — driver, pilot, crane hand, picker: cost is charged by the
  human touch, and the count of touches is a design choice; also:
  people, economy, craft.

## CLAIMS
What holds, written to be tested by a crossing query.

- One number predicts almost everything about a mode: energy per
  tonne-kilometre. Rank ship, train, lorry, aircraft by it and you have
  ranked costs, routes, emissions, and which goods can afford them.
- Two resistances fight every vehicle and swap places with speed:
  rolling stays steady, drag grows with speed squared — the crossover
  sets every sensible cruising speed ever chosen.
- Steel wheel on steel rail is the lowest rolling resistance in practice
  (~5x less than tyre), so a modest engine pulls thousands of tonnes;
  the same hard contact grips poorly, so rail is flat, signalled, and
  dear to lay. Prepared paths are cheap and must be fed; free paths are
  flexible and dear — so efficient modes are fed by flexible ones, and
  almost every real journey uses both.
- The last mile costs most because the load stops being a load: one
  container crosses an ocean as one item and arrives as a thousand
  addresses, each with its own door and its own person who is out.
  Scale lives in the middle of a journey and dies at its ends.
- On short journeys the vehicle is barely the point: loading, paperwork,
  and slots swallow the time. Door-to-door time is mostly spent
  stationary at the joins, and most damage happens in the handling.
- The container's invention was the abolition of touching the contents —
  handling collapsed from days to hours, and the agreement did more for
  trade than any faster ship (arithmetic in SCIENCE).
- Pairs multiply faster than members, so hubs buy frequency and
  connection — paying in distance, double handling, and one place where
  a bad night delays everything; direct wins the moment one lane can
  fill a vehicle by itself.
- Congestion is not too many vehicles but a system near capacity, where
  disturbances arrive faster than they clear — delay negligible below,
  vertical above. And the jam has no author: each driver picks the fast
  road, which is what makes it slow.
- Every trip is secretly a round trip: the vehicle must come home, and
  an empty return means the paying leg carries both fares. Freight
  rarely balances, so whole trades exist to fill the ride back, and the
  empty direction is the cheap direction.
- A supply chain is a chain: the shelf empties when any link snaps, and
  its strength is its weakest link, never its average — requirements in
  a row multiply (arithmetic in SCIENCE).
- Stock is sized by the wait and the wobble, never the average — halve
  the lead time, halve the stock — and a dependable slow supplier beats
  a fast erratic one: plans are sized by the worst case, so reliability
  is worth more than speed.
- A system produces what its slowest stage produces. An hour lost at
  the bottleneck is lost by the whole; an hour saved elsewhere is worth
  nothing. Fix the bottleneck and it does not vanish — it moves.
- Running with almost no stock works only when everything is
  dependable: the buffer was hiding every fault it absorbed. And nobody
  in a chain is paid for the flow — buyer for price, plant for
  busyness — each hits their number while the whole slows.
- Freight is charged on the larger of weight and bulk: a lorry of
  pillows pays as bricks, so packaging is a transport decision, and a
  box a third empty buys a third of a vehicle to move air.
- The medium shapes the machine: dense slow water makes enormous
  unhurried ships; thin fast air makes light aircraft that cannot
  pause; solid land makes small vehicles that stop anywhere. And cargo
  grows with the cube of a hull while its skin grows with the square —
  ships grew until ports and canals said stop.
- Where the weight sits decides control — loading aircraft and ships is
  a calculation, and a half-full tank sloshing across a wide deck can
  capsize what a full one cannot. A tyre has one budget of grip shared
  by turning, speeding, stopping. A vehicle is designed around its
  worst hour and carries that unused capability all its life.
- Distance died for words but not for bodies or hours: a message
  crosses the world in a blink for nothing while goods ride ships for
  weeks — and the planet still turns, so noon is local forever and
  markets hand off around the sun.
- Cities sit where journeys change mode — ford, river mouth, pass,
  harbor: wherever cargo must be lifted from one carrier to another,
  people gather to do the lifting.

## SCIENCE
The math and physics of moving, held — not mentioned: LAW to compute
with, RULE to follow, WORKED run to a real answer.

### PHYSICS — the price of moving a tonne

LAW: rolling force = coefficient x mass x gravity, steady with speed
  (lorry tyre ~0.007 · steel on rail ~0.0015 · car ~0.010); drag force
  = ½ x air density (~1.2 kg/m³) x drag area x speed², and drag POWER
  grows with speed cubed.
RULE: set the two equal and solve for speed — below it rolling
  dominates; above it drag runs away with everything.
WORKED: a 1,500 kg car, drag area 0.7 m²: rolling = 0.010 x 1,500 x
  9.8 = 147 N; drag matches at ½ x 1.2 x 0.7 x v² = 147 → v² = 350 →
  ~19 m/s ≈ 67 km/h. At 130 km/h (36 m/s): drag = 0.42 x 1,300 ≈ 546 N,
  ~4x rolling; wheel energy (147+546) x 1,000 ≈ 0.69 MJ/km against
  ≈ 0.41 at 90 km/h — the slower run spends ~41% less. This is why
  lorries are governed and motorway speed owns the fuel bill.

LAW: the energy ladder, fuel energy per tonne-km — big ship ~0.05 MJ ·
  rail ~0.2-0.3 · lorry ~0.7-1 · aircraft ~5-10; fuel holds ~43 MJ/kg.
RULE: tonnes x kilometres x the mode's figure; ÷43 for kg of fuel.
WORKED: move 10 tonnes 1,000 km (10,000 t-km): ship 10,000 x 0.05 =
  500 MJ → ÷43 ≈ 12 kg of fuel; lorry 10,000 x 0.9 = 9,000 MJ → ≈ 209
  kg; air 10,000 x 7 = 70,000 MJ → ≈ 1,628 kg — 1.6 tonnes of fuel to
  move 10, a ~140-fold ship-to-air spread. That is the geography of
  trade: ~9 of 10 traded tonnes go by sea, grain travels and
  strawberries fly, and the mode is picked by value and urgency, never
  by distance.

LAW: a displacement hull is walled at the speed of the wave its own
  length makes: v ≈ 1.25 x √(length in m), in m/s.
WORKED: a 10 m boat: √10 ≈ 3.16 → ~4 m/s ≈ 7.7 knots — a wall, not a
  slope. A 300 m ship: ~21.7 m/s ≈ 42 knots, so its 22-knot cruise
  loafs below the wall — length is speed, one more reason ships grew
  (and doubling a hull gives 8x cargo for 4x skin: drag per tonne
  halves each doubling). Above the wall there is only planing: ride on
  top, pay in fuel and slamming.

### PHYSICS — stopping and crashing

LAW: energy of motion = ½ x mass x speed² — double the speed, four
  times the energy; crash force = that energy ÷ the stopping distance.
WORKED: 1,500 kg at 50 km/h (13.9 m/s): ½ x 1,500 x 193 ≈ 145,000 J;
  into a 0.6 m crumple: ÷0.6 ≈ 242,000 N — ~16 g, survivable. At 100
  km/h: ≈ 580,000 J → 966,000 N — ~66 g, not. Every survivable design
  stretches the stop: crumple zones, yielding barriers, helmets.
RULE: stopping = speed x reaction time + speed² ÷ (2 x grip x gravity).
WORKED: 1.5 s reaction, dry grip 0.7: at 50 km/h — 20.8 + 14.1 ≈ 35 m;
  at 100 — 41.7 + 56.3 ≈ 98 m: double the speed, 2.8x the distance.
  Wet (0.4) at 100: 41.7 + 98.4 ≈ 140 m.
WORKED, why railways run on signals: steel grip ~0.1, so a train at
  160 km/h (44.4 m/s) needs 1,975 ÷ 1.96 ≈ 1,000 m — far beyond
  eyesight, so protection must be information: blocks and signals. On
  the runway the same square law prices the take-off run and the
  abort, and thin hot air raises the needed speed while weakening the
  engines: ~23% more runway per 10% thinning (1 ÷ 0.9² = 1.23).

### MATHEMATICS — networks, queues, chains

LAW: direct links between n places = n x (n-1) ÷ 2; a hub needs n.
WORKED: 10 cities direct: 45 routes; hubbed: 10. Fifty cities: 1,225
  against 50 — the hub fills vehicles and gives thin routes daily
  service, adding a transfer, a wait, and one point of collapse.

LAW: waiting at a busy resource ≈ (busy ÷ idle fraction) x service
  time, when arrivals and service both wobble.
RULE: never plan a shared resource near full — the last few percent of
  capacity cost almost all the delay.
WORKED: a junction serves one car per 2 s (1,800/hour). At 1,620
  arriving (90% busy): wait ≈ 9 x 2 = 18 s; at 1,710 (95%): 38 s; at
  1,782 (99%): 198 s. A 10% traffic rise turned 18 seconds into 3
  minutes — the wall, the same at ports, hospitals, and servers.

LAW: things in the system = arrival rate x time inside (any steady flow).
WORKED: a motorway section holding 1,200 cars passing 4,000/hour:
  1,200 ÷ 4,000 = 0.3 h = 18 minutes through. A port with 30 ships at
  anchor clearing 10 a day: a 3-day wait, computable from any deck.

LAW: a chain of delayed followers amplifies — when the time gap to the
  one ahead is shorter than the reaction time, each correction must be
  sharper than the disturbance received.
WORKED, the jam from nothing: at 90 km/h a car covers 37.5 m in a
  1.5 s reaction; spaced 25 m (a 1 s gap), each driver brakes HARDER
  than the one ahead, and a tap of the brakes compounds to a
  standstill a kilometre back — the wave rolling upstream at ~15-20
  km/h with no cause at its head. The same law runs up a supply chain
  (the bullwhip): each level sees only the orders below, adds safety,
  batches — a ~5% ripple at the till can swing ~40% at the factory,
  which then blames a wild market for its own echo.

LAW: requirements in a row multiply — chain reliability = one link's
  reliability raised to the number of links.
WORKED: five links each 98% on-time: 0.98⁵ ≈ 0.90; twenty links:
  0.98²⁰ ≈ 0.67 — one-in-three late from links each nearly perfect.
  Delays add the same way: three weeks of ocean, another three at
  berths, customs, slots, appointments — the joins, again.

### THE FREIGHT ARITHMETIC — this subject's own working math

LAW: every mode's cost = a fixed part + a per-distance part: sea and
  rail huge-fixed and tiny-per-mile (want to be full and long); road
  moderate at both ends; air small-fixed and dear-per-mile.
RULE: pick the mode by the shape of its cost at your distance and
  volume, never by its top speed.

WORKED, the standard box: hand-stowing cost ~$5.86 a tonne (1956); the
  container ~$0.16 — a ~37-fold cut. A dock gang moved ~20 tonnes an
  hour; one crane ~30 boxes x 20 t = ~600: loading 10,000 tonnes fell
  from 500 gang-hours to ~17 crane-hours. Ships stopped living at the
  quay, and freight's share of a shelf price fell toward nothing. The
  value is the sameness, not the box.

WORKED, the last mile: a 40 ft box at ~$2,000 ocean freight holding
  ~50,000 shirts: ~4 cents a shirt for 10,000 km. A courier van at
  ~$300 a day making ~130 stops: ~$2.30 a doorstep — ~58x the ocean,
  spent on the last 5 km, ~0.05% of the distance. Shared costs divide;
  one door splits nothing.

RULE, chargeable weight: charge the larger of actual weight and volume
  ÷ ~6,000 cm³/kg (air): a 50x40x30 cm box = 60,000 cm³ → 10 kg, even
  holding a 2 kg pillow.

LAW: the shelf between journeys is a reservoir sized by outflow over
  refill time, plus safety growing with the demand wobble and the
  SQUARE ROOT of the wait.
WORKED: selling 40 a day on a 20-day lead: reorder at 800. Daily
  wobble ~10: over 20 days it compounds to 10 x √20 ≈ 45, so ~90 of
  safety covers ~19 days in 20; cut the lead to 5 days: 10 x √5 ≈ 22
  → ~45 — quarter the wait, HALF the safety. Shortening the wait
  beats sharpening the forecast. Same root, pooled: four depots
  holding their own safety carry ~2x what one shared pool needs
  (√4 = 2).
RULE, the one-shot order (Christmas stock, the paper run): order at
  the demand percentile = profit-if-sold ÷ (profit + loss-if-unsold);
  a 50c paper selling at $2: 1.50 ÷ 2.00 = 75% — deliberately above
  the forecast.
RULE, order size: the best batch balances ordering against holding
  cost, moves with the square root of demand, and sits on a flat
  bottom — ~30% off the optimum costs ~3.5%. Batches are a symptom of
  changeover cost: make changeover cheap and small batches follow.
WORKED, flow beats batch: 10 items through 3 one-minute stations one
  at a time: first out in 3 minutes, last in 12; as one batch: first
  out in 30. Nothing sped up — waiting was removed.

## METHODS
How moving is done here — with what governs each and what breaks it.

- CONTAINERIZE (seal once, touch never): pack at the factory, open at
  the shop. Breaks at the last mile, where loads stop being loads.
- HUB THE THIN FLOWS, RUN THE FAT ONES DIRECT: governed by the pairs
  law; broken by one bad night at the hub.
- CONSOLIDATE (wait to fill): the full vehicle is cheapest and filling
  takes the customer's time — a clock decision dressed as a cost
  decision. Cross-dock where flows synchronize: sort, never store.
- PAD THE TIMETABLE: slack placed on purpose absorbs the constant
  small delays; a tight schedule passes each one along until a
  two-minute problem is a network collapse by afternoon.
- SIGNAL AND SEPARATE: where stopping outruns sight (rail) or wakes
  persist (air), safety is information — blocks and spacing in three
  dimensions and time. Capacity IS the safe gap: a runway at ~one
  movement per 60-90 s is full.
- NAVIGATE BY RECKONING AND FIX: speed, heading, and time give a
  position that drifts; a star, beacon, or satellite resets it — the
  art is knowing how far the estimate has decayed. In wind or current,
  crab: point away from where you are going to arrive there; the
  offset is a triangle nobody skips.
- LOAD BY CALCULATION: keep the weight point inside the envelope (too
  far back is the unrecoverable direction), tanks full or empty (the
  slosh), lashings for the worst hour.
- PROTECT THE BOTTLENECK: find the slowest stage, feed it, never let
  it idle — then spend to enlarge it, and ask where the limit moved.
- SHIFT THE DEMAND, NOT THE ROAD: off-peak prices, delivery slots, and
  quoted lead times move journeys into capacity that exists, at a
  fraction of the cost of building more (the mirror holds what
  building more did).
- RUN THE COLD CHAIN: a smaller, scarcer network planned against
  expiry, not dates — ship in order of when goods die; a delay is not
  lateness but total loss.
- CLEAR THE PAPER FIRST: the document is part of the shipment — one
  mismatched code holds a container for weeks; agree the handover
  point in writing, and who pays, insures, and owns the delay follows.
- SECOND-SOURCE THE PATH, NOT THE NAME: suppliers sharing a factory,
  port, or country three tiers down are one supplier; a backup that
  never gets an order has already lapsed — exercise it or lose it.
- HANDLE RETURNS AS A DIFFERENT TRADE: outbound is identical items in
  flow; returns arrive alone, unforecast, in unknown condition,
  needing judgement — many times the outbound cost. And stock spares
  by consequence: years of stock of what may never move, priced by
  the day the machine stands still.

## MEANS
What the methods run on, with the numbers that matter.

- THE SHIP: ~0.05 MJ per tonne-km, walled by its own wave; the
  cheapest thing civilization does per tonne, carrying ~9 of 10
  traded tonnes; stability is the lever between weight and buoyancy.
- THE TRAIN: steel-on-steel at ~0.0015; thousands of tonnes per
  engine, kilometre stopping, ~1-2% gradients bought with tunnels.
- THE LORRY AND THE VAN: door to door, feeder of every efficient
  mode; the courier's ~130 stops a day are the network's costly edge.
- THE AIRCRAFT: ~5-10 MJ per tonne-km; range and payload share one
  weight budget (a long-haul departure is near half fuel); cruises
  high because thin air cuts drag faster than it starves the engines.
- THE ENGINE AND THE MOTOR: the four-stroke keeps ~a quarter of its
  fuel (suck, squeeze, bang, blow — only the bang pushes) and needs
  gears to stay in its band; the electric motor, happy nearly
  everywhere, skips the gears and hands braking energy back instead
  of burning it in the discs.
- THE CONTAINER AND THE CRANE: ~600 tonnes an hour against the gang's
  ~20; the box's worth grows with everyone who keeps it. The pallet
  is its little sibling: a full container is touched a handful of
  times, a single picked item ~20 — the unit of handling IS the cost.
- THE TYRE: four hand-sized patches carrying all steering, braking,
  and stability on one grip budget; the suspension's real job is
  keeping them pressed to a road that is not flat.
- THE TIMETABLE AND THE SIGNAL: capacity as information — the solved
  puzzle plus the padding that survives contact with the day.
- THE WAREHOUSE: the reservoir; its honest measure is turns per year,
  not size — the same money in fast stock does several times the work.

## PURPOSE
The wants this subject answers — the doors a query enters by.

- GET THE THING WHERE IT IS NEEDED: right thing, right place, right
  time, in a world where nothing arrives on the day promised.
- FEED THE CITY: every shelf is the far end of a chain, and the
  chain's arithmetic is the city's quiet constitution.
- BRING PEOPLE TOGETHER: commute, market, visit — bodies still travel
  though words no longer do.
- MAKE DISTANT THINGS AFFORDABLE: pennies a shirt across an ocean is
  why the world trades at all.
- KEEP THE PERISHABLE ALIVE: the cold chain's race against expiry,
  from harvest and vaccine to the last arm and plate.
- SURVIVE THE WORST HOUR: margins carried unused for years, priced by
  the one day they are spent.

## HISTORY
How the knowledge arose, in what order, at what cost.

- Paths first: rivers, fords, passes — geography dealt the hand before
  anyone played; cities pooled where cargo changed carriers, and many
  outlived the crossing that caused them.
- Prepared paths bought speed: canals then rails cut the toll per
  tonne ~tenfold — and rail's gauge, chosen almost by accident, froze
  into every tunnel and border after it (the mirror holds the wars).
- The engine era put the path's power in the vehicle: steam, the
  quarter-efficient four-stroke, then flight — each mode finding its
  medium's toll and paying it.
- The box, 1956: an outsider's bet that touching cargo was the cost —
  $5.86 a tonne became $0.16, port days became hours, and world trade
  reorganized around an agreement on a shape (the mirror holds the
  decade of fights it took).
- The queue was understood late: congestion got its arithmetic only in
  the last century — roads were widened first and measured after (the
  mirror holds those receipts).
- Distance died for words: the message left the mailbag for the wire
  and then for nothing — and goods kept riding ships, so the world
  became one market with a three-week lag woven in.
- The chain learned its fragility in public: a virus, a stuck ship, a
  hundred vessels at anchor — every law in this file ran at once, on
  the news (the mirror holds the receipts).
