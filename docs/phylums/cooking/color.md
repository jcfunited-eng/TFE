## THINGS
The units this subject is about. Each sits in many phylums at once;
its other homes are named so a cascade can branch.

- WATER — the medium of almost everything cooked; also lives in:
  physics (phase change), chemistry (the universal solvent), weather,
  the body, farming.
- HEAT — the agent of transformation; also: physics (energy in
  motion), engines, weather, the body (fever).
- FAT — flavor carrier, heat medium above water's ceiling, texture;
  also: the body (fuel stores), chemistry (nonpolar), medicine.
- PROTEIN — the structure that unfolds and sets: meat, fish, egg,
  milk, gluten; also: the body, biology, medicine.
- STARCH — the thickener and the staple: grain, potato, rice; also:
  farming, chemistry (long sugar chains), economy (the staples).
- SUGAR — sweetness, browning fuel, preservative at strength, yeast
  food; also: chemistry, the body, farming, economy.
- SALT — seasoning, water-mover, preservative; also: chemistry
  (ions), the body (nerves run on it), oceans, economy (once money).
- ACID — brightness, denaturer, preservative, safety line; also:
  chemistry (pH), medicine, fermentation's product.
- AIR — leavening when trapped, spoiler when free (oxidation);
  also: physics (gas law), weather, flight.
- MICROBES — yeast, bacteria, mold: hired labor or the enemy,
  decided by control; also: life, medicine, farming, disease.
- MEAT AND FISH — muscle: protein fibers, connective collagen,
  fat, water (~75% of lean muscle is water); also: animals, farming,
  economy.
- EGG — the kitchen's binder, leavener, emulsifier, and clock
  (sets by degrees); also: life, chemistry.
- MILK — emulsion of fat in water plus protein and sugar; the
  mother of butter, cream, cheese, yogurt; also: farming, the body.
- FLOUR — ground grain: starch plus the two proteins that become
  gluten when wetted and worked; also: farming, economy (bread
  riots), history.
- VEGETABLES AND FRUIT — cell walls of cellulose and pectin around
  water, sugar, acid; cooking is the controlled demolition of those
  walls; also: farming, life, medicine.
- FIRE AND ITS HEIRS — flame, coal, element, induction, magnetron:
  the heat sources; also: physics, engineering, history (the first
  chemistry humans commanded).
- ICE AND COLD — the slower of all processes; also: physics,
  weather, preservation, economy (the ice trade).

## CLAIMS
What holds, written to be tested by a crossing query. Worth is
never absolute — it is applicability to the query at hand.

- Heat always moves from the hotter to the cooler, three ways:
  touch (pan), flow (oven air, boiling water), glow (broiler,
  coals). Rate grows with the temperature difference.
- Cooking proteins is one-way: heat unravels their folded shapes
  and the shapes never refold. Conditions: begins ~50-65°C
  depending on the protein; see SCIENCE for the ladder.
- Browning that makes flavor (proteins + sugars) needs BOTH a dry
  surface AND roughly 140°C and above. A wet surface is pinned near
  100°C by boiling water and cannot brown until the water is gone.
- Water at a boil is a thermostat stuck at its ceiling — more flame
  makes faster bubbles, not hotter water. The ceiling is 100°C at
  sea level and falls ~1°C per 300 m of altitude; pressure raises
  it (see SCIENCE).
- Oil and water refuse each other; a lasting mixture needs a
  mediator molecule that holds both (egg yolk, mustard). Condition:
  the mediator must be present BEFORE the oil is forced in small.
- Salt moves water without touching it: water crosses toward the
  saltier side through any wall that passes water but not salt.
  This is why brines plump, cures dry, and salted cabbage weeps.
- Small pieces cook faster than the same food large — not a little
  faster: time through the middle follows thickness SQUARED.
- Cold does not kill; it slows. Freezing stops microbes by locking
  water as ice; thawed food resumes spoiling where it left off.
- Safety is time AND temperature together, tradable: milder heat
  held longer equals fiercer heat held briefly (see the
  pasteurization trade in SCIENCE).
- Acid below pH 4.6 blocks the deadliest spoiler (botulism); this
  single number divides safe home canning from roulette.
- Taste is balanced by opposition, not erasure: acid cuts fat,
  salt mutes bitter, sweet rounds sour. Correction is addition.
- Flavor is mostly smell: the tongue reports five channels; the
  thousands of distinctions ride on aroma through the back of the
  nose. Hot food smells louder because warmth drives aroma into
  the air.
- Resting meat after fierce heat is part of the cooking: the
  inside keeps rising several degrees (carryover) and the juices
  thicken and hold instead of flooding the board.
- An emulsion, a foam, and a dough are all the same wager: work an
  unwilling mixture into a structure and set it before it escapes.

## SCIENCE
The applying math, physics, and chemistry written IN, with working
quantities, in kitchen terms. This is what lets a query compute.

HEAT AND WATER, THE NUMBERS
- Water soaks heat like almost nothing else: 4.2 joules to move
  one gram one degree. Oil takes about half that (~2 J/g°C). So
  water heats slowly, holds much, and punishes impatience.
- Bringing a liter of water from 20°C to the boil costs ~336 kJ.
  Boiling it AWAY costs almost seven times more: turning one gram
  of water to steam eats 2,257 J without rising a single degree.
  Consequences a query can use: a hard rolling boil cooks no
  faster than a bare simmer (same 100°C) and spends the whole
  difference making steam; steam burns are worse than water burns
  because that stored energy comes back out on skin; reducing a
  sauce is buying flavor with fuel.
- The boiling ceiling moves with pressure: ~1°C lower per 300 m of
  altitude (water boils near 90°C at 3,000 m — eggs and rice break
  their sea-level rules there). A sealed pot at one extra
  atmosphere raises the ceiling to ~121°C — the pressure cooker.
- Reaction speed roughly DOUBLES per 10°C. So 121°C in a pressure
  cooker runs roughly four times faster than 100°C; and the same
  law backwards is refrigeration: 4°C runs spoilage at a crawl.

THE TEMPERATURE LADDER (what sets where — the kitchen's clock)
- ~50-55°C: fish and meat's first proteins (myosin) begin to set;
  rare.
- ~62-65°C: egg white sets; yolk thickens ~65, sets ~70. Meat at
  60-65 is juicy-firm.
- ~66-73°C: the second meat protein (actin) sets and SQUEEZES —
  this is where juices are wrung out and meat turns dry. The gap
  between 65 and 72 is the difference between succulent and
  sawdust, and it is only seven degrees wide: this is why the
  thermometer beats the clock.
- ~65-85°C, held LONG: collagen (connective tissue) melts to
  gelatin. Tough cuts become spoonable — but only with hours. Fast
  heat can never do it; this is braising's whole secret.
- 100°C: water's sea-level ceiling. Wet methods stop here.
- ~140°C+: browning chemistry (proteins + sugars) runs at cooking
  speed — the flavor of sear, crust, toast, roast.
- ~160-180°C: sugar alone caramelizes.
- ~150-230°C: fats smoke — butter ~150, olive ~190-210, refined
  oils ~230. A smoking fat is breaking down; frying lives between
  ~160 and 190.

TIME AND SIZE, THE MATH
- Heat crossing food follows the square law: double the thickness,
  roughly QUADRUPLE the time to the center. A 4 cm roast is not
  twice the 2 cm one — it is four times. This one relation prices
  most cooking-time questions.
- Surface against volume: halve the dice, double the total surface
  per amount. More surface = faster heat entry, faster browning,
  faster drying. The same math is why a grated thing flavors
  instantly and why flour (all surface) can dust-explode while a
  wheat berry is inert.
- Scaling a recipe is the times rule on AMOUNTS but never on time
  or temperature: quantities are linear, heat travel is not (the
  square law), and pan surface sets evaporation. Double a stew's
  ingredients and its simmer time barely moves; double a loaf and
  its bake stretches by thickness squared.
- Salt and smoke walk into food on the diffusion clock: penetration
  follows the SQUARE ROOT of time, on the order of a centimeter per
  day into meat. An overnight brine seasons the outer centimeter; a
  ham cures for weeks because the math allows no shortcut.
- Ratios rule baking because chemistry needs proportions, not
  amounts: bread runs on water at 60-75% of flour's weight and salt
  near 2% of it; seasoning food in general lands near 1% salt by
  weight of the food — the band the tongue calls "seasoned, not
  salty."
- Living leaven compounds: warm yeast doubles roughly every hour
  and a half — the same arithmetic as interest, which is why a
  pinch of starter is enough and why doubling times, not amounts,
  are what recipes state.

THE CHEMISTRY IN THE POT
- Browning (proteins + sugars, ~140°C+, dry) builds hundreds of
  new aroma molecules that were in neither parent — flavor is
  MADE, not released. Caramel (sugar alone, hotter) is a different
  reaction with its own register.
- Emulsion mechanics: oil beaten into water survives only as
  droplets too small to find each other, held apart by a mediator
  whose molecule has an oil-loving end and a water-loving end (egg
  yolk's lecithin, mustard's coat). Mayonnaise is ~75% oil standing
  in ~25% water because the droplets are crowded small — force
  determines fineness, the mediator determines survival.
- Acid's two jobs: it denatures protein without heat (ceviche
  "cooks" in lime — sets protein but kills no parasites; the
  safety and the set are different claims), and below pH 4.6 it
  forbids botulism outright — pickling is chemistry as law.
- Water activity is preservation's one law wearing four coats:
  salting, sugaring at strength, drying, and freezing all deny
  microbes FREE water. Jam, jerky, salt cod, and the freezer are
  the same idea.
- Cut plants brown by a different route: an enzyme meets air at
  the wound (apple, avocado). Acid or heat stops the enzyme —
  lemon on the cut face, blanching before the freeze.
- Starch thickens by bursting: granules swell in hot water and
  unravel (~60-70°C), tangling the liquid. Cooled, the chains
  re-stack and weep — which is stale bread and split sauce; gentle
  reheat partly undoes it (toast resurrects bread).
- Alcohol does NOT all cook off: ~35% remains after 30 minutes of
  simmering, ~25% after an hour, ~5% only after 2.5 hours.

THE SAFETY ARITHMETIC
- The danger zone is 5-60°C: in its warm heart, bacteria double
  every ~20 minutes — one becomes a thousand in five hours. Below
  5 they crawl; above 60 they die on the time-temperature trade.
- The trade is exact and tradable: 63°C held 30 minutes equals
  72°C held 15 seconds (milk's pasteurization); poultry's 74°C
  "instant" equals 63°C held several minutes. Milder-longer equals
  hotter-shorter — the ladder's mirror image.
- Washing raw poultry moves its bacteria to the sink and counters;
  heat, not water, is the instrument (mirror holds the full entry).

## METHODS
How things are done here — each with what governs it, when it
applies, and what breaks it. The prepositioned how-to-build.

- ROASTING AND BAKING (dry, flowing heat, 140-250°C): browning
  possible; the square law prices the time; the ladder sets
  doneness. Breaks when: surface burns before center cooks (too
  hot for the thickness — drop heat or shield) or center dries
  chasing a browned surface (sear-then-gentle, or gentle-then-sear).
- FRYING, PAN AND DEEP (fat as the medium, 160-190°C): fat reaches
  browning temperatures water cannot; crust forms while inside
  steams. Breaks when: fat too cool (food drinks it — soggy), too
  hot (smoke, bitterness), pan crowded (temperature crashes, see
  water's heat cost; fry in small loads).
- BOILING, SIMMERING, POACHING (water, 70-100°C): even, capped,
  browning impossible. Poach the delicate (the ladder's low rungs),
  simmer the sturdy, boil only what cannot be wrung (pasta, greens).
  Breaks when: a boil batters what a simmer would set whole.
- STEAMING (water's vapor, 100°C): wet heat without drowning —
  nothing leaches into water that food never touches. Same ceiling,
  same no-browning law.
- BRAISING AND STEWING (sear dry, then low wet heat, hours): the
  two-method wager — browning's flavor first, then 65-85°C held
  long for collagen's slow melt. The only road tough cuts can take
  to tender. Breaks when hurried: high heat sets actin, wrings the
  meat dry, and never pays collagen's time bill.
- GRILLING AND BROILING (glow heat, fierce and one-sided): browning
  fast, thickness law brutal — thick pieces burn outside before
  the center moves; the answer is zones (fierce then gentle) or
  distance.
- PRESSURE COOKING (sealed water, ~121°C): wet heat above the
  ceiling, roughly 4x speed; collagen melts in an hour instead of
  four. Still below 140 — pressure never browns; sear first.
- SOUS-STYLE HOLDING (water at exactly the target, long): the
  thermometer made into the method — meat held AT 62°C can never
  pass it; time does collagen's work at juiciness's temperature.
  The trade: no crust (below 140 always) — finish with a sear.
- CURING, SALTING, DRYING, SMOKING (water activity denied slowly):
  preservation methods that became flavors. Governed by the
  square-root clock — days and weeks, no shortcut. Smoke adds its
  own chemistry to the surface the salt has dried.
- FERMENTING (microbes hired and fenced): salt at 2-5% fences OUT
  the spoilers while the salt-tolerant workers eat sugar and pay in
  acid (kraut, kimchi, pickles) or alcohol and gas (bread, beer).
  The acid then locks the safety claim (pH falls below 4.6). Breaks
  when the fence fails: too little salt, warmth's wrong workers,
  air where it must be excluded.
- CANNING (heat then seal): kill what is inside, forbid what is
  outside. High-acid goods need only boiling water; low-acid goods
  need pressure's 121°C because botulism spores laugh at 100 —
  the pH 4.6 line decides which law applies, no exceptions.
- EMULSIFYING, WHIPPING, KNEADING (structure by work): force plus
  mediator makes droplets (mayonnaise), force plus protein makes
  foam (whites, cream), water plus work makes gluten's net (dough).
  All three are races to SET the structure before it escapes.
- LEAVENING (trapped gas expands): yeast's slow exhalation, soda's
  instant chemical burp (needs an acid partner), or air beaten in —
  caught in gluten's or egg's net, expanded by heat, then SET by
  the ladder before it can collapse. A fallen cake opened the oven
  before the set.
- RESTING, CHILLING, RIPENING (time as a method): carryover
  finishes the roast off the heat; chilling firms what heat
  built; dough's slow cold rise trades hours for flavor (the
  microbes' side work makes the aroma). Doing nothing, on purpose,
  is a method with numbers.
- KNIFE WORK (pressure = force ÷ area): a sharp edge concentrates a
  modest push into cutting pressure; a dull one crushes. Small
  even pieces are the square law and the surface law made
  deliberate: same size = same finish time.
- TASTING AND CORRECTING (the feedback method): season, taste,
  oppose — acid against fat, salt against bitter, sweet against
  sour — in small steps, because addition is easy and subtraction
  is impossible (dilution is the only undo, and it costs
  everything else).

## MEANS
What the methods run on, with the numbers that matter.

- THE HEAT SOURCES: open flame and coals (glow + flow, fierce,
  uneven, flavor of the fire); gas (instant control); electric
  coil (slow to answer); induction (the pan itself is the heater —
  fastest answer, nothing wasted); the magnetron (microwaves shake
  water molecules directly, ~1-2 cm deep — inside-the-surface
  heating, wet by nature, hence its mirror entry on browning).
- THE VESSELS: copper answers instantly (conducts ~400 W/mK),
  aluminum nearly so (~235), cast iron slowly but holds a steady
  mass of heat (the sear's friend), steel barely conducts (~15)
  and needs a clad core, clay breathes and steadies. Choose by the
  method's need: answer speed for sauces, held mass for searing.
- THE HONEST INSTRUMENTS: the thermometer (the ladder made
  visible — the single largest upgrade lore ever received) and the
  scale (mass is truth; cups are approximations wearing
  confidence — flour by the cup swings 30% by packing).
- THE EDGE: a sharp knife, kept sharp — force ÷ area, and safety:
  the dull knife slips because it must be pushed.
- COLD BOXES: the refrigerator (4°C — spoilage at a crawl) and
  freezer (-18°C — water locked, the clock nearly stopped; quality
  decays by drying and ice-crystal spears even as safety holds).
- THE PANTRY OF PRESERVERS: salt, sugar, acid, smoke, dryness —
  the water-activity law as shelf goods.

## PURPOSE
The wants this subject answers — the doors a query enters by.

- MAKE IT SAFE: kill and forbid the invisible (the safety
  arithmetic; the oldest purpose after the next one).
- MAKE IT FOOD AT ALL: cooking is pre-digestion — cooked starch
  and protein yield far more energy to the body than raw; the
  species runs on this outsourced first stomach.
- KEEP IT: against the season and the journey (water activity,
  acid, cold) — the purpose that shaped cuisines before the
  refrigerator demoted it.
- MAKE IT GOOD: flavor built (browning, ferment, season, balance)
  — the purpose that promoted itself from luxury to the center as
  the others were tamed.
- FEED THE MANY FROM LITTLE: stretch, use everything, waste
  nothing (stew, stock, sausage, bread pudding — thrift's whole
  cookbook; economy lives here).
- BRING PEOPLE TO ONE TABLE: the meal as bond and ceremony —
  culture's kitchen door; recipes as inheritance.

## HISTORY
How the knowledge arose, in what order, at what cost — the order
carries why.

- Fire first, before history: cooking predates every written
  thing; the pre-digestion purpose made it worth the burns. Cost
  unknown and paid in full before anyone could write it down.
- Preservation second, from necessity: salt, smoke, dry, ferment —
  each found by survivors of the tries that failed (the mirror
  holds that record). Whole cuisines are the map of what kept:
  the salt fish of coasts, the hard cheeses of mountains, the
  sauerkraut of winters.
- Fermentation tamed without being understood: bread rose and beer
  brewed for thousands of years on lore alone — method preceded
  science by millennia, the standing proof that HOW can outrun WHY
  and still feed everyone.
- The acid and canning lesson, paid in deaths: sealed food killed
  until the pH line and the pressure canner were found (the mirror
  holds the botulism record). The safety arithmetic is written in
  that ink.
- The thermometer's late arrival: cooking ran on lore and thumbs
  until measurement entered the kitchen; the temperature ladder
  turned "until done" into degrees, and doneness stopped being an
  argument.
- Refrigeration reordered everything: the keeping purpose collapsed
  from the center of cuisine to a hum in the corner, freeing salt,
  smoke, and ferment to become flavors instead of necessities —
  methods that work becoming inferior for their first purpose and
  staying for their second.
- The microbe revelation: germ theory arrived and rewrote safety
  from superstition to arithmetic; fermentation's lore became
  employee management.
