## THINGS
The units this subject is about; other homes named so a cascade
can branch.

- DEEP TIME — the budget itself, ~4.6 billion years: enough to
  make slow causes sufficient for everything seen; also:
  astronomy (starlight demands the same budget), life (evolution
  spends the same account), economy (compounding at the limit).
- ROCK — not permanent, only slow; every pebble mid-journey
  between fire, layers, and pressure; also: building, craft,
  chemistry.
- MINERAL — an arrangement more than a substance: the same atoms
  stacked one repeating way to the last edge; diamond and pencil
  lead are both pure carbon, differing only in the stack; also:
  chemistry (shape does the work), craft, measurement.
- THE PLATES — the shell broken into rafts drifting at
  fingernail speed; their meetings are the mountains, trenches,
  and quakes; also: physics (convection), conflict (pressure at
  stuck borders — picture, not law).
- THE FURNACE — birth-heat plus radioactive decay keeping the
  deep rock soft enough to creep and melt; the engine under
  every surface story; also: physics, engineering, cooking (the
  soup pot's convection at supper scale).
- THE LAYERS — mud and sand settled flat, each atop the last: a
  diary read bottom-up; also: measurement, story (archives hold
  what got filed, not what happened).
- FOSSIL — an accidental burial: covered fast, left in peace,
  slowly turned to stone; a lottery few enter; also: life,
  measurement (survivorship bias loose in every field).
- SOIL — rock ground fine and mixed with the dead; the mineral
  half made at centuries per inch, the best of it delivered from
  elsewhere; also: farming (the living bank), plants.
- GROUNDWATER — most fresh liquid water, filling pores like a
  sponge, creeping a meter a day or a year; a well lifts rain
  older than anyone alive; also: farming, survival, law.
- THE RIVER AND ITS SAND — a conveyor whose real cargo is dirt:
  shortening mountains at one end, building deltas at the other,
  every grain in transit carrying its journey's story; also:
  transport (the flattest survey, free), farming, economy.
- ICE — glacier, sheet, and frozen ground: a carrier that sorts
  nothing, a shaper that rounds valleys and lowers seas; also:
  weather, astronomy (orbital pacing), survival.
- THE MAGNETIC FIELD — no buried magnet but a running dynamo of
  stirred molten iron: it drifts, weakens, and has flipped
  hundreds of times; also: physics, electronics, astronomy
  (Mars lost its field, then its air).
- MOUNTAIN AND VOLCANO — light crust afloat with an iceberg's
  root; the furnace venting where melting is possible, temper
  decided by stickiness; also: physics, weather (the
  rain-wringer), chemistry, story.
- THE OCEAN FLOOR — the youngest rock on the planet, born at
  ridges, buried at trenches, nowhere older than ~200 million
  years; the tape recorder of the field's flips; also: physics,
  measurement.
- ORE, OIL, AND COAL — buried concentration: metal sorted by hot
  water and time, plankton cooked in a narrow window, bogs
  pressed down a rank ladder; also: economy (wealth in a spot vs
  smeared), chemistry, conflict (what is cheap to seize).
- THE COAST — a flow, not a place: sand forever arriving from
  upcoast and leaving downcoast; the thin crowded ribbon where
  the advantages pile up; also: economy, weather, transport.
- PLACE — the double address: SITE is the ground itself,
  SITUATION is what it sits between — and situation changes
  without the ground moving an inch; also: economy, transport,
  conflict, governance.

## CLAIMS
What holds, written to be tested by a crossing query.

- Deepest is oldest: things settle downward, so a cliff is a
  book read bottom-up. Condition: the stack may be overturned —
  and beds carry their own up-arrows (ripple crests point up,
  mud cracks open upward, graded beds are coarse at the base).
- Anything cutting across a thing is younger than what it cuts;
  a lump inside a rock was a rock before its host. Pure logic,
  settling most arguments about order without a laboratory.
- Layers are laid flat and continuous; every tilt, bend, and
  break came later. And "the same layer" is a line of time, not
  a kind of stone: one age is sand at the shore, mud offshore,
  lime beyond — a creeping shoreline files those places into one
  stack, so a cliff can be a coastline stood on its edge.
- The loudest thing in a cliff is often a plain line where
  nothing was laid down or a stack was stripped away; at the
  famous gaps more time is missing at one surface than all the
  rock above and below represents.
- A rock's name is its history, not its ingredients: the same
  minerals become granite by cooling slowly, sandstone by
  settling, gneiss by being squeezed hot without melting. The
  route decides, not the recipe — and the wheel never stops:
  melt hardens, weather grinds, burial bakes, depth re-melts.
- The planet files its violence along seams: plot every quake
  and volcano on a blank globe and the plate edges draw
  themselves; a plate's middle is quiet by construction. A
  fault's offset names its push — dropped means pulled apart,
  ridden-up means squeezed, ground-past means sheared — and the
  three kinds sit at the three kinds of edge.
- The mantle is hot enough to melt and mostly does not: pressure
  holds it solid. Melting happens where pressure comes off
  (plates parting) or water is added (wrung from a sinking
  slab); heat is rarely what changes. That is why volcanoes
  stand at ridges and above trenches and almost nowhere else —
  except where a fixed plume writes an island chain.
- Stickiness decides a volcano's temper: runny lava vents its
  gas and builds wide pouring shields; stiff silica-rich lava
  traps gas until pressure wins and explodes. Lava is the least
  of the danger — ash on roofs, hot clouds outrunning cars, and
  mud floods off a melted snowcap do the killing; danger is
  reach times speed, and a fluid you can outwalk has neither.
- One quake has one magnitude (the break) and a thousand
  intensities (the shaking street by street); damage follows the
  shaking, not the headline. Soft ground shakes harder and
  longer than rock, and loose wet sand can quit being solid —
  buildings tip whole, buried tanks float.
- Mountains float: a high range carries a deep root, and as
  erosion shaves the top the block rises to keep its balance —
  why old ranges die so slowly, and why land still rises where
  the last ice sheets pressed it.
- Water is the sculptor and patience its strength: freeze and
  wedge, dissolve, grind, repeat, with deep time as the ally; a
  canyon is a river's paycheck. Most desert scenery is flood
  work — rare rain on bare ground — while wind only sorts, since
  air can lift dust and roll sand but cannot budge gravel.
- Moving water sorts by weight, so grain size is a fossil of
  speed. Ice sorts nothing — boulder and clay speck ride at one
  speed and drop jumbled — while its meltwater sorts what it is
  handed; the pair is a signature no other process forges.
- The water table is a gentler copy of the hills; where it meets
  the ground: a spring, a marsh, a stream that never dries.
  Holding water and yielding it are different properties — clay,
  mostly holes, gives nothing; gravel holds less and pours. A
  pumped well digs a widening invisible funnel; a spill arrives
  underground years later and stays decades — the slowness that
  hides the problem forbids the cure.
- In limestone country the drainage runs inside the rock:
  streams vanish down holes and reappear at springs in hours,
  unfiltered — clear water is not evidence of clean water. The
  same water that ate the cave rebuilds inside it, a fraction of
  a millimeter a year: one reaction run both ways.
- Soil sorts itself into a stack (dark and living, pale and
  washed, the pile-up zone, broken rock), and the breadbaskets
  sit on imported ground — river silt, glacier dust, volcanic
  ash. Soil made in place from the rock below is thin and slow.
- A slope stands while friction and roots beat the downhill
  pull; water tips the ratio not by lubricating but by pressing
  the grains apart from inside, stealing their friction. Most
  ground movement is too slow to watch and never stops; fast
  failures mostly happen where the ground was already creeping.
- Ground sinks for a short list of reasons, each fingerprinted:
  pumping drops a wide area evenly; a mined seam makes a sharp
  trough; drained peat burns away in air; dissolved limestone
  fails as a hole. Swelling clays and frost heave work the walls
  from below, unevenly — which is what cracks them.
- An ore body is somewhere nature already did the concentrating
  — hot water dropping leached metal in one crack, a melt
  settling its heavy grains, rain stripping a hillside for a
  million years. Oil forms in buried plankton mud, cooks in a
  narrow band, floats upward, and is caught only where four
  things line up — miss one and there is nothing. Coal is an
  ancient bog, not a forest, and its rank is a depth record.
- Latitude is a ration of sunlight and altitude spends the same
  coin: climbing is walking poleward. Water forgives and land
  does not — coasts keep a narrow late year while continental
  hearts bake and freeze. A range wrings the wind dry, putting
  rainforest and desert within sight of each other.
- The ground prices everything that crosses it: rivers have
  already surveyed the flattest line, so roads, rails, and
  cables stack in the same valley; ranges are crossed at a few
  passes; and everyone's cheapest-route arithmetic returns the
  same answer, so a very large world ends with very few throats.
- The floodplain is the river's overflow room — also the best
  soil and cheapest ground, so people build there knowing; the
  flood is an installment on a bargain. A delta is mud held up
  by fresh mud arriving: dam the river and deliveries stop; pump
  beneath and the pile squeezes down.
- The ground's worst is worse than anybody's memory: the biggest
  flood, wave, and slide run on gaps of centuries and human
  records are short. The rock keeps the longer book.
- The air is a waste product: green things spent hundreds of
  millions of years exhaling oxygen into an atmosphere that had
  none, rusting the world's iron before any could stay aloft.
  Life rearranges its planet — and carbon still runs two loops,
  fast through air and plants in years, slow through rock in
  hundreds of thousands.
- The planet sorted itself by weight while molten: iron sank to
  the core, light rock floated to the continents, which ride
  high because their rock is lighter than the ocean floors'. The
  heavy floor sits low; water fills it — why there are basins.

## SCIENCE
The math, physics, and chemistry of the ground, held — not
mentioned: LAW to compute with, RULE to follow, WORKED to prove
the rule runs.

### MATHEMATICS — the size of deep time

LAW: proportion. Age ≈ 4.6×10⁹ years; a day is 86,400 s, so one
  scaled second ≈ 4.6×10⁹ ÷ 86,400 ≈ 53,000 years.
RULE: divide an age in years by 53,000 for seconds to midnight.
WORKED: written history (~5,000 yr): 5,000 ÷ 53,000 ≈ 0.09 s —
  the last tenth of a second. The dinosaurs' end (~66 million
  yr): 66,000,000 ÷ 53,000 ≈ 1,245 s ≈ 21 minutes ago. Deep time
  is the budget that makes slow causes sufficient.

### PHYSICS AND CHEMISTRY — the clocks in the rock

LAW: a radioactive parent halves on a fixed beat: remaining =
  (1/2)^(t ÷ half-life). Half-lives: carbon-14 5,730 yr ·
  potassium-40 1.25 billion · uranium-238 4.47 billion. The
  clock starts at SEALING — when a crystal cools enough to stop
  leaking — not at the mountain's rise or the animal's death.
RULE: read the halvings off the parent-daughter ratio; age =
  half-life × halvings.
WORKED: charcoal holding 1/8 of its carbon-14: 1/8 = 1/2³, three
  halvings, 3 × 5,730 = 17,190 years.
WORKED: a zircon with equal uranium-238 and its lead: half gone,
  one halving = 4.47 billion years. The oldest zircons read
  ~4.4 billion; meteorites, never recycled, read ~4.56 — the
  planet's age is read off the leftovers, because the rock wheel
  erased its own first pages.
RULE (choosing the clock): carbon-14 dies of its own speed —
  after 10 half-lives (57,300 yr) only 1/1,024 remains, below
  clean measurement, so it dates nothing much past ~50,000
  years; deep time needs slow clocks. A sandstone cannot be
  dated at all — its grains sealed in the mountain they came
  from — so bracket it between datable ash beds.
LAW (cross-check): independent clocks with different chemistries
  must agree on one sample, and do; agreement between unrelated
  methods is the whole strength.

### MATHEMATICS — plate arithmetic

LAW: speed = distance ÷ time; plates run ~1-10 cm/yr, fingernail
  speed — and deep time turns it into oceans.
RULE: age an ocean by width ÷ spreading rate; speed a plate by
  island spacing ÷ age difference; age a collision by crumpled
  distance ÷ closing rate.
WORKED (ocean): the Atlantic, ~5,000 km = 5×10⁸ cm, at ~2.5
  cm/yr: 5×10⁸ ÷ 2.5 = 2×10⁸ = 200 million years — and no ocean
  floor anywhere is older than about that. Arithmetic and rocks
  agree.
WORKED (chain): a fixed plume under a moving plate writes its
  history — the live island at one end, each behind it older,
  cooler, lower. An island ~500 km out aged ~5 million years:
  5×10⁷ cm ÷ 5×10⁶ yr = 10 cm/yr, matching the ~9 satellites
  measure. The bend in the chain is the day the plate turned.
WORKED (mountains): closing at ~5 cm/yr for ~50 million years
  consumes 5 × 5×10⁷ = 2.5×10⁸ cm = 2,500 km of crust — the
  crumple that is the Himalaya, still rising ~5 mm a year with
  erosion clawing most of it back.
WORKED (fault budget): a fault loading at 3.5 cm/yr whose big
  quakes each slip 5 m stores 500 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 140 years between big
  ones. This forecasts the century, never the day — the day is a
  certified cannot; the mirror holds why.

### PHYSICS — floating mountains

LAW: a floating body displaces its own weight. Crust ~2.7 g/cm³
  in mantle ~3.3: for height h, the root r solves 2.7(h + r) =
  3.3r, so r = 4.5h.
WORKED: a 5 km peak needs ~22.5 km of root — on normal ~35 km
  crust, the ~60-70 km measured under the great ranges. Erosion
  nearly self-repairs: shave 1.0 m and the block rebounds
  2.7/3.3 ≈ 0.82 m, so lowering a range 1,000 m costs stripping
  1,000 ÷ 0.18 ≈ 5,500 m of rock — why worn ranges still stand
  hundreds of millions of years after their collisions ended.

### PHYSICS — the shaking

LAW: each magnitude step is ~32× energy: ratio = 10^(1.5 × ΔM).
WORKED: magnitude 7 against 5: 10^(1.5×2) = 10³ = 1,000× — two
  steps on the scale, three zeroes in the ground.
LAW: a tsunami is the whole column moving, at √(g × depth).
WORKED: over 4,000 m: √(9.8 × 4,000) = √39,200 ≈ 198 m/s ≈ 710
  km/h, jetliner speed, barely a meter high. Over 40 m: √392 ≈
  20 m/s ≈ 71 km/h — the front slows tenfold, the back keeps
  coming, and the height has nowhere to go but up.
LAW: quakes are the lamp for the deeps — two kinds of shaking,
  and the kind that cannot cross liquid casts a far-side shadow
  that proved the outer core molten. The interior model earns
  its keep by predicting every future arrival at every station
  to the second.
LAW: a wave entering slower material grows — soft ground shakes
  harder than rock; a drained lakebed can wreck a city ~400 km
  from a fault that barely moved the hills beside it.

### PHYSICS — the furnace and its gradient

LAW: the ground warms ~25-30°C per km down, paid by birth-heat
  plus decay — the same decay that runs the clocks runs the
  engine. Temperature ≈ surface + gradient × km.
WORKED: 3 km at ~28°C/km: 15 + 84 ≈ 99°C — why deep mines need
  refrigeration.
WORKED (oil window): plankton mud cracks to oil at ~60-120°C: at
  ~28°C/km that is (60−15) ÷ 28 ≈ 1.6 km down to (120−15) ÷ 28 ≈
  3.8 km. Cooler stays waxy; hotter cracks oil to gas; hotter
  still leaves only carbon — one mud, four fates, chosen by
  depth. Coal walks the same ladder: peat, brown, house-coal,
  anthracite, each step a depth record.

### PHYSICS — water the sculptor

LAW: the heaviest grain a current moves rises roughly as speed
  to the sixth power.
WORKED: double the speed, move stones 2⁶ = 64× heavier; a flood
  at triple speed shifts 3⁶ = 729× — the rare event does the
  work the common one gets credit for; canyons and desert fans
  are flood work.
LAW: water expands ~9% on freezing; confined, it pushes at
  roughly ten times what rock holds in tension — the wedge in
  every crack, run every freezing night.
LAW: flow through a passage throttles as width to the fourth
  power.
WORKED: halve the pore, pass 1/16 the water; a clay pore ~100×
  finer than sand's passes ~100⁴ = 100,000,000× less — why clay
  is a lid (the artesian seal) and gravel a pipe, and why
  capacity and yield are separate ratings on one box.
WORKED (cutting rate): a canyon ~1,800 m deep cut in ~5-6
  million years: 1,800,000 mm ÷ 5.5×10⁶ yr ≈ 0.3 mm a year —
  three sheets of paper. Patience, not strength.

### CHEMISTRY — stone dissolved, sorted, re-laid

REACTION (karst, both ways): rain + air's carbon dioxide → weak
  acid; acid + limestone → dissolved rock carried away. In the
  cave the water loses its gas and lays the stone back, drop by
  drop — the kettle's limescale is this deposit at home.
LAW (evaporite order): a drying salt sea drops its minerals in
  fixed order, least soluble first — a thick gypsum or salt bed
  IS a basin that dried, its layers reporting how far.
LAW (crystal size): ordering takes time — slow depth-cooling
  grows visible grains (granite), air-chilled lava is too fine
  to resolve (basalt), no time at all is glass (obsidian).
LAW (freezing order): a melt crystallizes across a range,
  dropping heavy dark minerals first, leaving the remainder
  paler — one starting rock yields both families.
LAW (rebuilding unmelted): squeezed hot rock rebuilds its
  minerals to suit the new conditions, flat crystals growing
  across the squeeze — why slate splits on planes that ignore
  its mud beds; and each new mineral is stable in a narrow
  window of heat and depth, so its presence brackets what the
  rock went through, in the same fixed order on every mountain.
LAW (two attacks): frost, heat, and roots break rock; water and
  weak acid rot it; climate picks the winner — the limestone
  that stands a thousand dry years dissolves off a wet city
  church in a hundred.
LAW (the sorted planet): mass ≈ 5.97×10²⁴ kg over volume ≈
  1.08×10²¹ m³ gives average density 5.5 g/cm³ against surface
  rock's 2.7 — the deep must run about double the shell, and
  iron fits. The floated crust is ~46% oxygen and ~28% silicon
  by weight, so nearly every rock is a silicate: those two atoms
  in a four-cornered unit linked into chains, sheets, and
  frameworks — thousands of minerals named, about a dozen
  building nearly everything under any boot.

### THE SOIL AND PLACE ARITHMETIC

LAW: soil's mineral half forms at ~an inch per several centuries
  (~0.05 mm/yr); a bared ploughed slope can lose ~1 mm/yr.
WORKED: 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20 — each such year spends twenty years of
  making. The account balances only under cover or delivery;
  stripped dry land degrades in place, and an ordinary drought
  then finds nothing holding the ground. The desert does not
  arrive; the resistance leaves.
LAW: air cools ~6.5°C per km climbed — climbing is walking
  poleward.
WORKED: 2,000 m takes off ~13°C: a 30°C lowland keeps a 17°C
  highland — why great tropical cities sit at altitudes absurd
  anywhere else.
LAW: water stores ~5× rock's heat per gram per degree (4.18 vs
  ~0.8) and stirs it downward while ground plays only its skin —
  the sea swings small and late, the continent's heart hard and
  on time.
LAW: independent requirements multiply into rarity.
WORKED: four needs each holding on ~a third of land hold
  together on ~(1/3)⁴ = 1/81 — one acre in eighty (illustrative
  numbers, real law): why farmland gathers into a few belts and
  a great coastline holds almost no harbors.
LAW: dealings fall off steeply with distance — every crossing
  costs per journey, so the far option loses a thousand small
  decisions; terrain that raises the price of contact preserves
  every difference it encloses.
RULE (the gravity guess): expect the traffic between two places
  to run as their sizes multiplied, divided by the distance
  between them squared — double the distance, quarter the
  dealings. Rough, but it ranks the pairs right.

## METHODS
How the reading is done — followable, with what governs and what
breaks each.

- READING A CLIFF: start at the bottom, check the up-arrows in
  case the book was turned, place every crack and pebble by
  cross-cutting and inclusion, and treat gap-lines as loud —
  missing time is still time. Breaks when beds are read as kinds
  of stone instead of lines of time.
- CORRELATING: match layers by fossils that were everywhere at
  once and did not last long — common, widespread, quick to
  change. A species that survived a hundred million years dates
  nothing; a marker's worth is how narrowly it pins the answer.
- DATING: pick the clock by the age asked; date only sealed
  containers; bracket the unsealable between ash beds; always
  run a second independent clock.
- READING FORCES: the fault's offset names the push; folded
  versus snapped names rate and depth (slow, hot, buried flows —
  fast, cold, shallow breaks); joint sets name the unloading.
  The break records the load that broke it.
- READING TRANSPORT: a sand grain tells distance (roundness),
  sortings survived (evenness), and journey length (everything
  but quartz rots en route). Cross-beds lean downstream — a
  quarry face is a wind rose from three hundred million years
  back. Jumbled till beside sorted outwash says ice; U-valleys
  and bedrock scratches point back the way it came.
- SOUNDING THE DEEP: use every quake as a lamp — arrival times
  at every station drawn into a model that must keep predicting
  future arrivals to the second or be redrawn. Nobody has
  sampled below a scratch; prediction is the only real test, and
  it passes.
- FORECASTING BY ZONE AND BUDGET: map the seams, run the slip
  budget for the recurrence, harden buildings to the zone's
  shaking, and buy seconds at the event — the fast wave outruns
  the damaging one (~6 vs ~3.5 km/s), so 100 km buys 100/3.5 −
  100/6 ≈ 12 seconds. This is the whole colored answer; naming
  the day is the mirror's certified cannot.
- PROSPECTING: follow the concentrator, not the metal — where a
  flow slowed, a fluid cooled, a melt settled. The gold pan
  repeats the river in a dish: gold at 19.3 g/cm³ against sand's
  2.65 drops first where the swirl slackens. For oil, check all
  four links — source, cooking window, reservoir, cap — because
  missing one gives nothing.
- FINDING AND KEEPING WATER: read the table as a softened copy
  of the hills; expect yield from coarse ground and refusal from
  clay; respect the funnel every pump digs (near wells rob each
  other, shallow first); treat karst as plumbing, not
  filtration; treat any spill as decades.
- INVESTIGATING GROUND: the only material nobody chose, made, or
  can fully inspect — a survey samples ~a millionth of what the
  building sits on, so establish the KIND, then hunt its worst.
  Read the standing creep gauges: curved trunks, tilted
  gravestones, cracks that reopen after repair.
- SITING: read site and situation separately — can you build,
  and will anyone want to be there. Price the floodplain bargain
  honestly, and size the worst from the rock's long book, never
  the town's short memory.
- KEEPING SOIL: covered, delivered, spent no faster than the
  making — the twenty-to-one arithmetic is the whole method.

## MEANS
What the reading runs on, with the numbers that matter.

- BOOTS, HAMMER, HAND LENS: the whole ordered chart of periods
  was built by walking, matching, and stacking — a century
  before anyone could put a year on any of it.
- THE SEISMOMETER NETWORK: the planet-sized lamp-reader; every
  quake lights the interior for every station.
- THE MASS SPECTROMETER: the clock-reader, counting parent and
  daughter atoms — without it the halving law has no instrument.
- THE DRILL: truth at a point, and only a point — the deepest
  hole reaches ~12 km of the 6,371 to the center: 0.2%, a
  scratch. Everything deeper is read by lamp.
- SATELLITES: plate speed watched directly, centimeters per
  year, confirming the chain arithmetic; ground deformation
  mapped between passes.
- THE MAGNETOMETER: reads the sea floor's frozen compasses — the
  mirror-striped tape recorder that ended a fifty-year argument.
- MAPS AT A STATED SCALE: a reading is a question at a chosen
  grain, and the grain is part of the answer — a coastline
  measured with a shorter ruler gets longer without limit.
- METEORITES: the offsite copies — cores and leftovers of other
  worlds, keeping the original clock the working copy erased.

## PURPOSE
The wants this subject answers — the doors a query enters by.

- KNOW WHAT THE GROUND WILL DO: under a foundation, a slope, a
  city — the reading that keeps buildings up.
- FIND WATER: table, aquifer, spring — and keep it clean and
  unspent.
- FIND METAL AND FUEL: read the concentrators; the economy's raw
  ledger opens here.
- KEEP THE SOIL: feed everyone forever instead of once.
- FORESEE THE VIOLENCE: zones, budgets, hardened buildings —
  bought by the decade, never by the day.
- READ THE PAST: the ground is the only witness to almost
  everything that ever happened; the diary was kept whether or
  not anyone came to read it.
- PLACE THINGS WELL: site and situation, passes and harbors —
  why places are the way they are, and where the next thing
  should stand.

## HISTORY
How the knowledge arose, in what order, at what cost.

- The order came before the numbers: the chart of periods was
  walked out and stacked a century before any of it had a year;
  dates were hung on afterward, and any can be revised without
  disturbing the order. Sequence and size are separate claims on
  separate methods.
- The clocks arrived with radioactivity, and one discovery fixed
  two things at once: decay heats the planet (the missing term
  in every cooling estimate) and dates the rock (the term every
  age lacked). The mirror holds the confident wrong number that
  preceded it.
- Drift was right and rejected fifty years for want of an engine
  — and the rejection was proper method: a matching coastline is
  a suggestion, not a test. The sea floor settled it: mirrored
  magnetic stripes, floor ages rising outward, nothing older
  than ~200 million years — a mechanism plus a prediction that
  could have failed.
- Quakes became lamps: the shadow of the wave that cannot cross
  liquid proved the outer core molten — the interior read by
  echoes and tested the only honest way, by prediction.
- The ice ages were read off the land — rounded valleys, distant
  boulders, lowered seas — paced by an orbital whisper amplified
  by ice's whiteness and the ocean's carbon grip. The last
  retreat, ~12,000 years ago, opened the calm farming began in.
- The air turned out to be biography: banded iron beds are the
  receipts of the long rusting before oxygen could stay aloft.
  Life is a geological force.
- The extinctions were counted in thinning layers; one of five
  carries an asteroid's planet-wide signature — the sky is in
  the ground's diary too.
- The planet's own birthday came from offsite: meteorites keep
  the original clock this world's rock wheel erased.

## RELATIONS
First-class connections — each names what runs along it.

- PHYSICS: buoyancy and the floating crust, wave speed and
  refraction, convection, pressure and melting, the freeze
  wedge, the sixth- and fourth-power throttles.
- CHEMISTRY: crystal stacking, dissolution both ways,
  crystallization order, decay chains, the silicate frame, the
  oil and coal ladders.
- MATHEMATICS: proportion at planetary scale, halvings, budgets,
  sixth powers, stacked-condition multiplication, grain inside
  every map answer.
- ASTRONOMY: the shared age budget, orbital pacing of the ice,
  meteorites as offsite clocks, impacts in the diary, tilt
  making seasons, tides trading the earth's spin for the moon's
  slow retreat.
- WEATHER: the sun's furnace above answering the rock's furnace
  below — two engines, one planet; desert belts, rain shadow,
  wind and current patterns repeating on every continent.
- LIFE, PLANTS, and ANIMALS: the fossil lottery and its leaning
  record, the oxygen biography, reefs and bogs as rock-makers,
  roots holding slopes and wedging cracks, cover deciding
  whether soil stays, ranges that must walk when climates walk.
- FARMING: soil as delivered bank, the flood's yearly silt,
  fossil water spent as if income, the dry line that good years
  lure settlement past.
- BUILDING and ENGINEERING: foundation before frame, soft ground
  and liquefaction, swelling clay and frost heave, subsidence
  fingerprints, quake codes, channels dredged and canals cut —
  geography bought by hand.
- ECONOMY: ore in a spot against harvests smeared over
  provinces, transport cost as a wall around markets, stocks
  drawn down that look like flows until gone.
- TRANSPORT: rivers as free surveys, passes funneling
  everything, sea lanes worn into water, chokepoints made by
  everyone's same arithmetic.
- CONFLICT: terrain as force multiplied, pass and strait as
  targets, flat open ground as invitation.
- GOVERNANCE and LAW: borders on ridges against rivers that
  move, one basin under several flags, title to ground against
  title to what flows beneath it.
- MEASUREMENT: magnitude against intensity (source and effect
  are different quantities), rank cheaper and firmer than value,
  prediction as the only real test of a model.
- SURVIVAL: the water map, the flood room, ground that quits
  when wet, the worst that is worse than memory.
- CRAFT: splitting stone the way it wants to go, the quarry
  wedge in the joint, grain in steel as grain in schist.
- COOKING: fudge and obsidian are one law (cooling sets grain),
  the reducing pot is the evolving melt, the sugar stage is the
  index mineral — the ground's chemistry at supper scale.
- MEDICINE and BODY: interiors read by echoes; bone thickening
  along its loads as rock rebuilds along its squeeze.
- STORY and BELIEF: flood memories, mountain gods, the deep past
  as every culture's first cosmology — and the diary that now
  answers those stories with receipts.
