# 151 DEEP LOGISTICS AND SUPPLY — getting things where they are needed
The trade of having the right thing in the right place at the right
time, done in a world where nobody knows what will be wanted and
nothing arrives on the day it was promised. Almost every law here is
a trade between two costs that are paid by different people at
different times — which is why the arithmetic is simple and the
practice is hard. Every entry forbids something.

ESSENCE: stock is not a thing you have, it is a bet you have paid
  for. Every item sitting on a shelf was bought to cover something
  nobody knows — how much will sell, how late the lorry will be, how
  many will be faulty. Remove all the uncertainty and you would need
  almost no stock at all.
ROOT: chance+risk / holding a reserve is buying protection against a
  spread of outcomes, and the size of the reserve follows the spread
  and not the average.
THREAD: money (cash held against an uncertain month), the body (fat
  as a store against a lean season), computing (a buffer sized for
  the jitter and not for the rate).
ASKED-AS: stock levels too much inventory warehouse full cash tied up shortage buffer why holding

ESSENCE: there are two costs and only one of them appears in the
  accounts. Holding stock costs money that can be counted to the
  penny — space, capital, insurance, damage, obsolescence. Running
  out costs a lost sale, a stopped line, a customer who does not
  come back, and none of that lands anywhere that can be added up.
  So the visible cost wins the argument every time.
ROOT: money / a decision goes to whichever side of the ledger is
  measured, so an unmeasured cost is treated as zero.
THREAD: medicine (the harm of a treatment being visible and the harm
  of not treating being invisible), governance (a spending cut that
  is counted against a service loss that is not), farming (the cost
  of a spray against the cost of the year it saved).
ASKED-AS: stockout cost lost sale inventory carrying holding warehouse cutting stock target measuring justify

ESSENCE: how much stock you need is set by the wait. During the
  time between deciding to order and the goods arriving, demand
  keeps happening and you have nothing coming — so the shelf must
  hold everything that will be wanted in that window, plus a margin.
  Halve the lead time and you halve the stock, without changing
  anything else at all.
ROOT: mathematics / a stock is a reservoir, and a reservoir is sized
  by the outflow over the refill time.
THREAD: farming (a store sized by the length of the hungry gap),
  water (a reservoir sized by the dry season), computing (a queue
  sized by how long a restart takes).
ASKED-AS: lead time supplier weeks stock cover reorder point delivery long shipping reducing inventory

ESSENCE: how much you need to hold is driven by how much demand
  wobbles, not by how much there is. Two products selling the same
  hundred a week need utterly different stock if one sells twenty a
  day every day and the other sells nothing for six days and a
  hundred on the seventh. The average is the part that everyone
  looks at and the part that hardly matters.
ROOT: chance+risk / a reserve exists to cover the distance between
  the outcome and the expectation, so it is set by the spread.
THREAD: weather (a reservoir built for the drought, not the annual
  rainfall), money (income variance mattering more than income),
  medicine (a patient's swings mattering more than their mean).
ASKED-AS: demand lumpy variable average weekly sales spiky forecast stock swings steady erratic

ESSENCE: a long lead time that is always the same can be planned
  around. A short one that is sometimes three days and sometimes
  three weeks cannot — because you must hold stock for the worst
  case, so the whole benefit of the short average is thrown away.
  Reliability is worth more than speed, and it is the thing buyers
  most often fail to buy.
ROOT: chance+risk / a plan is sized by the tail of the distribution,
  so the tail is what has to be paid for.
THREAD: transport (a train timetable's value being in its keeping),
  medicine (a drug's dosing depending on predictable absorption),
  people (a colleague who is reliable beating one who is fast).
ASKED-AS: supplier late sometimes unreliable delivery dates slipping variable lead time planning promised erratic

ESSENCE: the extra stock kept for safety is not a percentage or a
  feeling — it comes out of three things: how much demand wobbles,
  how long the wait is, and how sure you want to be. It grows with
  the wobble, it grows with the square root of the wait, and it
  grows very fast indeed as certainty approaches complete.
ROOT: mathematics / independent variations add up as a square root
  rather than by simple addition, so a longer window is less risky
  per day than a short one.
THREAD: chance+risk (errors in a long run partly cancelling), money
  (portfolio risk falling with the square root of the number of
  holdings), building (a factor of safety chosen and stated).
ASKED-AS: safety stock formula how much extra buffer service level calculating demand variation lead time

ESSENCE: the last few per cent of reliability cost as much as all
  the rest. Going from ninety to ninety-five per cent of orders
  filled might add a fifth to the stock; going from ninety-five to
  ninety-nine can double it, and the last fraction of a per cent has
  no ceiling at all. The curve is gentle for most of its length and
  then turns almost vertical.
ROOT: mathematics / covering a wider slice of a bell-shaped spread
  means reaching into a tail where each further step needs far more
  reserve.
THREAD: engineering (the last decimal of reliability costing more
  than the machine), medicine (screening's last few cases), computing
  (each extra nine of uptime costing what all the previous ones did).
ASKED-AS: service level ninety nine per cent fill rate stock cost target availability expensive last

ESSENCE: for a thing bought once for one occasion — the Christmas
  stock, the newspaper run, the conference lunches — the right
  quantity is not the expected demand. It is set by the ratio
  between what one too many costs you and what one too few costs
  you. Where the loss on a leftover is small and the loss on a
  shortage is large, the correct order is well above the forecast.
ROOT: chance+risk / the best decision under uncertainty balances the
  costs of the two kinds of error, and there is no reason for those
  costs to be equal.
THREAD: medicine (a test's threshold set by which mistake is worse),
  law (a standard of proof chosen the same way), farming (sowing
  extra seed because a gap costs more than a thinning).
ASKED-AS: how many to order christmas seasonal leftover unsold shortage one off event stock quantity

ESSENCE: a forecast is accurate for tomorrow, poor for next month
  and worthless for next year, and the decay is not gentle. So the
  useful question is never how to forecast better but how to need
  less forecast: shorten the lead time, and the horizon you must
  guess across shrinks with it.
ROOT: chance+risk / uncertainty accumulates with time, so error
  grows with the distance you are predicting across.
THREAD: weather (a five-day outlook against a five-week one), money
  (a business plan's third year), computing (a cache that works
  because it only has to predict the next second).
ASKED-AS: forecast accuracy wrong long term planning horizon months ahead demand predicting better model

ESSENCE: forecasts get better the more you lump together and worse
  the more you split apart. Total sales next month can be predicted
  closely; sales of one colour in one shop on one day cannot be
  predicted at all. So planning is done at the level where the
  numbers behave, and the detail is handled by holding stock or by
  deciding late — never by forecasting harder.
ROOT: mathematics / independent wobbles partly cancel when added,
  so a sum is proportionally steadier than any of its parts.
THREAD: astronomy (a population predictable where an individual is
  not), insurance (a pool that works only because it is a pool),
  medicine (a trial result true of a group and not of a patient).
ASKED-AS: forecast by store item level total sales aggregate accurate detailed planning sku daily

ESSENCE: one shared pile of stock serves the same reliability with
  far less inventory than the same goods split across many places —
  because a quiet week in one place covers a busy week in another,
  and they cannot do that from separate rooms. Splitting the pool
  in four does not need four times as much, but it needs about twice.
ROOT: mathematics / independent variations combine as a square root,
  so the reserve needed grows with the square root of the number of
  separate pools.
THREAD: insurance (many small risks in one pool), power grids (one
  connected network needing less spare capacity than many islands),
  people together (a shared rota needing fewer people than separate
  ones).
ASKED-AS: central warehouse regional depots stock split branches pooling inventory total more locations

ESSENCE: the cheapest way to serve many variants is to keep the
  stock in the form they have in common, and do the last step that
  makes them different only when the order is in hand. Paint mixed
  at the counter, computers configured to order, garments dyed after
  cutting — all one idea: hold the general, commit late.
ROOT: chance+risk / the total across variants is far steadier than
  any single variant, so stock held before the split enjoys the
  steadiness.
THREAD: cooking (a base sauce finished several ways), computing (a
  general system configured at run time), teaching (a common
  foundation before specialisation).
ASKED-AS: paint mixing counter configure order variants colours sizes stock late customising generic base

ESSENCE: a handful of lines are most of the money and most of the
  lines are almost none of it — and the two groups need opposite
  treatment. The few big movers deserve tight control, frequent
  small deliveries and high availability; the long tail deserves
  simple rules, rare orders and a frank acceptance that some of it
  will not be there. Treating everything alike wastes effort on the
  tail and starves the head.
ROOT: money / value in almost any catalogue is distributed very
  unevenly, so uniform effort is misallocated by construction.
THREAD: money (a few holdings carrying a portfolio), computing
  (optimising the hot path and leaving the rest), teaching (effort
  aimed where it changes the most).
ASKED-AS: fast movers slow moving lines dead stock abc analysis policy few items most sales

ESSENCE: your sales figures are not your demand figures. Nobody buys
  what was not on the shelf, so every stockout quietly deletes the
  demand it caused, and the record says sales were low that week. A
  system fed on its own sales therefore learns to stock less of the
  things it kept running out of.
ROOT: evidence / a measurement taken through a filter measures the
  filter, and here the filter is the availability you are trying to
  set.
THREAD: medicine (a disease appearing rare because it is not
  tested for), computing (a log that only records successes),
  governance (a service judged by the people it managed to serve).
ASKED-AS: sales history demand stockout lost sales data forecast low reorder shelf empty record

ESSENCE: everything built on a stock system rests on whether the
  system's number matches the shelf, and it usually does not.
  Miscounts, theft, breakage, wrong scans and returns put stock in
  the wrong place in the record; the machine then decides not to
  order because it believes there are eight, and the shortage is
  invisible until a customer stands in front of an empty space.
ROOT: evidence / a control system acts on its reading, so an
  inaccurate reading produces confident wrong action.
THREAD: accounting (a reconciliation that must be done regularly or
  not at all), computing (a cache that has drifted from the truth),
  navigation (a dead-reckoned position needing a fix).
ASKED-AS: stock count wrong system says shelf empty inventory accuracy cycle counting shrinkage records mismatch

ESSENCE: stock goes off even when it does not go off. A part
  superseded by a new model, a garment out of season, a package with
  last year's design, a component nobody has designed in for two
  years — all still perfect, all worth less every week, and the loss
  is only recognised on the day someone finally admits it. That
  admission is postponed for years because it lands in one period's
  accounts.
ROOT: money / value comes from what someone will pay now, so an
  unsold good is losing value continuously whatever its condition.
THREAD: money (a loan book carrying bad debt at face value),
  farming (a store that keeps but loses quality), computing (data
  kept forever because deleting requires a decision).
ASKED-AS: old stock obsolete write off dead inventory clearance discontinued warehouse aging value books

ESSENCE: the honest measure of a stock operation is not how much is
  there but how many times a year it turns over. Money spent on
  goods that sell in a week is doing several times the work of the
  same money in goods that sell in a quarter — so two businesses of
  identical size can be running on utterly different amounts of
  capital, and the difference is speed and nothing else.
ROOT: money / a return is earned per cycle, so the number of cycles
  in a year is a multiplier on everything.
THREAD: money (a business's return depending on how often capital
  cycles), farming (two harvests a year against one), restaurants
  (table turnover deciding the takings more than the prices).
ASKED-AS: stock turns turnover ratio inventory days cash tied working capital measure performance speed

ESSENCE: order in large amounts and you pay less per order but hold
  more stock; order in small amounts and you hold less but pay the
  ordering cost again and again. The best size balances the two, and
  it moves with the square root of demand — but the real lesson is
  that the curve near the bottom is almost flat, so being roughly
  right costs almost nothing and being precise buys almost nothing.
ROOT: mathematics / where one cost falls and another rises with the
  same variable, the total has a minimum, and near a minimum the
  slope is small.
THREAD: engineering (an optimum where two curves cross), the mind
  (deliberating past the point where the decision improves), money
  (rebalancing a portfolio too often).
ASKED-AS: order quantity how much order size economic minimum ordering cost holding batch optimum calculating

ESSENCE: batches exist because changing over costs something. A
  machine that takes four hours to set up will be run in long runs;
  a lorry that costs the same whether it is full or empty will wait
  until it is full. So a batch size is never really a choice — it is
  a symptom, and the way to shrink it is to attack the changeover,
  not to instruct people to run smaller batches.
ROOT: money / where a fixed cost is paid per event, the sensible
  event is large, so making the event cheap is what makes small
  events sensible.
THREAD: cooking (a kitchen that can cook one portion because it is
  set up to), computing (a deployment done daily because it was made
  cheap), teaching (short frequent assessments only where marking is
  fast).
ASKED-AS: batch size changeover setup time machine runs long production small lots flexible switching

ESSENCE: a small ripple in shop demand becomes a swell at the
  wholesaler and a wave at the factory. Each level in a chain sees
  only its own customer's orders, adds a little safety, reacts to
  what looks like a trend, and orders in batches — and each of those
  reasonable habits amplifies the signal it passes upstream. The
  factory then concludes the market is wildly volatile, and it is
  looking at its own echo.
ROOT: engineering / a chain of controllers each reacting to the one
  in front, with delay, is an oscillator whether or not anyone
  wanted one.
THREAD: engineering (a feedback loop with lag going unstable),
  money (markets amplifying a small piece of news), people together
  (a rumour growing at each retelling).
ASKED-AS: orders swinging factory feast famine wholesaler retailer demand amplified chain overreacting stock cycles

ESSENCE: when supply is short and it is shared out according to how
  much each customer ordered, every customer orders more than they
  need — and the manufacturer, reading its swollen order book,
  concludes the shortage is worse than it is. The shortage has
  become partly self-made, and it ends in a cliff of cancellations
  the moment supply recovers.
ROOT: strategy / a rule that rewards a behaviour will be met with
  that behaviour, and here the rule rewards exaggeration.
THREAD: money (a bank run made of the fear of one), governance (a
  subsidy allocated by claimed need), farming (a water right claimed
  in full so it is not lost).
ASKED-AS: allocation shortage ordering more than need chips supply double ordering cancelled panic buying customers

ESSENCE: a price promotion does not create a customer, it moves a
  purchase in time — so the spike is mostly people buying early and
  buying deeper, and the trough afterwards is the bill. The factory
  meanwhile builds capacity and stock for a demand that was
  manufactured by its own sales department.
ROOT: money / a lower price brings forward and concentrates buying
  that would have happened anyway, so a demand curve in time is
  being rearranged rather than raised.
THREAD: money (a tax deadline moving activity rather than creating
  it), farming (a subsidy year distorting the following one),
  computing (a benchmark run that does not resemble real use).
ASKED-AS: promotion sale spike demand forecast distorted discount pull forward trough factory planning peaks

ESSENCE: every level of a chain that holds its own safety stock is
  covering the same uncertainty again. Four layers each protecting
  themselves against the layer above hold four times the reserve and
  give worse service than one properly placed pool, because each
  layer's protection also lengthens the next layer's lead time.
ROOT: mathematics / protection duplicated at each stage adds up
  while the risk it covers does not, since it is the same risk.
THREAD: money (fees taken at every layer of a fund structure),
  governance (each department holding its own contingency),
  computing (retries stacked at four levels producing a storm).
ASKED-AS: distributor wholesaler retailer stock layers duplicated safety chain visibility echelons total inventory

ESSENCE: the amount of work sitting in a system, the rate it comes
  out, and how long each piece takes to get through are locked
  together by one relation: work in the system equals rate times
  time. There are only ever three dials, and turning any two sets
  the third — so a promise to cut lead times without cutting the
  queue or raising the rate is a promise that cannot be kept.
ROOT: mathematics / a steady flow through a container must satisfy
  this identity whatever the system is doing inside, which is why it
  applies to factories, hospitals and phone lines alike.
THREAD: medicine (a waiting list falling only by treating more or
  listing fewer), roads (traffic density, flow and journey time),
  computing (requests in flight, throughput and latency).
ASKED-AS: lead time reduce queue waiting work in progress throughput backlog how long shorten flow

ESSENCE: work waiting in a process is not neutral. It is cash spent
  and not yet earned, it is space, it is damage, it is rework
  waiting to be discovered — and worst, it is delay between making a
  mistake and finding it, so a system full of work in progress
  learns slowly and repeats its faults hundreds of times before
  anyone notices.
ROOT: engineering / a long queue between cause and effect breaks the
  feedback that would have corrected the cause.
THREAD: computing (a large batch of untested changes), teaching
  (marking returned weeks after the work), farming (a disease found
  at harvest rather than in the field).
ASKED-AS: work in progress piles up between stations queue rework found late feedback slow batches

ESSENCE: as a resource gets busier, waiting does not grow gently —
  it grows toward a wall. Up to about seventy per cent busy, a
  system responds quickly; past ninety, small increases in load
  double and quadruple the queue; at a hundred, the wait is
  unbounded. So an operation run at full utilisation has traded away
  every ounce of its responsiveness for the last few points of
  efficiency.
ROOT: mathematics / when arrivals and service times both vary, the
  gaps that idle time would have absorbed are gone, and the queue
  has nowhere to recover.
THREAD: medicine (a hospital at full beds losing the ability to
  admit), roads (a motorway at capacity collapsing into stop-start),
  the mind (a diary with no gaps that cannot absorb one surprise).
ASKED-AS: busy waiting times explode capacity utilisation ninety percent full booked queues slack idle

ESSENCE: queues are made by variability, not by shortage of
  capacity. A machine that can do sixty an hour and receives sixty
  an hour will still build an endless queue if either the arrivals
  or the work times wobble — because a fast hour cannot bank time to
  pay for a slow one. Cut the variation and the queue falls without
  adding a single machine.
ROOT: mathematics / idle time cannot be stored, so a system pays for
  every slow spell and is never repaid for a fast one.
THREAD: transport (bus bunching from tiny timing differences),
  computing (jitter causing backlog at a server that is fast
  enough), the body (an irregular heartbeat at an adequate rate).
ASKED-AS: queue forms enough capacity variation arrivals bunching waiting line random uneven smoothing flow

ESSENCE: a system produces what its slowest stage produces, and no
  more. Everything upstream of that stage can only fill it or flood
  it; everything downstream can only wait for it. So the whole
  output of a factory, a kitchen or a hospital is a single number
  belonging to one place — and almost all improvement effort is
  spent somewhere else.
ROOT: engineering / a series arrangement is limited by its narrowest
  member, so capacity is a property of one point rather than of the
  system.
THREAD: the body (fitness limited by whichever system gives out
  first), computing (a pipeline limited by one stage), farming (a
  crop limited by the scarcest nutrient).
ASKED-AS: bottleneck slowest station output capacity improving speed machine backlog constraint throughput factory line

ESSENCE: an hour lost at the constraint is an hour lost by the whole
  operation and it can never be recovered. An hour saved anywhere
  else is worth nothing at all. That one asymmetry decides where the
  maintenance goes, where the good operator stands, where the
  overtime is spent, and why the constraint is fed, guarded and
  never allowed to stand idle waiting for parts or a decision.
ROOT: engineering / where one point sets the total, its time is the
  only time that carries the system's value.
THREAD: sport (a team's tactic organised around one player), the
  mind (protecting the hours in which the real work happens),
  medicine (the theatre being the scarce resource a hospital is
  arranged around).
ASKED-AS: bottleneck idle waiting parts protecting buffer priority machine downtime overtime efficiency measuring elsewhere

ESSENCE: the whole of constraint thinking is one sentence: find the
  thing that limits you, get everything else out of its way, and
  only then spend money making it bigger. What follows from it is
  the uncomfortable part — that most local efficiency is waste, and
  that busy is not the same as productive.
ROOT: engineering / improvement is only real where it moves the
  limiting quantity, so the first task is always identification.
THREAD: medicine (treating the condition that limits the patient),
  computing (profiling before optimising), farming (the scarcest
  nutrient setting the yield whatever else is added).
ASKED-AS: theory constraints improving system where start efficiency programme waste focus limiting factor identify

ESSENCE: fix a bottleneck and you do not remove it, you move it —
  the next-slowest stage becomes the limit, often somewhere nobody
  was watching and often at a much higher rate. So the question
  after every improvement is not whether it worked but where the
  limit went, and a team that does not ask is soon optimising a
  place that stopped mattering.
ROOT: engineering / removing the narrowest member of a series leaves
  a new narrowest member immediately.
THREAD: trades (a repair relocating the weak point next door),
  roads (a widened junction pushing the jam one block on), medicine
  (one joint replaced and the next one starting to hurt).
ASKED-AS: fixed bottleneck now elsewhere moved constraint shifted new slowest stage capacity next limit

ESSENCE: moving work one piece at a time through a series of steps
  finishes each piece far sooner than moving it in big batches, with
  no step going any faster. Ten items done one at a time through
  three stations produce the first finished item after three
  minutes; done as a batch, after thirty. Nothing was sped up — only
  the waiting was removed.
ROOT: mathematics / in a batch, every item waits for every other
  item at every step, so the waiting grows with the batch size at
  each station.
THREAD: cooking (plating a table's meals together against as ready),
  computing (streaming against buffering), teaching (feedback on
  each exercise against at the end of term).
ASKED-AS: batch flow one piece lead time faster waiting stations production line small lots

ESSENCE: running with almost no stock only works when everything
  else is dependable — the machines, the supplier, the quality, the
  transport, the information. Take the stock out of an undependable
  system and it simply stops, several times a week. So low inventory
  is the last thing you get, not the first thing you do.
ROOT: engineering / a buffer exists to absorb variation, so removing
  it exposes every variation it was hiding.
THREAD: money (living without savings requiring a steady income),
  the body (training hard requiring sleep and food first), computing
  (removing retries only after the network is sound).
ASKED-AS: just in time lean stock removed line stopped supplier reliable kanban failed implementing inventory

ESSENCE: inventory is a blanket over the faults. A machine that
  breaks weekly, a supplier who is late, a step that makes scrap —
  none of it is felt while there is a pile in front of the next
  stage. Lower the pile deliberately and the problems surface one at
  a time in order of seriousness, which is uncomfortable and is
  exactly the point.
ROOT: evidence / a system that never fails visibly cannot be
  improved, because it is not saying where it hurts.
THREAD: money (a large cash balance hiding a poor business), the
  body (painkillers letting an injury continue), governance (a
  reserve fund that postpones a needed reform).
ASKED-AS: reducing inventory problems appear breakdowns hidden buffer stock exposing issues lean water rocks

ESSENCE: expediting one order does not create capacity, it takes it
  from every other order — so a system where a few things are rushed
  is a system where everything else is slightly later, and a system
  where everything is rushed has no priorities at all. The hot list
  is a confession that the ordinary flow no longer works.
ROOT: mathematics / a queue reordered is still the same queue, so
  every place gained by one job is a place lost by another.
THREAD: medicine (triage only working while most patients are not
  urgent), computing (a task scheduler thrashing between jobs),
  people together (an office where everything is urgent).
ASKED-AS: expediting rush orders urgent priority hot list chasing late everything jumping queue capacity

ESSENCE: capacity does not arrive in small increments. You cannot
  buy a third of a machine, a fifth of a warehouse or half a lorry —
  so growing operations spend their lives either uncomfortably tight
  or embarrassingly empty, and the moment of stepping up is always
  wrong in one direction. The lump size decides how bumpy the ride
  is.
ROOT: money / where the smallest purchasable unit is large, the
  match between capacity and demand can only be approximate.
THREAD: building (a bridge built for the traffic of thirty years
  hence), power (a station's whole output arriving at once), teaching
  (a class that needs a second teacher at one extra pupil).
ASKED-AS: capacity step change new machine warehouse too big small growing expansion timing lumpy investment

ESSENCE: it is often cheaper to change the demand than to chase it.
  Off-peak pricing, delivery-day choices, lead times quoted longer
  when the plant is full, a discount for taking last year's model —
  all of them move demand into the capacity that exists, and all of
  them cost a fraction of the capacity they save.
ROOT: money / price and terms are levers on when people buy, so the
  demand curve in time is something an operation can act on rather
  than only receive.
THREAD: power (tariffs shifting load off the evening peak),
  transport (cheaper travel outside the rush), medicine (appointment
  systems spreading a day's arrivals).
ASKED-AS: peak demand smoothing pricing off peak discount delivery slots capacity spreading load shifting customers

ESSENCE: the fastest goods are the ones that never go into store.
  Deliveries arriving from many suppliers are broken down and
  reloaded onto outbound vehicles within hours — sorted rather than
  stored. It cuts handling, space and time, and it demands that
  everything arrive in the right quantity in a narrow window, which
  is the whole reason it is rare.
ROOT: engineering / storage exists to decouple two flows, so where
  the flows can be synchronised the storage has no work to do.
THREAD: post (a sorting office that never stores), computing
  (streaming data through rather than writing it down), transport
  (a connecting flight with no overnight).
ASKED-AS: cross docking warehouse storage sorting transferring lorries direct distribution centre picking stock through

ESSENCE: routing everything through a hub buys frequency and
  connection and pays for it in distance, handling and risk. Every
  parcel travels further and is touched twice more; every route gets
  a daily service it could never justify alone; and one bad night at
  the hub delays everything in the system at once. Direct running
  wins the moment a single lane can fill a vehicle by itself.
ROOT: money / consolidation lowers the cost per item and raises the
  cost per item's journey, so the crossing point is set by how much
  volume a lane has.
THREAD: flight (an airline's connecting bank against point-to-point
  carriers), computing (a central exchange against direct peering),
  people together (an organisation routing all decisions through one
  office).
ASKED-AS: hub depot direct delivery routing parcels sorting centre detour frequency network failure central

ESSENCE: waiting to fill a vehicle saves money and costs time, and
  the two cannot both be had. A part-load dispatched today arrives
  tomorrow and costs double; held for three days it goes at a
  quarter of the rate and arrives on Friday. So consolidation is a
  clock decision dressed as a cost decision, and the goods sitting
  on the dock are paying for the saving.
ROOT: money / a fixed cost per vehicle makes filling it the cheapest
  path, and filling takes time that belongs to the customer.
THREAD: baking (an oven filled before it is lit), computing
  (batching messages to save round trips), post (a collection time
  that decides the whole delivery promise).
ASKED-AS: part load consolidation waiting full lorry pallet shipping cost slower groupage cut off dispatch

ESSENCE: each way of moving things has a different shape of cost.
  Sea and rail are enormous to start and almost nothing per extra
  mile, so they want to be full and long. Road is moderate at both
  ends and wins on flexibility. Air costs little to begin and a
  great deal per mile, so it is chosen by value and urgency, never
  by distance. The mode is picked by the shape, not by the speed.
ROOT: money / a cost made of a fixed part and a per-mile part
  produces a different winner at every distance and every volume.
THREAD: money (fixed and variable costs deciding a business's
  scale), computing (a slow high-bandwidth channel against a fast
  narrow one), travel (why a long trip and a short one choose
  differently).
ASKED-AS: shipping air sea road rail cost comparing freight distance urgent cheap mode choosing

ESSENCE: journeys are slow at the joins. A container crosses an
  ocean in three weeks and can spend another three waiting for a
  berth, a customs clearance, a train slot and a delivery
  appointment — and the same is true of a pallet moving between two
  buildings a mile apart. Nearly all of a door-to-door time is spent
  stationary, and nearly all damage happens while being handled.

ESSENCE: the cost of moving something is dominated by how many times
  a person touches it, and that cost per item rises enormously as
  the unit gets smaller. A full container is handled a handful of
  times; a pallet a few dozen; a single item picked, packed and
  carried to a door may be handled twenty times on its own account.
  Choosing the unit of handling is choosing the cost.
ROOT: money / labour is charged by the touch, so cost per item is a
  function of how many items share each touch.
THREAD: cooking (portioning as the expensive part of catering),

ESSENCE: freight is charged on the larger of what a thing weighs and

ROOT: money / a vehicle runs out of space and of weight allowance at

ESSENCE: refrigerated transport is a separate, much smaller network

ROOT: engineering / where the product deteriorates during the

THREAD: medicine (vaccines lost to one failed refrigerator),

ESSENCE: where goods expire, they must be shipped in order of when
  they die and not in order of when they arrived — and stock with
  three days of life left is a different product from the same stock
  with thirty, worth less to every customer. So shelf life is an
  inventory of its own, spent as surely as money and gone whether or
  not the goods move.
ROOT: farming / harvest is a one-way gate on a moving clock, so a
  perishable good is losing value from the moment it exists.
THREAD: medicine (a drug stock rotated by expiry), money (an option

ESSENCE: paper stops goods as effectively as a broken axle. A wrong

ROOT: law / a rule enforced at a chokepoint has the force of a

ESSENCE: at some named point on the journey, ownership and risk pass

ROOT: law / a contract binds at a specific clause, and here the

ESSENCE: buying everything from one supplier is cheaper — better
  prices, less administration, a closer relationship, one quality

ESSENCE: two suppliers are not two suppliers if they share a

ESSENCE: a second source that never receives an order is not a

ROOT: engineering / an untested standby is an assumption, and

ESSENCE: a shortage and an allocation are different situations that
  look identical from the shop floor. In a shortage there is not
  enough in the world; in an allocation there is enough but somebody
  else has been given it. The first is answered by finding new
  supply or new design; the second is answered by negotiation,
  contracts and being the customer worth serving — and using the
  wrong answer wastes the whole period.
ROOT: money / scarcity and priority are different mechanisms, and
  only one of them can be argued with.
THREAD: medicine (a drug shortage against a rationing decision),
  governance (famine as failure of claim rather than of harvest),

ESSENCE: goods coming back are a different business from goods going

ROOT: engineering / a flow of identical items and a flow of unique

THREAD: cleaning (sorting mixed waste being far harder than sorting

  writing structured output).

ESSENCE: spare parts turn the ordinary economics upside down.
  Demand is rare, irregular and impossible to forecast; the item may
  sit for years; and the cost of not having it is not a lost sale
  but a machine, an aircraft or a hospital standing still. So the
  right stock is often several years of demand of something that
  might never move.
ROOT: money / the correct reserve follows the cost of running out,
  and here that cost is enormous while the holding cost is small.
THREAD: medicine (an antidote stocked for the case that may never
  come), power (a spare transformer with a two-year lead time),
  fire services (equipment justified by consequence rather than use).

ESSENCE: nobody in the chain is paid for the flow. The salesperson

  their number while making the whole system slower and dearer. Most
  supply chain dysfunction is honest people meeting their targets.
ROOT: money / people optimise what they are measured on, so a set of

ESSENCE: efficiency means having nothing spare; resilience means
