# 158 DEEP CIVILISATIONS — patterns across societies that rose and fell
Not a list of empires and not a set of dates. These are the shapes
that keep turning up: what has to be true before a state can exist
at all, how far a ruler's arm can actually reach, which failure
arrives on schedule in every century, and what really happens to
ordinary people when the palace stops writing things down.

ESSENCE: states do not appear wherever people are numerous. They
  appear where a surplus can be seen, counted, moved and taken — and
  that needs a crop which ripens together, stores well, and cannot
  be hidden. Where food grows scattered, underground, or a little at
  a time all year, nobody has ever built one.
ROOT: power / rule runs on extraction, and extraction needs the
  thing taken to be visible on a date that somebody else can name.
CANNOT: no state on a diet of roots and forest food — a tuber keeps
  in the ground and is dug when wanted, so there is no harvest
  moment to tax. And no early state without a granary: the tax must
  be storable, or the collector arrives with nothing to carry away.
THREAD: farming (a crop that ripens all at once), history (the
  granary and the tax collector arriving together), government (a
  state that cannot see a thing cannot rule it).
ASKED-AS: why states formed grain taxes harvest first kingdoms farming surplus rulers began crops

ESSENCE: nobody can be supported full-time until somebody else grows
  more than they eat. Priest, soldier, scribe, smith and king are
  all paid out of the same margin, so the thickness of a society's
  ruling layer is set by how much spare food the fields make, and by
  nothing else at all.
ROOT: money / a person doing one thing all day has to be fed by
  others, so the number of such people is a division sum.
CANNOT: no standing hierarchy on bare subsistence — where everyone
  must farm, nobody can be an official, so authority stays part-time
  and has to be re-earned. And no reading a large elite as evidence
  of a rich people: it is evidence of a large take, which is a
  different fact entirely.
THREAD: history (the city as division of labour made physical),
  farming (yield per worker as the ceiling on everything above it),
  power (the size of a state as an arithmetic outcome).
ASKED-AS: surplus food supported priests soldiers rulers farming feed specialists hierarchy why possible

ESSENCE: a writing system with thousands of signs takes a decade to
  learn, so it produces a small trained class whose position depends
  on that difficulty. An alphabet of two dozen letters can be
  learned in a season, and once it escapes the palace it cannot be
  called back in.
ROOT: teaching / the cost of learning a skill decides how many
  people can hold it, and a monopoly maintained by difficulty is
  still a monopoly.
CANNOT: no broad literacy on a script that takes years — the numbers
  simply do not close, whatever anyone intends. And no keeping a
  cheap script restricted: any merchant, soldier or heretic can pick
  it up, and the same letters write the tax roll and the complaint
  about the tax roll.
THREAD: history (a copying cost collapsing and moving the real
  constraint), power (control of records as control of the
  argument), language (a written standard disciplining the spoken).
ASKED-AS: alphabet cuneiform hieroglyphs scribes learning writing hard few people literacy spread letters

ESSENCE: a pyramid, a cathedral or a parade ground is an argument
  you cannot look away from. It says: this authority commanded
  thousands of people for years and they did it — and the proof goes
  on standing there whether or not anybody still believes the reason
  it was given.
ROOT: government / belief does the daily work of ruling, and belief
  needs something everyone can see at once and agree that they are
  both seeing.
CANNOT: no monument without an organised surplus and a labour
  system, so no reading one as piety alone — it is a public account
  of what could be commanded. And no cheap substitute for it: a
  claim made in words can be denied, and a hundred metres of stone
  cannot.
THREAD: government (legitimacy as the thing that saves you spending
  force), building (a load path as a public proof), archaeology
  (survival strongly favouring exactly this kind of object).
ASKED-AS: pyramids cathedrals huge buildings why built power show labour thousands workers monuments impress

ESSENCE: a canal system is a machine that has to be run. Somebody
  must decide who takes water on which day, force the shared
  clearing of silt, and settle the fights — so societies that farm
  by irrigation grow a thick layer of officials and written rules
  whether or not anyone ever wanted them.
ROOT: people together / a shared resource that fails if anybody
  defects requires an enforcer, and an enforcer becomes a permanent
  institution.
CANNOT: no large irrigation without coordination — one farmer
  breaching a bank upstream ruins a hundred downstream, so the works
  and the administration cannot be had separately. And no irrigation
  without an ending written into it: canals silt and watered dry
  soils go salty, so more effort is needed every year to stand
  still.
THREAD: government (a rule is a wish until somebody is paid to
  enforce it), farming (salt as the long bill for watering dry
  ground), water (a right to a flow, handed out by a person).
ASKED-AS: irrigation canals officials rules water sharing ancient states bureaucracy silt salt farming

ESSENCE: herders and farmers need each other and rob each other, and
  both are permanent. The herder has animals, mobility and no
  granary; the farmer has grain, cloth and a fixed address. Trade
  and raid are the same relationship at two different prices, and
  which one is running depends on the harvest.
ROOT: money / where two economies each hold what the other lacks,
  exchange follows — and where one side cannot be caught and the
  other cannot run, taking is sometimes cheaper than buying.
CANNOT: no herding society self-sufficient in grain and metal, so no
  clean separation of the two worlds — the frontier is a market
  before it is ever a battle line. And no cheap defence of an open
  frontier: a mobile raider picks the time and place, and a wall has
  to be everywhere at once.
THREAD: war (a raid as an economic act), trade (a frontier working
  as a market), farming (two ways of using the same grass).
ASKED-AS: nomads raids farmers steppe herders trade frontier attacks settled towns walls tribes

ESSENCE: a horse archer was a soldier who fed himself, moved three
  times as fast as an army, and had been training since childhood at
  no cost to any treasury. For two thousand years that made the
  grasslands the most dangerous ground on any map — and gunpowder
  plus the plough ended it inside a century.
ROOT: war / an advantage that costs one side nothing and the other
  side a fortune stays decisive until the price of the answer falls.
CANNOT: no matching that mobility with infantry who must carry their
  food, so no beating steppe cavalry by chasing it. And no steppe
  power after cheap firearms and settled farming: the grass was
  ploughed, the guns were bought with taxes, and the advantage never
  came back.
THREAD: history (a weapon redistributing power rather than winning a
  battle), farming (grassland as a military resource), power (the
  means of force deciding who holds it).
ASKED-AS: mongols horsemen steppe cavalry fast archers why unstoppable gunpowder ended nomads empires

ESSENCE: a sea power buys reach and trade and cannot occupy
  anything; a land power can take and hold ground and cannot get at
  an island. Each is nearly untouchable by the other, which is why
  their wars run long, end in exhaustion, and are usually settled by
  a third party's money.
ROOT: war / the means of moving force decide what force can do, and
  fleets and armies simply cannot reach the same targets.
CANNOT: no naval power deciding a war by sea alone — a fleet cannot
  take a capital, so it can only strangle and wait. And no land
  power projecting past its own coast without a fleet it does not
  have: hulls take years and trained crews take longer.
THREAD: war (sea control against sea denial), trade (a blockade as a
  weapon that works slowly), money (whoever funds both sides shaping
  how it ends).
ASKED-AS: sea power land power navy army war long athens sparta britain france blockade

ESSENCE: long-distance trade before international law ran on
  scattered communities of one people — a cousin in every port, who
  could be trusted because ruin at home was the price of cheating.
  The network was the enforcement, and a shared religion or language
  was how membership got proved.
ROOT: law / an agreement needs a way of punishing the breaking of
  it, and where no court reaches across a border, reputation inside
  a closed group is the only mechanism left.
CANNOT: no long-distance credit between strangers with no shared
  enforcement, so no trade beyond what can be paid for on the spot.
  And no diaspora advantage once courts and insurance work across
  borders: the very thing that made the group valuable turns into an
  ordinary business cost.
THREAD: law (a contract as the stranger's version of a
  relationship), money (credit resting on an ability to punish),
  anthropology (a network as the actual product being made).
ASKED-AS: merchant communities abroad trust trade networks traders foreign ports credit families cousins

ESSENCE: wherever strangers must deal regularly, one language
  becomes the shared one — and it is picked by traffic, never by
  beauty or grammar. It is the tongue of whoever holds the trade,
  the army or the paperwork, and it outlives the power that
  installed it by a very long time.
ROOT: a shared standard is worth more the more people share it, so
  whichever is already most used keeps winning for that reason
  alone.
CANNOT: no neutral choice of a common tongue — whichever wins
  advantages its native speakers permanently, in school, in court
  and in argument. And no common language without loss: the second
  language of everybody becomes the first language of somebody, and
  the small tongues around it thin out.
THREAD: language (a written standard chosen by whoever held the
  presses), history (a standard nobody can change alone), power (a
  shared language as a state's cheapest glue).
ASKED-AS: common language trade english latin arabic why everyone speaks lingua franca learn second

ESSENCE: the faiths that crossed the world are the ones that could
  be detached from a place, a bloodline and a language — a god not
  tied to one river, a membership you can join rather than be born
  into, and a text that survives translation. Local cults are not
  less holy; they simply cannot travel.
ROOT: whatever spreads is whatever can be carried, and a belief tied
  to one mountain has no way of arriving anywhere else.
CANNOT: no universal religion built on ancestors — a cult of one
  family's dead cannot admit a stranger, so its ceiling is that
  family. And no travelling without portable practice: what survives
  relocation is whatever needs no particular ground, season or
  shrine to be done properly.
THREAD: anthropology (a rite doing its work by being performed),
  trade (what moves in a merchant's baggage), language (translation
  as a condition of spreading at all).
ASKED-AS: religions spread world christianity islam buddhism converting joining local gods travel faith

ESSENCE: no empire ever ruled its provinces directly. It ruled
  through local men who already had authority, paid them with a
  share of the take, and called the arrangement administration. So
  the empire's grip is exactly as good as those men's interests, and
  every province holds a ready-made government waiting for a bad
  year.
ROOT: power / rule needs agents, and agents far away must be given
  enough discretion to act — which is the same thing as giving them
  a base.
CANNOT: no governing at distance without intermediaries, so no
  empire that is not partly a standing negotiation with the people
  it conquered. And no loyal intermediary in a crisis: the man who
  holds a province for you holds it, and when the centre weakens he
  simply stops sending.
THREAD: government (a permanent staff outlasting its ministers),
  economy (an agent with interests of his own), history (an empire's
  reach set by its communications).
ASKED-AS: empire ruled locals governors chiefs collected taxes provinces rebellion loyalty distance agents

ESSENCE: the edge of an empire is where the money goes out. Frontier
  provinces are poor, need garrisons, produce almost nothing
  taxable, and get defended for reasons of prestige long after the
  accounts stopped making sense. Expansion is profitable early and a
  loss for centuries.
ROOT: money / the yield of new ground falls because the good land is
  taken first, while the cost of holding it rises with distance.
CANNOT: no empire that can stop expanding comfortably — an army
  built for conquest is expensive at rest and dangerous when idle,
  so the machine runs on past the point of profit. And no cheap
  withdrawal: pulling back from a frontier is read everywhere as
  weakness, which is its own bill.
THREAD: war (occupation costs), government (a benefit given being
  nearly impossible to withdraw), economy (the point where the next
  unit stops paying for itself).
ASKED-AS: empire expansion cost frontier garrisons borders defending far provinces expensive stopped growing

ESSENCE: the commonest way for a strong state to fall over is the
  death of a strong ruler. Personal authority cannot be inherited —
  only the chair can — so every handover is an open question with
  armies attached, and a system that solves succession badly gets
  destroyed by its own best periods.
ROOT: power / an office stands up because everybody is in the habit
  of obeying it, and a habit attached to one person expires with
  that person.
CANNOT: no personal rule with a safe succession — the very qualities
  that made the ruler strong are the ones that cannot be handed on.
  And no succession rule that is free: strict inheritance produces
  incompetents, open contest produces civil wars, and every society
  is choosing which of the two it prefers.
THREAD: government (you can only punish a decision you can
  attribute), history (revolutions as legitimacy failures),
  anthropology (standing granted by others rather than declared).
ASKED-AS: king died civil war succession heir throne fight brothers empire collapse ruler replaced

ESSENCE: a good century produces more sons of rich families than
  there are places for them. They are educated, connected, expensive
  and unemployed at the top — and a surplus of frustrated insiders
  is far more dangerous to a regime than any amount of ordinary
  poverty, because they know how the machine works.
ROOT: people together / positions have to be assigned, and when
  qualified claimants outrun the chairs, the losers do not accept
  the verdict quietly.
CANNOT: no stable elite that grows faster than its own offices — the
  arithmetic forces a fight, and the fight is between insiders
  rather than between classes. And no fixing it by making more
  offices: each new post creates its own claimants and its own
  running cost.
THREAD: government (a legitimacy failure rather than a misery peak),
  economy (competition for a fixed number of rents), history (change
  happening by replacement of people).
ASKED-AS: too many educated no jobs elites unrest factions rich sons positions competition instability

ESSENCE: for most of history a strong government took a few pennies
  in the pound of what its people produced, and a modern one takes
  about a third. That rise is the real history of the state, and it
  happened because what could be taxed changed — from things caught
  at a gate to incomes written in a book somebody else was already
  keeping.
ROOT: government / a tax is only collectable where the thing taxed
  is already being recorded by someone with a reason of their own to
  record it.
CANNOT: no income tax before employers, banks and firms keep their
  own books — the state cannot afford to watch every transaction
  itself, so it taxes where somebody else is already watching. And
  no ancient ruler collecting like a modern one however absolute:
  absolutism describes the claim, and the receipts describe the
  reality.
THREAD: economy (the cost of collection deciding which taxes exist),
  history (writing invented for accounts), accounting (a ledger kept
  for one purpose serving another).
ASKED-AS: taxes ancient kings collected little modern government third income records gates customs

ESSENCE: the same plague can free peasants in one country and bind
  them in another. Where landlords were many and had to compete for
  scarce hands, workers bargained; where landlords were few and
  could act together, they tied the survivors to the soil by law.
  The disease supplies the scarcity and the politics decides who
  collects it.
ROOT: money / a shortage raises a price only where buyers must
  compete, and where the buyers can combine, the shortage is
  answered with force instead.
CANNOT: no automatic gain to labour from a labour shortage — the
  outcome hangs on whether the other side can act as one. And no
  reading a wage change as a law of nature: the same shock, in the
  same century, gave opposite results on either side of a line drawn
  across one continent.
THREAD: economy (bargaining power depending on the number of
  buyers), law (a statute doing what a market would not), history
  (the plague raising wages — and where it did the reverse).
ASKED-AS: black death serfdom eastern europe peasants freed tied land wages landlords opposite

ESSENCE: a run of cold or dry decades does not destroy a society; it
  raises the price of everything and shortens everybody's temper.
  Harvests fall, the taxes are still demanded, migrants arrive, and
  arrangements that worked in good years stop working — so climate
  turns up in the record as war, revolt and famine rather than as
  weather.
ROOT: a system with no slack fails at whatever it was already
  weakest at, and the trigger then gets mistaken for the cause.
CANNOT: no reading a collapse straight off a climate record — the
  same drought is survived by one polity and fatal to its
  neighbour, so weather is a load and not a verdict. And no society
  with real reserves failing at the first bad year: the
  vulnerability was built long before the trigger arrived.
THREAD: chance (a shock finding the weakness already present),
  farming (a harvest as the buffer everything else lives on),
  history (contingency hidden afterwards by hindsight).
ASKED-AS: little ice age drought caused collapse famine revolts climate history weather empires stress

ESSENCE: collapse is a loss of complexity, not a loss of people. The
  palace stops, long-distance trade stops, writing stops, the big
  buildings stop — and most of the population goes on farming, often
  eating better and working less, because the layer that was taking
  a third of the crop has gone.
ROOT: money / an elaborate society is expensive, and the expense is
  paid by the people at the bottom, so removing it is not a
  straightforward loss to them.
CANNOT: no collapse that looks the same from the palace and from the
  field — the sources were written by the losers of it, which is why
  every surviving account is a lament. And no complexity without a
  surplus to buy it: when the surplus falls, the layers come off in
  the reverse of the order they went on.
THREAD: archaeology (bones recording ordinary health while monuments
  record what was affordable), economy (overheads only a growing
  take can carry), history (what survives being what the rich
  built).
ASKED-AS: civilisation collapsed what happened people ordinary life after rome dark age simpler villages

ESSENCE: a dark age is usually dark to us and not to the people in
  it. When the scribes stop being paid the writing stops; when trade
  in fine goods stops, the diggable evidence thins out. The silence
  is a measurement of the recording system, and we keep reading it
  as a description of the life.
ROOT: evidence / any record is produced by an institution, so the
  end of the institution ends the record without ending the events.
CANNOT: no history of a period whose record-keepers were the first
  thing to go, so no arguing from that silence that nothing
  happened. And no even darkness: the gap is deep where writing was
  a state monopoly and shallow where a temple, a church or a
  merchant class kept writing for reasons of its own.
THREAD: archaeology (arguing from absence on a tiny sample), keeping
  knowledge (formats dying with their institutions), history (an
  era's voice being the voice of its clerks).
ASKED-AS: dark ages nothing written records lost why gap history missing sources period silent

ESSENCE: when a conquest is recorded as total, the fields, the field
  boundaries, the villages, the saints' days and the local families
  usually carry straight on. The names at the top change and the tax
  goes to a new address, and the deep arrangements outlast every
  dynasty that claims to have replaced them.
ROOT: people together / an arrangement that works is expensive to
  replace, so a new ruler keeps it and puts a new name on it.
CANNOT: no conqueror rebuilding a countryside — the incomers are too
  few and they need this year's harvest, so the practical choice is
  always to inherit. And no reading a change of ruler as a change of
  society: a chronicle records the succession because successions
  are what chronicles record.
THREAD: archaeology (the ground recording routine while documents
  record events), government (permanent staff outlasting ministers),
  history (institutions as frozen answers to old questions).
ASKED-AS: conquest changed everything really invaders villages continued same fields names dynasty life

ESSENCE: in several unconnected places, within a few centuries,
  people began asking the same new questions — whether the powerful
  are also bound by right and wrong, whether a person owes anything
  to a stranger, and what a good life is as distinct from a
  successful one. The answers differ wildly; the questions barely
  differ at all.
ROOT: one species meeting one new situation — cities, coinage,
  writing, and empires large enough to be full of strangers —
  produces the same headings out of different heads. (UNSURE whether
  the clustering in time is real or an effect of what survives.)
CANNOT: no such question in a society where everyone you deal with
  is a relative — a rule for strangers is only needed once strangers
  are routine. And no single origin for it: the ideas appear in
  places with no plausible contact, so borrowing cannot be the
  explanation.
THREAD: anthropology (the same headings everywhere with wildly
  different contents), right and wrong (rules stretched past the kin
  group), history (an invention arriving when its parts do).
ASKED-AS: same time philosophers buddha confucius greece prophets questions morality everywhere why coincidence

ESSENCE: an old law code is not mainly a working manual for judges.
  It is a public advertisement that this ruler brings order — carved
  where people gather, opening with the king's name and the gods'
  approval, addressed to everybody, about who deserves to be obeyed.
ROOT: government / legitimacy is cheaper than force, so a ruler
  spends on visible fairness the way another might spend on walls.
CANNOT: no code proving what courts actually did — surviving
  verdicts routinely ignore the written tariff, so the text records
  an intention and not a practice. And no lawgiver publishing
  without claiming: being seen to have set the rules is the whole
  purpose of publication.
THREAD: law (the statute against the enforcement), anthropology (a
  stated rule against the observed one), power (a written promise
  working as a currency).
ASKED-AS: hammurabi law code carved stone ancient rules justice king published courts actually followed

ESSENCE: forced labour did not end because people worked out that it
  was wrong; plenty already knew. It ended where a coalition with
  power decided to spend real money and force on stopping it, and
  where the work could be got another way — and it came back under
  other names wherever either of those was missing.
ROOT: money / an institution ends when it stops paying or when
  somebody with force makes it stop, and moral argument works by
  changing who is holding the force.
CANNOT: no abolition without enforcement — a ban with nobody
  patrolling it relocates a trade rather than ending it. And no
  clean ending: indenture, debt bondage, convict labour and
  confiscated wages followed immediately, doing the same work under
  legal names.
THREAD: history (coerced labour tracking the crop), law (what a
  statute permits, a ledger will attempt), economy (a wage set by
  what the alternative pays).
ASKED-AS: slavery ended abolition why stopped moral economic navy indenture bonded labour replaced

ESSENCE: there were three quite different projects wearing one word.
  A trading empire wanted posts and a monopoly and few settlers; an
  extractive one wanted a workforce and a mine; a settler one wanted
  the land and had no use for the people already on it. What a place
  became depended mostly on which of the three arrived.
ROOT: money / an enterprise builds whatever institutions its revenue
  requires, and those institutions then outlive the enterprise.
CANNOT: no settler colony where the disease burden killed the
  settlers, so the map of which kind went where was partly written
  by mosquitoes. And no separating an institution from its
  afterlife: the courts, land titles and labour rules built for
  extraction are still standing and still doing their job.
THREAD: geography (disease deciding where dense settlement was
  possible), government (institutions as frozen answers to dead
  questions), economy (why some countries never catch up).
ASKED-AS: colonies different types settlers plantations trading posts empire land labour extraction varied

ESSENCE: why one corner of the world industrialised first is the
  largest open argument in the subject. Coal in the right place,
  cheap colonies, wages high enough to make machines worth buying,
  patent law, competing states, useful science, plain luck — each
  explanation is defended by serious people and none of them settles
  it.
ROOT: history / an invention waits on its parts, and where many
  prerequisites ripened together no single one can be shown to be
  the cause. (UNSURE, and honestly so: this entry records a live
  dispute rather than a finding.)
CANNOT: no single-cause account surviving contact with the
  comparisons — every proposed cause is present somewhere that did
  not industrialise. And no experiment available: there is one case
  and no control, so the question cannot be settled by evidence of
  the ordinary kind.
THREAD: history (prerequisites ripening, and the priority fights
  that follow), evidence (one case and no control group), economy
  (why catching up is easy in theory and rare in fact).
ASKED-AS: industrial revolution why britain first europe china debate coal colonies causes historians

ESSENCE: the new countries kept the old lines almost everywhere, on
  purpose. The borders had been drawn by strangers and cut through
  peoples — and the leaders agreed to keep them anyway, because the
  alternative was every neighbour claiming every group forever. It
  was a bad settlement chosen over a worse one.
ROOT: government / a boundary's value is mostly that it is settled,
  and an agreed wrong line does more work than a contested right
  one.
CANNOT: no redrawing one border without opening all of them — the
  principle used to justify one change justifies every other change
  too. And no line satisfying both the land and the people where the
  two are interleaved: a border has one position and the populations
  have many.
THREAD: geography (borders following rivers, ridges or nothing at
  all), war (a settlement that sows the next war), government
  (sovereignty as a claim needing recognition by others).
ASKED-AS: african borders straight lines colonial kept independence why not redrawn tribes divided

ESSENCE: no society has ever thought of itself as an episode. The
  people inside the strongest moment of any empire wrote as though
  the arrangement were the natural order and the alternatives were
  barbarism — and the few who did warn of decline were usually
  complaining about morals rather than seeing any mechanism.
ROOT: the mind / whatever is learned first is filed as the world
  rather than as one version of it, and one stable lifetime is
  enough to establish that.
CANNOT: no society seeing its own ending in advance, because the
  signal is indistinguishable from the ordinary bad years it has
  survived many times before. And no reading a period's confidence
  as evidence of its strength: the loudest talk of permanence often
  sits in the last century.
THREAD: history (every age believing it is the modern one),
  anthropology (your own arrangements not feeling like
  arrangements), story (a period looking coherent only once it has
  an ending).
ASKED-AS: empires thought forever decline warning signs people knew end coming permanent felt normal

ESSENCE: herding is not an earlier stage on the road to farming. It
  is a specialised way of using land too dry, too steep or too cold
  to plough, and it needs farmers next door to trade with — so it
  appears after agriculture rather than before it, and it survives
  exactly where crops cannot.
ROOT: the living world / an animal turns grass a person cannot eat
  into food a person can, which is worth doing only where that grass
  is the best the land offers.
CANNOT: no pastoral society independent of grain — herders
  everywhere buy it, trade for it or take it, so the pure nomad has
  never existed. And no ladder of stages: societies have moved into
  herding and back out of it repeatedly, in both directions, as
  conditions changed.
THREAD: anthropology (no living people being anybody's ancestors),
  farming (livestock as a way of storing a bad harvest), geography
  (grass at the edge of what the rainfall allows).
ASKED-AS: nomads herders stage before farming pastoral tribes grass dry land cattle sheep

ESSENCE: for most of history there was somewhere to go. Hills,
  marshes, forests and deserts held large populations who farmed
  scattered crops, kept no records, and arranged their lives so that
  nobody could be taxed — not because they had never heard of
  states, but because they had, and had left.
ROOT: power / extraction needs the thing taken to be findable, so a
  way of life arranged to be unfindable is a defence rather than a
  backwardness.
CANNOT: no escaping in a full world — the refuges closed when roads,
  aircraft and land registers reached them, and that closing is
  recent. And no reading such peoples as leftovers: the practices
  that make a population hard to tax are choices, and often
  deliberate reversals of farming.
THREAD: anthropology (no ladder of societies from simple to
  advanced), geography (terrain raising the cost of reach),
  government (being legible as the precondition of being ruled).
ASKED-AS: hill tribes avoided state taxes hiding forests marshes escaped rulers stateless people refuge

ESSENCE: an army of farmers goes home for the harvest and cannot be
  used against its own villages. A paid professional army does
  neither — it is available all year, it eats from the treasury
  rather than from the countryside, and it is the reason a ruler can
  act against his own nobles and his own people.
ROOT: power / the means of force decide who holds it, and a force
  fed from one hand belongs to that hand.
CANNOT: no long campaign with a seasonal levy — the war stops at
  harvest, which is why early wars were short and local. And no
  professional army without steady revenue: the pay must arrive
  every month, so the army and the tax system get built together or
  not at all.
THREAD: history (coin as a state technology for paying soldiers),
  government (the force that protects a state can take it), economy
  (a fixed cost that has to be met in cash).
ASKED-AS: standing army professional soldiers paid levy harvest militia king power nobles taxes

ESSENCE: a ruler who can borrow can fight a war before winning it.
  But lenders only lend to a state that can be made to repay, so the
  governments that borrowed cheapest were the ones whose rulers had

ESSENCE: rulers again and again staffed their inner government with

ESSENCE: a walled city is a statement that the countryside is

ESSENCE: a farming household that has to eat every year does not try
  to grow the most food. It tries to avoid the year with none.

  the best one, obligations to neighbours instead of savings — all
  of it looks inefficient and all of it is insurance.

  against good ones, so the sensible play is to cut the worst case
