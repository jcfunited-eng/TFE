# 160 DEEP CITIES AND INFRASTRUCTURE — the systems that make settlement possible
A city is a few million people living on top of arrangements nobody
thinks about: water arriving, waste leaving, the current holding
steady, a road clearing before the next hour's traffic. Every one of
them has a capacity, a failure mode, and a bill that falls due
later. This file is about those, not about buildings.

ESSENCE: the first limit on how big a settlement can get is how much
  water arrives every day and how far away it has to come from.
  Food, fuel and work can all be fetched from further off as
  transport improves; water is heavy, needed daily, and cannot be
  economised below a floor.
ROOT: physics / water is dense and will not compress, so moving it
  costs energy in direct proportion to its volume and the height it
  is lifted.
THREAD: geography (settlements sitting where the water sits), energy
  (pumping as a city's largest single electricity user), history
  (works built long before anyone understood germs).
ASKED-AS: city water supply limit reservoir aqueduct grow bigger daily amount pipes distance source

ESSENCE: a water or power system is not sized for what people use.
  It is sized for the worst hour of the worst day — everybody
  showers at the same time, cooks at the same time, and waters the
  garden in the same week — so pipes and cables are built several
  times larger than the average suggests and sit mostly idle.
ROOT: engineering / a network has to deliver the maximum
  simultaneous demand, and an average tells you nothing whatever
  about simultaneity.
THREAD: logistics (queues made by variability rather than by
  shortage), energy (a grid built for the coldest evening of the
  decade), economy (a fixed cost carried by rare use).
ASKED-AS: peak demand water pressure drops everyone showers pipes sized average usage evening capacity

ESSENCE: what matters about a reservoir is not how much it holds but
  how much it can supply reliably through the worst drought on
  record. A huge lake fed by a small river yields little; a modest
  one fed by a big catchment yields a lot. The useful number is a
  rate, not a volume.
ROOT: measurement / a store's worth is set by the flow through it
  against the length of the shortfall it must bridge.
THREAD: chance (a rare event sitting outside a short record),
  geography (the catchment as the real asset), logistics (cover
  measured in days of demand).
ASKED-AS: reservoir size drought water supply refill rainfall catchment yield storage empty summer restrictions

ESSENCE: water in pipes has to be pushed uphill and held back coming
  down, so a hilly city is cut into zones, each with its own tank
  and its own pressure. Too little and the top floors run dry; too
  much and the pipes burst. The map of those zones is the contour
  lines wearing a different name.
ROOT: physics / pressure in a standing column depends only on
  height, so every ten metres of elevation is a fixed change nobody
  can design away.
THREAD: physics (a column of fluid and its own weight), building
  (roof tanks doing the same job one address at a time), maintenance
  (leakage driven by pressure more than by age).
ASKED-AS: water pressure low top floor tank hill zones bursts pipes pumping high buildings

ESSENCE: a water network loses a large slice of everything it
  pumps — often a fifth or more — through thousands of small leaks
  in buried pipes nobody can see. Finding one costs more than the
  water it wastes, so the honest question is not how to stop leaks
  but how much leakage is worth stopping.
ROOT: economy / repair cost climbs steeply as leaks get smaller and
  rarer while the value saved falls, so there is an economic level
  of loss and it is not zero.
THREAD: maintenance (a fault cheaper to tolerate than to find),
  economy (the last few per cent costing as much as all the rest),
  measurement (a loss inferred rather than observed).
ASKED-AS: water leaks pipes lost percentage repair mains burst underground waste finding cost supply

ESSENCE: treating water at the works is not enough, because the
  pipes between there and the tap are long, warm in places and never
  sterile. So a little disinfectant is left in the water on purpose,
  to keep working all the way along — and the far end of the
  network, where it has faded, is where the quality problems live.
ROOT: chemistry / a disinfectant is consumed as it works, so its
  concentration falls with time and distance travelled.
THREAD: chemistry (a reaction continuing after it leaves the
  vessel), public health (the pipe and the drain doing more than any
  clinic), measurement (a reading valid only where it was taken).
ASKED-AS: chlorine taste water pipes quality tap tested plant dead end stagnant flushing network

ESSENCE: a sewer does not destroy anything; it moves it. The first
  great sewer systems carried a city's waste straight into the river
  that city drank from, and death rates rose before they fell. What
  changed everything was separating the intake from the outfall —
  first in distance, and eventually by treatment.
ROOT: public health / infection travels from waste to mouth, so the
  whole benefit lies in breaking that path and not in the pipe.
THREAD: geography (upstream and downstream as a political
  relationship), public health (preventing exposure beating treating
  cases), law (a harm exported past the boundary that would price
  it).
ASKED-AS: sewers river waste dumped downstream drinking water cholera treatment outfall city disease

ESSENCE: older cities put rain and sewage down one pipe, which works
  until it rains hard — then the flow exceeds what the works can
  take and the excess goes into the river untreated, by design.
  Separating them means a second network under every street, which
  is why almost nobody has finished doing it.
ROOT: engineering / a system sized for the average cannot carry the
  extreme, so an overflow is built in somewhere and the only
  question is where it discharges.
THREAD: drainage (rain as the largest and least controllable input),
  economy (a capital cost that is almost all excavation), law (a
  permitted discharge that looks like a failure and is a design).
ASKED-AS: sewage overflow rain storm drains combined river pollution heavy rainfall separate pipes cost

ESSENCE: sewage treatment is three jobs in order — let the solids
  settle, let bacteria eat what is dissolved, then polish and
  disinfect what is left. Each stage removes a different thing,
  costs more than the last, and only works if the one before it did
  its job properly.
ROOT: chemistry and the living world / different contaminants are
  removed by different mechanisms, and settling, digestion and
  disinfection do not substitute for one another.
THREAD: chemistry (a process built of stages in sequence), the
  living world (a managed population doing industrial work),
  maintenance (a system whose failure surfaces one stage later).
ASKED-AS: sewage treatment works stages settling bacteria tanks clean water discharged smell process river

ESSENCE: everything a city consumes ends up mixed together in one
  pipe, so a sample from the sewer measures the whole population at
  once — what it eats, what it takes, what infection is going round
  — days before anybody visits a doctor.
ROOT: measurement / a mixed flow is an automatic average over
  everyone contributing to it, which is a sample no survey could
  afford to buy.
THREAD: measurement (a proxy carrying an assumed constant), public
  health (surveillance not depending on people coming forward),
  evidence (a record made with no audience in mind).
ASKED-AS: sewage testing drugs virus levels city population monitoring wastewater samples early warning flow

ESSENCE: a power network is built so that any single thing can fail
  without anybody noticing. Every important line, transformer and
  generator has a partner able to take its load — so the rule is not
  "nothing breaks" but "one thing may break at a time".
ROOT: engineering / failures of individual components are
  unavoidable and roughly independent, so reliability is bought by
  arranging that no single one matters.
THREAD: logistics (efficiency meaning nothing spare), engineering
  (redundancy through independent paths), government (preparation
  that looks like waste until the week it does not).
ASKED-AS: power grid backup redundancy line fails spare capacity blackout single fault design standard

ESSENCE: the frequency of the mains is the balance between
  generation and demand made visible. Every generator on the network
  turns in step, so if demand exceeds supply they all slow together
  and the frequency falls. Reading it is reading the whole system's
  books in real time.
ROOT: physics / rotating machines tied to one network are
  mechanically locked together, so their common speed reports the
  net torque on the entire set.
THREAD: physics (a shared shaft as a shared measurement), control (a
  feedback loop whose speed decides stability), energy (storage as
  the missing piece of every electricity system).
ASKED-AS: mains frequency hertz grid balance generators demand supply drop blackout inertia stability seconds

ESSENCE: the great blackouts get the attention, but almost every
  power cut a household actually experiences comes from the last
  mile — a tree on a line, a car into a pole, a fuse in the street.
  Transmission is heavily engineered and rarely fails; distribution
  is long, cheap and exposed.
ROOT: engineering / reliability spending goes where a failure
  affects the most people, so the parts serving few customers each
  get the least protection.
THREAD: economy (spending following consequence rather than
  frequency), maintenance (tree-cutting as the largest single
  cause), measurement (an average outage figure hiding where faults
  really are).
ASKED-AS: power cut street tree line pole fuse local outage grid blackout neighbourhood restored

ESSENCE: most power stations need electricity to start up — for
  pumps, fans, controls and cooling. If the whole network goes down
  there is nowhere to get it from, so restoring a grid means
  starting a handful of special plants that can begin unaided and
  building the system outward, section by section, over many hours.
ROOT: engineering / a system whose operation depends on its own
  output cannot restart itself, so recovery needs a resource
  deliberately kept outside the loop.
THREAD: computing (a bootstrap needing something outside itself),
  engineering (a dependency loop), government (capacity that only
  shows its worth in a crisis).
ASKED-AS: blackout restart grid power stations need electricity start black restore hours sections outage

ESSENCE: buried cables do not blow down in storms, and they cost
  several times as much to lay and far more to repair — because
  finding a fault underground is slow and digging is expensive. The
  real choice is between frequent short failures and rare long ones.
ROOT: engineering / protection against one class of failure is
  bought by accepting worse behaviour in another, and no arrangement
  is best on both counts.
THREAD: maintenance (repair time as the hidden half of reliability),
  infrastructure (digging as the dominant cost of everything under a
  street), economy (a trade between how often and how long).
ASKED-AS: power lines underground overhead storms cost bury cables repair digging outage duration streets

ESSENCE: a gas network stores its own buffer inside the pipes. The
  pressure is raised overnight and drawn down through the morning
  peak, so the mains themselves act as a tank — which is how gas
  meets a heating demand several times larger than the electricity
  system's peak without anything like the same trouble.
ROOT: physics / a compressible fluid stores energy by being
  squeezed, and a large network held at pressure holds a great deal
  of it.
THREAD: energy (storage as the deciding difference between fuels),
  physics (compressibility as a store), safety (a hazard managed by
  making it detectable).
ASKED-AS: gas pipes pressure storage heating peak morning network supply leak smell added

ESSENCE: heat is cheap to make and expensive to move. A district
  system pipes hot water from one plant to hundreds of buildings,
  and it only pays where those buildings are close together and want
  heat for most of the year — so the same scheme is excellent in a
  dense cold city and absurd in a warm suburb.
ROOT: physics / heat leaks out of a pipe in proportion to its length
  and the temperature difference, so the loss is fixed and the
  benefit depends on how much is delivered per metre.
THREAD: energy (waste heat as a resource with a delivery problem),
  geography (density as the condition for anything shared), economy
  (a network needing minimum take-up before it can exist).
ASKED-AS: district heating pipes hot water buildings shared boiler city dense losses connect scheme

ESSENCE: moving water is usually the largest single thing a city's
  utilities spend electricity on — lifting it out of the ground,
  pushing it up to the tanks, and pumping the sewage back down to
  the works. A water bill is very largely an energy bill wearing a
  different name.
ROOT: physics / raising a mass against gravity costs a fixed amount
  of energy per ton per metre, and a city moves an enormous number
  of tons every day.
THREAD: energy (a fixed physical cost hidden under an unrelated
  service), geography (a city's elevation deciding its running
  costs), economy (two prices coupled through an invisible input).
ASKED-AS: water pumping energy electricity cost lifting uphill treatment bills utility power biggest

ESSENCE: the water mains under a street are not sized for drinking.
  They are sized for the flow a fire brigade needs out of a hydrant,
  which is many times what the houses use — so the whole network
  runs oversized and slow-moving for an event that is rare and
  matters enormously.
ROOT: engineering / a network sized for its largest required draw
  serves every smaller draw automatically, and the largest draw here
  is not a domestic one.
THREAD: safety (a rare requirement dominating an everyday design),
  public health (stagnation as the price of capacity), economy
  (capital spent on a use almost never exercised).
ASKED-AS: water mains size hydrant fire brigade flow street pipes oversized drinking demand quality

ESSENCE: streets do two incompatible jobs — carrying traffic
  through, and giving access to the buildings on them. A road doing
  both does neither well, so networks get sorted into a hierarchy: a
  few big roads that move and many small ones that serve, with the
  conflict pushed onto the junctions between them.
ROOT: engineering / movement wants few interruptions and access
  requires constant ones, so the two demands cannot be met on the
  same piece of pavement.
THREAD: architecture (a street working or failing like a room),
  logistics (a network sorted into trunks and branches), government
  (a decision about who carries a shared cost).
ASKED-AS: roads main street residential through traffic access hierarchy rat runs junctions capacity distributor

ESSENCE: a road's capacity is not set by its width but by its
  junctions. Between them cars flow easily; at them everybody has to
  take turns — so adding lanes between two crossings simply moves
  more cars into the same queue, and a network runs at the speed of
  its worst intersection.
ROOT: logistics / a system produces what its constraint produces,
  and on a road network the constraint is wherever flows have to
  cross each other.
THREAD: logistics (a bottleneck that moves rather than vanishes),
  engineering (a chain limited by one stage), economy (an investment
  whose benefit is captured by the next constraint).
ASKED-AS: traffic jams junction lights lanes widening road capacity queue bottleneck roundabout intersection flow

ESSENCE: traffic lights do not create capacity, they divide it.
  Every second given to one direction is taken from another, and a
  few seconds are lost at every change while the junction clears —
  so a signalled route spends a real fraction of its time empty on
  purpose, and coordinating lights along a road is worth more than
  perfecting any single one.
ROOT: measurement / a fixed resource shared among competing claims
  is a scheduling problem, and the scheduling overhead is itself a
  real cost.
THREAD: computing (scheduling with switching costs), logistics
  (changeover time as lost capacity), choosing (a fixed pot divided
  among claimants).
ASKED-AS: traffic lights timing green wave sequence junction wait longer coordinated signals delay phases

ESSENCE: a motorway moves the most vehicles just below the point
  where it jams. Past that, everybody brakes, gaps open, and
  throughput actually falls — so the fullest road is not the busiest
  one, and the worst thing that can happen to a road's output is for
  it to become completely full.
ROOT: flow / spacing between vehicles grows as drivers react to each
  other, so density and speed trade against each other and their
  product peaks somewhere in the middle.
THREAD: logistics (waiting rising steeply as a resource approaches
  full), physics (flow as density times speed), crowds (the same
  curve for people on foot).
ASKED-AS: motorway jam slower more cars capacity flow breakdown stop start traffic congestion ramp

ESSENCE: a bus or train line needs enough people living and working
  within a short walk of it to fill vehicles all day. Below that
  number the service runs empty, gets cut back, becomes useless, and
  loses the passengers it had — so the failure is a spiral rather
  than a shortfall.
ROOT: economy / a scheduled service costs nearly the same full or
  empty, so cost per passenger is dominated by how many people are
  within reach of it.
THREAD: geography (settlement pattern deciding what services are
  possible), economy (a fixed cost divided by a shrinking base),
  architecture (density as the thing that pays for the bus).
ASKED-AS: bus train empty cut service frequency passengers density suburbs walk station subsidy spiral

ESSENCE: from a passenger's point of view, time spent waiting counts
  about twice as heavily as time spent moving. So a slower bus every
  ten minutes beats a faster one every forty, and the most effective
  improvement to almost any transit system is running it more often
  rather than making it quicker.
ROOT: the mind / time spent unable to act and not knowing when it
  ends is experienced as far longer than time spent travelling.
THREAD: the mind (waiting felt as longer than moving), logistics
  (batch size deciding responsiveness), economy (an improvement
  costing operating money rather than capital).
ASKED-AS: bus every ten minutes timetable waiting frequency faster service turn up go schedule

ESSENCE: a bus spends a surprising share of its journey standing
  still with the doors open. Every second of boarding is multiplied
  by every stop and every trip, so paying before you board, two
  doors, and a floor level with the kerb do more for speed than any
  change to the engine or the traffic.
ROOT: logistics / total time is moving plus stopping, and the
  stopping term grows with the number of stops rather than with
  distance.
THREAD: logistics (changeover time dominating throughput), design
  (a step and a door deciding a whole system's speed), economy (a
  small saving multiplied by an enormous count).
ASKED-AS: bus slow stops boarding doors tickets wheelchair kerb dwell time journey passengers speed

ESSENCE: fares almost never cover the cost of running public
  transport, and chasing that target destroys the service. Raising
  fares and cutting quiet routes loses passengers, which raises cost
  per passenger, which justifies the next cut — so a system managed
  to its farebox ratio shrinks itself toward nothing.
ROOT: economy / most of the cost is fixed and much of the benefit
  lands on people not paying the fare, so the fare can never measure
  the service's worth.
THREAD: economy (a fixed cost divided by a shrinking base),
  government (a service whose benefits are not captured by its
  price), measurement (a metric that steers the thing it measures).
ASKED-AS: fares cover cost subsidy public transport losing money routes cut passengers ratio roads

ESSENCE: a rule requiring a fixed number of parking spaces per flat
  or per shop shapes a city more than any plan does. Once the spaces
  and the aisles to reach them are drawn, buildings must stand back
  from each other — so a code written to make streets work quietly
  forbids the street it was written for.
ROOT: geometry / space is conserved, so land committed to storing
  vehicles is land unavailable for everything else, in a ratio
  nobody chose on purpose.
THREAD: architecture (a rule deciding built form more than any
  design does), economy (a cost bundled into a price and hidden from
  the user), geography (land use decided by a number in a code).
ASKED-AS: parking spaces required flats shops rules land wasted cost rent car free minimum

ESSENCE: the three metres at the edge of a street is the most
  fought-over land in a city. Parking, deliveries, buses, taxis,
  bins, cycle lanes, trees, cafe tables and drainage all want it —
  and it is allocated almost entirely by historical accident and by
  whoever complains loudest.
ROOT: economy / a scarce resource given away free gets allocated by
  queueing, habit and political noise rather than by value.
THREAD: economy (a free resource reliably over-consumed), logistics
  (loading as the invisible half of urban freight), government (an
  allocation settled politically rather than by use).
ASKED-AS: kerb parking loading deliveries bus stop cycle lane space street competing uses double

ESSENCE: the last few miles of a delivery cost more than the
  thousands before it. One lorry crossing a continent is efficient;
  a hundred vans stopping every two minutes in traffic, with nowhere
  to park and nobody in to receive, is not — and ordering online has
  multiplied the stops without changing any of the streets.
ROOT: logistics / cost is driven by the number of handling events
  and stops rather than by distance, and a city maximises both.
THREAD: logistics (cost dominated by touches), economy (a saving
  nobody is positioned to capture), geography (the kerb as the
  binding constraint).
ASKED-AS: deliveries vans city parcels last mile parking failed redelivery online shopping congestion streets

ESSENCE: the cost of collecting rubbish is almost all in stopping
  and starting, not in carrying. Which is why collections are weekly
  rather than daily, why bins are standardised so a machine can lift
  them, and why adding a fourth recycling stream can cost more than
  the material it recovers is worth.
ROOT: logistics / a round's cost is driven by the number of stops
  and the time at each, so anything adding stops or handling is
  multiplied across the whole city.
THREAD: logistics (stops rather than distance driving cost), economy
  (a marginal service costing more than its yield), people together
  (a system resting on cooperation nobody enforces).
ASKED-AS: bin collection weekly rubbish recycling separate streams lorries cost rounds contamination sorting council

ESSENCE: each disposal route wins on something different. Landfill
  is cheapest and permanent — the space is gone and it leaks for
  decades. Burning recovers energy and needs expensive cleaning and
  a steady supply. Recycling saves real material for metals, glass
  and paper, and barely breaks even for most mixed plastics.
ROOT: chemistry / recovering a material is worth doing when the
  recycled route costs less energy than making it new, and that
  comparison comes out differently for every material.
THREAD: chemistry (energy of production deciding what is worth
  recovering), economy (a market being what makes recycling real),
  measurement (a rate reported as tonnes collected rather than
  tonnes used).
ASKED-AS: recycling worth it landfill incineration burning plastic waste energy honest better disposal rubbish

ESSENCE: trees in a street do several measured things — they shade
  the pavement by a wide margin, cool the air a little, slow
  traffic, hold back some rainfall, and raise the price of the
  houses. What they do not do is any of it cheaply or soon: they
  need root room, water, and forty years.
ROOT: the living world / a tree's benefits scale with its canopy,
  and canopy is a function of age and of how much underground room
  it was given at the start.
THREAD: the living world (growth as the limiting rate),
  infrastructure (roots and buried services fighting for one space),
  government (a benefit arriving several administrations later).
ASKED-AS: street trees shade cooling planting benefits roots pavement damage watering canopy years house

ESSENCE: every roof and every paved yard turns rain that would have
  soaked in into water that must be piped away. A city manufactures
  its own floods gradually, one extension at a time — and the drain
  laid decades ago is being asked to carry runoff from ground that
  was fields when it was sized.
ROOT: water / rain either soaks in, evaporates or runs off, and
  covering ground removes the first two options entirely.
THREAD: water (a catchment's response set by its surface), law (a
  small individual act with a shared cost), infrastructure (a design
  assumption quietly falsified over decades).
ASKED-AS: flooding paving driveways rain runoff drains soakaway gardens concrete surface water sewers

ESSENCE: a flood defence does not stop flooding. It raises the size
  of event needed to cause one, and it makes that flood worse when
  it finally comes — because more has been built behind the wall,
  and the water arrives all at once on being overtopped instead of
  gradually.
ROOT: chance / a barrier changes the shape of the outcomes rather
  than removing the tail, and cutting frequency while raising
  consequence is a trade and not a cure.
THREAD: chance (frequency traded against severity), geography
  (floodplains built on anyway), government (a protection that
  encourages the behaviour it was protecting against).
ASKED-AS: flood defences wall protection worse built behind exceeded barrier river levee risk downstream

ESSENCE: a hundred-year flood is not one that comes every hundred
  years. It is one with a one in a hundred chance in any given year
  — so two can arrive in consecutive years with nothing wrong, and
  across the life of a house the chance of meeting one is not small
  at all.
ROOT: chance / independent yearly trials produce clusters, and a
  long-run average says nothing whatever about the spacing of
  events.
THREAD: chance (rare events clustering), measurement (a figure
  derived from a series that is too short), geography (a hazard
  record shorter than the hazard's own cycle).
ASKED-AS: hundred year flood twice ten years chance meaning return period risk statistics insurance

ESSENCE: deferring maintenance is borrowing at a very high rate. The
  repair not done this year costs more next year and far more once
  the damage reaches the structure — but the saving is immediate and
  visible while the cost is delayed and lands on somebody else, so
  it is deferred nearly everywhere, nearly always.
ROOT: economy / decay compounds, so a postponed repair grows faster
  than money does, and the arithmetic of delay is always
  unfavourable.
THREAD: cleaning (order against decay, paid for continuously),
  government (a benefit invisible until it is withdrawn), economy (a
  cost pushed into the future at a terrible rate).
ASKED-AS: maintenance deferred repairs backlog roads bridges potholes worse later budget cuts crumbling

ESSENCE: everything built has a replacement date, and a city built
  quickly gets a replacement bill that arrives all at once. Pipes
  laid in one decade fail in one decade; a generation of bridges
  reaches its design life together — so the pattern of past building
  is a schedule of future spending nobody has budgeted.
ROOT: engineering / a component's life is roughly fixed when it is
  installed, so a cohort put in together comes out together.
THREAD: logistics (capacity arriving in indivisible lumps), economy
  (depreciation as a real cost that sends no bill), maintenance (a
  failure distribution with a shape rather than a flat rate).
ASKED-AS: pipes replaced age failing all together life expectancy bridges renewal programme built decade

ESSENCE: the expensive part of a buried service is never the pipe or
  the cable. It is the hole — closing the road, breaking the
  surface, working round everything already down there, and putting
  it all back. Which is why services share corridors, and why nobody
  wants to be the second one to dig.
ROOT: economy / the shared cost of access dominates the private cost
  of the asset, so whoever digs first is paying for everybody.
THREAD: economy (a shared cost nobody volunteers to bear), logistics
  (batching to avoid a repeated setup), government (coordination
  needing an authority nobody wants to create).
ASKED-AS: road dug up again utilities cables pipes trenches street works coordination cost holes

ESSENCE: the ground under a street is more crowded than the street
  above it. Water, sewers, gas, electricity, telephone, fibre,
  drains and the abandoned remains of all of them share a few metres
  of depth with no plan and no reliable register — so every
  excavation is exploratory, and hitting something is normal rather
  than careless.
ROOT: keeping knowledge / a record made by many parties over a
  century with no shared standard and no penalty for being wrong is
  not really a record.
THREAD: keeping knowledge (an archive that was never kept),
  engineering (as-built differing from as-drawn), infrastructure (a
  shared space with no owner).
ASKED-AS: underground pipes cables hit digging maps wrong services buried unknown abandoned crowded street

ESSENCE: people on foot behave like a fluid and have a capacity
  curve too. Below a certain density a crowd flows freely; past it
  everybody shuffles; and past that, the pressure of people behind
  can injure people in front who cannot move at all.
ROOT: flow / throughput is density times speed, and speed collapses
  as density climbs, so a crowd's ability to move has a peak and
  then a cliff.
THREAD: flow (density times speed), building (a stair or a doorway
  as the constraint on everything behind it), safety (a design case
  that must assume the worst arrival pattern).
ASKED-AS: crowd crush people packed exits stadium concert flow density panic pushing barriers safety

ESSENCE: some structures fail gracefully — a beam cracks, sags and
  warns. Others have members whose failure brings the whole thing
  down with no notice at all. Which kind a bridge is decides
  everything about how it must be inspected, and the ones with no
  second load path have to be looked at by a person, on the metal,
  forever.
ROOT: engineering / redundancy in a structure means an alternative
  load path, and a structure without one converts a local defect
  into a total collapse.
THREAD: engineering (a load path as a proof, and its absence as a
  risk), maintenance (inspection as the only substitute for
  redundancy), chance (a failure with no precursor).
ASKED-AS: bridge collapse inspection cracks warning steel corrosion single member redundancy checked structure failure

ESSENCE: a tunnel's real danger is not collapse, it is smoke. Heat
  and fumes have nowhere to rise to and travel faster than people
  walk, so a tunnel's ventilation is a fire-control system first —
  built to push smoke one way and hold a clear path the other, and
  the choice of which way has to be made in seconds.
ROOT: physics / hot gas rises and then runs along the ceiling of any
  enclosed space, so a long tube channels a fire's products straight
  at whoever is downwind of it.
THREAD: physics (buoyant flow in a confined space), building (fire
  safety as buying time rather than preventing fires), war (a
  decision taken under time pressure on partial information).
ASKED-AS: tunnel fire smoke ventilation escape trapped fans direction passages underground safety heat deaths

ESSENCE: keeping a city moving through snow and ice is a forecasting
  problem rather than a machinery one. Salt has to be spread before
  the ice forms, which means acting on a prediction that may be
  wrong — so the choice is between spending on a night that turns
  out mild and being late on a night that does not.
ROOT: chance / a preventive action has to be taken before the event
  is confirmed, so the decision is made under uncertainty and judged
  afterwards with certainty.
THREAD: weather (a forecast used as a decision rather than as
  information), government (spending judged in hindsight on a
  decision made in advance), maintenance (prevention costing less
  and looking like waste).
ASKED-AS: gritting salt roads ice snow forecast before treated slippery cleared council winter decision

ESSENCE: a utility paid per unit sold loses money when its customers
  use less, so the same company is asked to sell water or power and
  to persuade people to save it. The result is predictable:
  conservation programmes that stay small, and price rises after
  every successful one.
ROOT: economy / an organisation acts on the incentive it is paid by,
  and paying for volume buys volume.
THREAD: economy (an agent acting on its own incentives), government
  (regulation deciding what a monopoly is rewarded for), energy (a
  system where the cheapest unit is the one not used).
