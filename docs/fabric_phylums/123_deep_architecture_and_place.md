# 123 DEEP ARCHITECTURE AND PLACE — buildings and the spaces between
A building is an argument with weather, light, sound and people, and
the spaces between buildings are the same argument at a larger size.
These are the laws that hold whichever style is chosen, and they
decide the answer long before anybody picks a colour.

ESSENCE: before a building is a style or a plan it is a machine for
  making one climate inside out of a different one outside — holding
  heat in or keeping it out, admitting light but not too much, moving
  air, shedding water. Everything else is arranged on top of that job.
ROOT: physics / heat, light, air and water all move from more to less
  by themselves, so a shelter is a set of deliberate obstacles placed
  in the way of things that were going to happen anyway.
CANNOT: no building that does not answer its climate — it can only
  answer it well, badly, or by burning fuel to cover the difference.
  And no design that is right everywhere: the same drawing built in
  two climates is two machines, one of which is broken.
THREAD: the body (clothing chosen by weather), farming (a greenhouse
  is this device aimed at plants), the living world (a burrow, a
  termite mound, a nest with a lining).
ASKED-AS: house cold hot inside heating bills climate shelter comfortable building purpose design

ESSENCE: the sun comes from one half of the sky and never the other,
  and it rides high in summer and low in winter — so which way a
  building faces decides more about its warmth, its light and its
  running cost than any material chosen afterwards.
ROOT: astronomy / a tilted spinning planet gives every place a fixed
  band of sky the sun keeps to, so the geometry is fully known before
  the building is drawn and stays true for its whole life.
CANNOT: no fixing a bad orientation later — the walls do not turn. And
  no window that is right on every face: the same glass that is a free
  heater on one wall is a hole in the insulation on another, and a
  glare problem on a third.
THREAD: farming (which slope ripens first), the living world (moss on
  one side, dens dug to face the morning), navigation, sundials.
ASKED-AS: which way faces south north facing sunlight window room dark warm morning afternoon

ESSENCE: heavy walls and light walls solve different problems. Mass
  soaks up heat during the day and hands it back at night, which only
  helps where day and night differ sharply. Insulation simply slows
  heat leaving, which is what a long cold winter needs. Swap them and
  you have built the wrong building carefully.
ROOT: physics / storing heat and resisting its passage are two
  separate properties, and a material may have either, both, or
  neither of them.
CANNOT: no thick stone wall keeping a house warm through a northern
  winter — it stores heat and then gives it away outward just as
  willingly as inward. And no lightweight insulated box staying cool
  through a desert afternoon: nothing inside it can absorb the day.
THREAD: cooking (a heavy pot evens out a flame while a vacuum flask
  resists), deserts with thick adobe against forests with thin timber,
  caves, the body (fat stores, clothing resists).
ASKED-AS: thick walls stone insulation cold house desert adobe heat storage overnight temperature swing

ESSENCE: daylight is not one thing — it is a small fierce sun and an
  enormous soft sky, and the sky is the one that lights a room well.
  Good daylighting is mostly the business of getting sky onto a
  ceiling while keeping the sun itself out of people's eyes.
ROOT: light / a large dim source gives soft even light and a small
  bright one gives hard shadow and glare, and the sky and the sun are
  precisely those two sources arriving together.
CANNOT: no even daylight from one small opening — the wall beside it
  is bright and the far corner is a cave, and the eye judges by that
  contrast rather than by the amount. And no daylight without heat:
  both come through the same hole.
THREAD: photography (a softbox is a manufactured sky), painters
  choosing north-facing studios, the living world (a forest floor lit
  entirely by scattered light).
ASKED-AS: natural light room dark window skylight gloomy bright daylight lamps overcast sky

ESSENCE: a window lights a room to roughly twice its own head height
  and then gives up. Past that line the room is lit by lamps whatever
  the weather does, which is why a deep floor plate is an electric
  building by construction and no switch will change it.
ROOT: physics / light spreads and weakens with distance, and it enters
  through an opening of fixed size at a fixed height above the floor.
CANNOT: no daylighting the middle of a deep floor from its edges — the
  answers are a taller head, a second side, a rooflight, or a
  courtyard cut through the middle. And no adding one later without
  cutting a hole: depth is settled when the outline is drawn.
THREAD: old buildings being narrow for exactly this reason, the
  courtyard block, offices built before electric light, greenhouses.
ASKED-AS: deep room dark middle office lighting always windows narrow building courtyard atrium

ESSENCE: too much light in the wrong place is a worse fault than too
  little. The eye sets itself by the brightest thing in view, so a
  bright window at the end of a dim room turns everything else black,
  and the room reads as gloomy while being flooded with light.
ROOT: the mind / vision works on contrast rather than on quantity and
  adjusts to the brightest patch, not to the average.
CANNOT: no comfortable room with a bright source in view and nothing
  lit around it — the cure is lighting the surfaces beside the window,
  not adding lamps elsewhere. And no judging comfort by the amount of
  light: one figure is pleasant spread out and painful concentrated.
THREAD: driving into a low sun, a screen in a bright room, theatre
  lighting, photography and its fill light.
ASKED-AS: glare bright window screen squinting dark room blinds contrast eyes uncomfortable

ESSENCE: glass lets sunlight in and then will not let the heat back
  out, so every window is a small unregulated heater. On the sunny
  face in winter that is a gift; on the same face in summer, and on
  the afternoon side all year, it is the whole reason the building
  needs cooling at all.
ROOT: physics / short-wave sunlight passes through glass, warms what
  it lands on, and the warmed thing radiates back at a wavelength the
  glass will not pass.
CANNOT: no window admitting light and refusing heat — the two arrive
  together and can only be traded against each other. And no heavily
  glazed building in a hot climate that does not pay for its glass in
  cooling, every day, for as long as it stands.
THREAD: greenhouses, a car left in a car park, the living world
  (basking), the energy bill nobody connects to the view.
ASKED-AS: window heat sun summer overheating conservatory glass cooling blinds solar gain

ESSENCE: a shelf above a window stops a high summer sun and lets a low
  winter one straight in — but only on the face the midday sun
  crosses. At the ends of the day the sun comes in almost horizontally,
  and no overhang of any depth stops that.
ROOT: astronomy / the sun's height changes with the season on one side
  of the sky and is low at every sunrise and sunset everywhere.
CANNOT: no horizontal overhang shading a low sun — east and west glass
  needs upright fins, shutters, planting, or simply less glass. And no
  shading device that works without knowing the latitude: the same
  overhang is correct in one city and useless in another.
THREAD: hats with brims, farming (rows aligned to the sun), the living
  world (leaves angled against midday), awnings on old shopfronts.
ASKED-AS: overhang shade window awning summer winter angle west facing afternoon hot

ESSENCE: a blind inside the glass stops the light after the heat has
  already entered the room — the sun crossed the window and now the
  blind is a warm object indoors. Shading works outside the glass or
  it barely works at all.
ROOT: physics / heat that has got in must be carried out again,
  whereas heat turned away outside was never a problem, and glass
  makes the trip effectively one-way.
CANNOT: no internal blind matching an external shutter, whatever it is
  made of or coated with. And no retrofitting external shading at the
  price it would have cost at the start: it needs fixings, access and
  weatherproofing that a finished wall does not have.
THREAD: hot countries and their shutters, windscreen sunshades placed
  inside and working poorly, greenhouses whitewashed on the outside.
ASKED-AS: blinds curtains outside shutters awning heat internal reflective film hot room

ESSENCE: air is moved through a building by three things and no
  others — wind pushing one side and pulling another, warm air rising
  and drawing cool air in beneath it, or a fan. Every ventilation
  design ever built is one of those or a mixture, and each fails in
  its own weather.
ROOT: physics / air flows from higher pressure to lower, and pressure
  differences come from wind, from temperature, or from a machine.
CANNOT: no cross ventilation through one opening — air needs a way in
  and a way out, so a single window on one wall barely stirs a room.
  And no wind-driven scheme on a still day nor a stack-driven one when
  inside and outside match: both natural methods fail exactly when
  cooling is most wanted.
THREAD: chimneys, ships' wind scoops, the living world (termite mounds
  and burrows built around this), cooking (an extractor hood).
ASKED-AS: ventilation airflow stuffy open windows both sides fan draught stack fresh

ESSENCE: old buildings ventilated themselves through their gaps —
  badly, and at great expense in fuel. Sealing a building saves that
  heat and abolishes that accidental ventilation in the same stroke,
  so a tight building must be given air deliberately or the moisture
  and stale air have nowhere left to go.
ROOT: physics / the leaks that lost heat were also carrying away water
  vapour and cooking smells, so removing them removes two jobs when
  only one was intended.
CANNOT: no tight building without planned ventilation — the mould that
  appears after new windows go into an old house is this law arriving
  on schedule. And no judging airtightness by eye: it has to be
  pressure-tested, because the leaks are where nobody would look.
THREAD: fixing things (curing a symptom and relocating the problem),
  medicine (block one route and the fluid finds another), a sealed
  food container that sweats.
ASKED-AS: draughty new windows condensation mould sealed house ventilation trickle vents damp

ESSENCE: how warm a room feels is set by four things at once — the air
  temperature, the temperature of the surfaces around you, how much
  the air is moving, and how damp it is. A room at twenty degrees
  beside a cold window is cold; the same air over a warm floor is not.
ROOT: physics / a body loses heat by radiating to surfaces, by
  conducting into air, and by evaporating sweat — and the thermostat
  measures only the middle one of the three.
CANNOT: no comfort bought with air temperature alone — heating the air
  to make up for cold surfaces is expensive and still feels wrong. And
  no single number describing a room: two people in it are in
  genuinely different conditions depending on what each of them faces.
THREAD: the body (wind chill and humidity), a fire that warms one side
  of you, cellars that feel cold in August, underfloor heating.
ASKED-AS: room feels cold thermostat degrees draught window radiant floor humid heating comfortable

ESSENCE: a tall building is a chimney whether anybody wanted one or
  not. Warm inside air climbs the stairs and lift shafts and escapes
  at the top, hauling cold air in at the bottom — which is why the
  lobby doors of tall buildings fight you and the top floor is always
  the warm one.
ROOT: physics / warm air is lighter, so a column of it standing inside
  a colder column outside makes a pressure difference that grows with
  every storey of height.
CANNOT: no tall shaft without this pull. And no curing it at any one
  opening: the effect is driven by the whole height at once, so it is
  fought with lobbies, revolving doors and shafts kept closed, never
  by sealing the front door harder.
THREAD: chimneys, mine ventilation, fire (smoke climbing a shaft
  faster than anyone can walk), the living world (a burrow with two
  mouths at different heights).
ASKED-AS: tall building doors hard open lobby wind lift shaft floor warm smoke

ESSENCE: water almost never comes through the middle of a roof or a
  wall. It comes in at the changes — roof to wall, window to opening,
  the parapet, the chimney, the balcony, the pipe passing through. The
  material is rarely the problem; the joins between materials are.
ROOT: building / a joint is a deliberate interruption, and water needs
  only one continuous path to travel the entire way in.
CANNOT: no watertight building without continuous detail at every
  transition — a hundred metres of perfect roof and one bad upstand is
  a leaking roof. And no finding a leak where the stain is: water runs
  sideways along a surface before it drops, so the entry is nearly
  always somewhere else.
THREAD: making (things break where they were joined), clothing (a coat
  that leaks at the seams and the zip), plumbing (leaks at fittings
  and never in the middle of a pipe).
ASKED-AS: leak roof wall window flashing parapet chimney stain ceiling water entering

ESSENCE: the wall that lasts is not the one that keeps every drop out
  — it is the one built so that water which does get in runs down a
  gap and back out at the bottom. A good wall is a raincoat with a
  drain, never a sealed bag.
ROOT: building / no barrier is perfect, so the real question is not
  whether water arrives but where it goes and how quickly it leaves
  again.
CANNOT: no wall keeping water out forever by sealing alone — sealant
  ages, joints move, and on the day it fails a sealed wall has no
  second line at all. And no drained cavity working with its weep
  holes blocked: the drainage is the entire design, it is invisible,
  and it is the first thing filled in during a well-meaning repair.
THREAD: clothing (a breathable jacket with a lining), boat hulls given
  bilges rather than a promise, window frames drained to the outside.
ASKED-AS: cavity wall weep holes rain screen sealant caulk failed drainage water out

ESSENCE: air carries invisible water, and when it touches something
  cold enough that water turns into a drop. Inside a wall this happens
  at whatever layer sits at the wrong temperature — so a wall is
  designed to put the wet point where it can dry rather than where it
  can rot.
ROOT: physics / cold air holds less water than warm air, so cooling
  damp air far enough forces the water out onto whatever surface is
  there to receive it.
CANNOT: no wall without a dew point somewhere inside it in cold
  weather — it can be placed, never abolished. And no wall that is
  correct in two climates: vapour drives outward in a cold country and
  inward in a hot humid one, so the barrier that saves a house in one
  place quietly destroys it in the other.
THREAD: a cold drink sweating in summer, spectacles fogging indoors, a
  loft insulated on the wrong side, tents with two skins.
ASKED-AS: condensation inside wall vapour barrier dew point mould insulation wrong side rot

ESSENCE: heat leaves through whatever crosses the insulation — a steel
  beam, a concrete balcony, a metal window frame, a timber stud. These
  are a small share of the wall and can carry a large share of the
  loss, and they announce themselves as cold stripes where the mould
  grows first.
ROOT: physics / heat takes the easiest path available, and a
  conductive path through an insulating layer is enormously easier
  than everything around it.
CANNOT: no insulated building without breaking those paths — the
  insulating layer has to be continuous or it is a blanket with holes
  cut in it. And no concealing a thermal bridge: it draws its own
  location on the wall in condensation.
THREAD: a saucepan with a metal handle, gloves with a seam, the body
  (heat lost from ears and fingers), damp corners in a new flat.
ASKED-AS: cold spot mould corner balcony steel beam metal frame insulation gap condensation

ESSENCE: a building's price is not spread evenly over its area. The
  roof, the ground slab, and every corner, junction and change of
  plane cost far more per metre than a plain wall — so a compact
  simple shape is cheap and a picturesque one with many wings is
  expensive before a single material has been chosen.
ROOT: making / complexity is paid for in labour and in joints, and a
  corner is a joint that also needs its own detail, its own flashing,
  and its own hour of somebody's thinking.
CANNOT: no cheap building with a complicated outline — the plan's
  shape fixes the cost before any finish is specified. And no compact
  building from a sprawling plan: the outline and the budget are one
  decision made at two different moments.
THREAD: clothing (a plain coat against a tailored one), boats, the
  same surface-to-volume sum that also drives the heating bill, tents.
ASKED-AS: building cost roof corners shape complicated wings expensive simple box budget

ESSENCE: the frame holding a building up can be shown or covered, and
  both are honest choices with different bills. Shown, the structure
  must also be weatherproof, fireproof and worth looking at. Hidden,
  it needs a second set of surfaces and creates a void nobody will see
  into for a hundred years.
ROOT: building / a structural member has one job by necessity and
  picks up several more the moment it is left in view.
CANNOT: no expressed structure that is only structural — exposed steel
  wants fire protection and paint, exposed concrete wants a finish
  quality that cannot be patched. And no hidden structure that gets
  inspected: what is covered is trusted, and what is trusted is not
  looked at until something fails.
THREAD: ships (frames within, plating without), the body (a skeleton
  inside against an insect's shell outside), furniture, timber barns.
ASKED-AS: exposed beams concrete ceiling structure hidden plasterboard frame visible columns fireproofing

ESSENCE: nothing stays as it was delivered. Some materials age into
  something better — stone, brick, copper, oiled timber — and some
  only ever look worse than the day they went up. Choosing a material
  is choosing what it will look like in thirty years, and the sample
  board shows none of that.
ROOT: physics / disorder wins, so every surface is on its way
  somewhere; the only real choice is whether the destination is one
  anybody wants.
CANNOT: no material that stays new. And no honest verdict from a fresh
  sample — the question is what it looks like dirty, wet, faded, and
  streaked where the rain runs off, and that is answered only by
  finding an old one and going to look at it.
THREAD: making (quality judged off the surface a hand can reach),
  copper roofs turning green on purpose, painted render on a wet
  elevation, denim and leather.
ASKED-AS: weathering ageing stains streaks patina cladding faded looks old material sample

ESSENCE: the corridors, stairs and landings are not what is left over
  between the rooms — they are the frame everything else hangs from.
  Get the movement right and ordinary rooms work; get it wrong and no
  room is far enough from the traffic to be any good.
ROOT: people together / a building is a set of relationships between
  places, and the paths are what make those relationships real or
  impossible.
CANNOT: no good plan with bad circulation. And no circulation that is
  free — every metre of corridor is floor area bought, heated, cleaned
  and unusable, so the plan is a running argument between reaching
  everywhere and wasting nothing.
THREAD: towns (a street network deciding what its buildings can be),
  the body (vessels laid before organs), computing (the connections
  before the boxes), museums where the route is the exhibition.
ASKED-AS: layout corridor hallway stairs plan flow rooms circulation wasted space traffic

ESSENCE: every building has rooms people are meant to occupy and rooms
  that serve them — stairs, lifts, plant, ducts, lavatories, stores.
  Deciding early where the serving spaces gather is what stops them
  turning up later in the middle of the good rooms.
ROOT: building / services need continuous vertical runs and rooms need
  uninterrupted floor, and those two demands cannot stand on the same
  ground.
CANNOT: no building without service space — the only choice is whether
  it was planned or improvised. And no adding it afterwards without
  eating the rooms, which is where every boxed-in duct and dropped
  ceiling in an old building came from.
THREAD: ships (engine room and cabins), the body (organs and their
  plumbing), a kitchen's working side against its eating side,
  factories, backstage.
ASKED-AS: plant room ducts risers toilets storage service core layout hidden boxed pipes

ESSENCE: in any building of more than a couple of floors, the stairs,
  lifts and vertical pipes are the one thing that cannot move
  afterwards. Walls, uses, finishes, even the outside skin can all be
  changed — but where the core stands is permanent, and it decides
  what the building can ever be used for.
ROOT: building / vertical continuity is a geometric requirement, so a
  shaft is one decision taken on every floor simultaneously.
CANNOT: no relocating a core in a standing building at a sane price.
  And no long-life building with its core in the wrong place: every
  layout that position forbids stays forbidden for a century.
THREAD: trees (a trunk that cannot be re-rooted), computing (an early
  structure outliving every feature built on it), cities (a rail line
  laid once), the spine.
ASKED-AS: lift stairs core position building layout cannot move renovation conversion shaft

ESSENCE: the spacing of the columns is chosen for the structure and
  the size of the rooms is chosen for the people, and the two numbers
  rarely agree. Where they clash, either a room gets a column in an
  awkward place or the frame gets more expensive — and settling that
  argument is most of the plan of any large building.
ROOT: building / the cost of a beam climbs steeply with its span, so
  column-free floor is bought rather than assumed.
CANNOT: no long span without depth or expense — clear floor is always
  paid for somewhere, in beam depth, in storey height, or in steel.
  And no room grid independent of the structural grid: one bends to
  the other, and pretending otherwise produces the column beside the
  bed.
THREAD: car parks whose bay sizes set the whole frame, cathedrals,
  bridges, making (a design settling between parties who want
  different things).
ASKED-AS: columns spacing grid span open plan office layout beam depth structure

ESSENCE: the distance from one floor to the next is among the most
  consequential numbers in a building. It must hold the room, the
  beam, the ducts, the lights and the ceiling at once — and squeezing
  it saves money on every wall and every stair while making the rooms
  mean and the services impossible.
ROOT: building / several independent thicknesses are stacked in the
  same dimension, so each is competing with the others for the same
  few inches.
CANNOT: no generous room and shallow services inside a tight floor
  height. And no raising it later — this is the number that decides
  which future uses a building can accept, which is why low-ceilinged
  buildings are the ones nobody can convert.
THREAD: old warehouses becoming flats because they were tall, post-war
  offices that cannot become anything, ships' decks, packing a case.
ASKED-AS: ceiling height floor storey low rooms ducts services conversion warehouse loft

ESSENCE: buildings are measured against the body whether anyone
  intends it or not — a door, a step, a handrail, a sill, a bench are
  all sized by people, and they are the ruler an onlooker reads the
  whole building with. Take them away and a building loses its size.
ROOT: the mind / size is judged by comparison, and the comparison
  available to somebody standing in front of a building is themselves.
CANNOT: no sense of scale without something of known size in view,
  which is why a glass wall with no visible floors can look small or
  enormous with equal ease. And no building that reads well close up
  and from far away on one set of proportions: scale has to be built
  in at more than one size.
THREAD: photographs with a person included for scale, film sets and
  models, mountains that look near in clear air, typography.
ASKED-AS: scale proportion building looks huge doors windows human size monumental feels

ESSENCE: the good places in a building are often the in-between ones —
  porches, landings, window seats, the widening at the top of a stair.
  A move from outside to inside, or from public to private, works
  better as a short sequence than as a single door.
ROOT: the mind / attention needs a moment to change its setting, and a
  threshold is that moment given a floor and a roof.
CANNOT: no abrupt transition that feels right — a front door opening
  straight into a sitting room makes both worse, and the cure is a
  space rather than a curtain. And no threshold without a difference:
  it works by marking a change, so between two identical conditions it
  is only a wasted metre.
THREAD: ritual (a ceremony marking a change of state), story (a scene
  needing an entrance), clothing (the coat taken off at the door),
  Japanese houses built around this one idea.
ASKED-AS: porch entrance hallway front door straight into threshold arrival between inside outside

ESSENCE: rooms sit on a ladder from most public to most private, and
  people are unhappy when the ladder is broken — a bedroom off a
  sitting room, a lavatory opening onto the dining table, a bathroom
  reached through a study. Each room has to be as private as its use.
ROOT: people together / privacy is control over who may see and hear
  you, and a plan grants or refuses that control by where it puts its
  doors.
CANNOT: no private room reached through a public one — the privacy is
  broken by the journey, not by the door at the end of it. And no
  curing it with decoration: the fault is in the sequence, and only a
  changed sequence repairs it.
THREAD: hotels, the living world (a den with a narrowing entrance),
  people and power (who is admitted how far in), open-plan offices
  with no quiet room anywhere.
ASKED-AS: bedroom off living room bathroom through privacy layout awkward guests door sequence

ESSENCE: a room labelled on a drawing is a guess. People eat in
  kitchens, work in bedrooms, store things in dining rooms, and use
  the front room twice a year. The plans that survive are the ones
  whose rooms are the right size and shape for several uses, not the
  ones whose labels turned out correct.
ROOT: people together / behaviour follows convenience, and no drawing
  binds what anybody does on a wet Tuesday afternoon.
CANNOT: no room used only as named. And no highly specific room
  keeping its purpose for a building's life — the tighter the fit, the
  sooner it becomes a store, so specialisation in a plan is always a
  short-dated bet.
THREAD: teaching (the lesson as taught against the lesson as planned),
  software (features nobody uses beside workarounds everybody does),
  gardens with a worn track across the lawn.
ASKED-AS: dining room never used kitchen table home office spare bedroom storage labels actual

ESSENCE: where the path people want and the path the plan offers
  differ, the people win. They wear a track across the grass, prop the
  fire door, put a desk in the corridor, and come in through the back.
  A plan that ignores how people move does not fail loudly — it gets
  quietly overwritten.
ROOT: premise — behaviour follows effort, and the shortest convenient
  route beats any instruction, permanently and without argument.
CANNOT: no design that changes movement by asking. And no reading a
  building's real plan off its drawings once it has been used for a
  year: the worn carpet, the propped doors and the moved furniture are
  the true document.
THREAD: desire lines worn into parks, signs added exactly where a
  design failed, computing (users defeating a workflow), people (rules
  kept and reasons lost).
ASKED-AS: shortcut worn path grass propped door back entrance ignored signs people walk

ESSENCE: people find their way by landmarks, daylight, and a view of
  where they are going — not by signs. A building you can understand
  from inside needs very few; one covered in them is confessing that
  the plan does not explain itself.
ROOT: the mind / a place is remembered as a picture and a sequence, so
  something visible and distinct at each decision point is what memory
  can actually hold on to.
CANNOT: no signage rescuing a plan that never shows you the
  destination. And no wayfinding in a building where every corridor
  looks the same: identical is unrememberable, however carefully it is
  labelled.
THREAD: navigation (steering by a headland), hospitals with coloured
  routes, airports arranged so you can see the aircraft, multi-storey
  car parks with numbered levels nobody recalls.
ASKED-AS: lost building signs confusing corridors same hospital wayfinding landmark exit find

ESSENCE: a stair's comfort is set by the relation between the height
  of each step and the depth of its tread, and the human range is
  narrow. Get it wrong by an inch and everybody stumbles forever, and
  it cannot be repaired without rebuilding the entire flight.
ROOT: the body / walking is a rhythm of a fixed stride and climbing
  borrows that rhythm, so the geometry that suits a leg is not open to
  negotiation.
CANNOT: no comfortable stair outside the ratio. And no correcting one
  step in a flight — a single riser of a different height is the one
  people fall on, because the foot has already learned all the others.
  And no stair fitted into a space too short for it: the run is
  dictated by the height it climbs.
THREAD: the body and its gait, ladders, ramps governed by the same
  fixed geometry, ergonomics in general.
ASKED-AS: stairs steep steps trip riser tread height uncomfortable staircase space fall

ESSENCE: a ramp is not a fitting, it is a length. A rise of one metre
  at a usable slope needs twelve metres of run plus landings — so
  step-free access is a decision made when the plan is first drawn,
  and bolted on later it eats the front garden.
ROOT: mathematics / a slope is a ratio, so the height and the length
  are one fact, and the acceptable ratio is set by what an arm can
  push.
CANNOT: no step-free entry without the room for it. And no accessible
  building from a ramp alone: door widths, the lift, turning circles,
  the lavatory and level thresholds are one system, and it is only as
  good as its worst link.
THREAD: mountain roads and their hairpins, canals and their locks,
  engineering (a chain and its weakest link), prams and delivery
  trolleys using the same design nobody counted.
ASKED-AS: wheelchair ramp slope steps entrance access lift door width threshold gradient

ESSENCE: fire safety is not about preventing fires, it is about buying
  minutes — enough for everyone to get out and for help to arrive.
  That is where two ways out from everywhere comes from, and walls
  that hold a fire in one room for an hour, and stairs kept clear of
  smoke.
ROOT: chance / the event cannot be reliably prevented, so the design
  aims at the consequence instead of at the cause.
CANNOT: no single escape route that is safe — one way out means the
  fire has only one place to block. And no fire resistance without
  continuity: a fire wall with an unsealed pipe through it is not a
  fire wall, and a self-closing door propped open is just a corridor.
THREAD: ships (bulkheads), aircraft (exit rows and the ninety-second
  rule), medicine (triage buying time), people and power (every clause
  written after the disaster that produced it).
