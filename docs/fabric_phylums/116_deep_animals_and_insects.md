# 116 DEEP ANIMALS AND INSECTS — how creatures make a living
Every animal is a solution to the same short list of problems:
get fuel, avoid becoming fuel, hold a temperature, and leave
copies. The bodies differ because the solutions do.

ESSENCE: an animal's symmetry announces how it feeds. A creature
  that meets food from every direction is built round a centre —
  anemone, jellyfish, starfish — and has no front at all. A
  creature that goes somewhere has a front, and the front collects
  the senses, and the brain follows the senses.
ROOT: physics / a body that travels meets its world at one end, so
  that end is where detection pays for itself.
CANNOT: no radial animal chasing anything down — going somewhere
  requires a forward, and a body with no forward has no chase in
  it. And no brain anywhere but the leading end in a traveller:
  the sense organs go where the news arrives and the processing
  goes with them, over and over, in unrelated lineages.
THREAD: engineering (a vehicle's controls at the leading end),
  building (a shop door placed where the traffic already is).
ASKED-AS: jellyfish starfish symmetry body plan head front brain animals shape radial worms

ESSENCE: a great many animals are one unit repeated — a worm's
  rings, an insect's sections, your own vertebrae and ribs.
  Repetition is cheap to grow and cheap to alter, because a single
  segment can be specialised for one job without redesigning any
  of the others: a leg here, a jaw there, a wing on two of them.
ROOT: heredity / one instruction used many times is far cheaper
  than many instructions, and a repeat can be modified singly.
CANNOT: no cheap novelty without spare copies to experiment on —
  an animal with one of a thing cannot alter it and keep using it.
  And no claw, jaw or feeler out of nowhere: every specialised
  appendage is a modified leg, so the entire toolkit came out of
  the repetition and nothing came from scratch.
THREAD: making things (a standard bracket bolted everywhere then
  varied), language (one repeated frame taking different words).
ASKED-AS: worms segments insects body parts repeated legs claws antennae modified spine vertebrae

ESSENCE: a gut with a mouth at one end and an exit at the other
  lets an animal run a production line — grinding at one station,
  dissolving at the next, absorbing at a third, all at once and
  all on different mouthfuls. A sac with a single opening must do
  everything in turn and cannot eat while it empties.
ROOT: engineering / specialised stations in series beat one
  station doing everything, once there is throughput to fill them.
CANNOT: no continuous feeding through one opening, so an anemone
  shuts down between meals by construction. And no divided
  chemistry in a sac: an acid stage and a neutral stage cannot
  occupy the same room at the same time, which caps how difficult
  a food such an animal can attempt.
THREAD: making things (an assembly line against a single bench),
  cooking (a kitchen with stations against one pot on the stove).
ASKED-AS: gut digestion one way mouth anus jellyfish stomach food factory stages continuous eating

ESSENCE: a skeleton is either a frame you hang meat on or a box
  you live inside, and the choice settles nearly everything. The
  box protects, seals water in, and gives muscles a hard anchor at
  the surface. The frame grows continuously and never has to be
  abandoned. Nothing does both.
ROOT: engineering / a shell carries its load in its skin while a
  frame carries it in a core, and the two grow in opposite ways.
CANNOT: no enlarging a box — a solid case can only be replaced,
  never extended. And no armour on a frame without building a
  second skeleton for it: a turtle and an armadillo had to grow
  one outside to get what a beetle is issued with at hatching, and
  they pay for it in weight the beetle never carries.
THREAD: building (a load-bearing wall against a steel frame),
  packaging (a rigid case that protects and cannot be enlarged).
ASKED-AS: bones shell crab insect skeleton inside outside armour protection frame difference beetle vertebrate

ESSENCE: an exoskeleton fails on the way up for three reasons at
  once. Its weight climbs faster than its strength, so it must
  thicken out of proportion. The animal breathes through passive
  air tubes that only work over short distances. And every growth
  step means a day of being naked and soft. Any one would cap the
  size; together they cap it hard.
ROOT: mathematics / area grows as the square while volume grows as
  the cube, and passive gas movement fails across distance.
CANNOT: no dog-sized insect in this planet's air — the tubes
  cannot supply the middle of a body that large, whatever else is
  arranged. And no giant that is both armoured and moulting: the
  bigger the case, the longer and more lethal the soft interval
  after each replacement.
THREAD: building (walls that must thicken faster than height
  rises), engineering (a design that works at one size only).
ASKED-AS: giant insects why not huge spiders movies size limit breathing tubes oxygen shell heavy

ESSENCE: growing inside a box means shedding the box. The animal
  splits its old case, drags itself out — including the linings of
  its own breathing tubes — swells with air or water before the
  new one hardens, then waits hours or days as something pale,
  soft and unable to run. Most predation happens in that window.
ROOT: physics / a rigid container cannot be enlarged, so growth
  has to be a run of replacements instead of a continuous process.
CANNOT: no growth between moults, so growth arrives in jumps and
  an animal's size records how many it has survived. And no
  defending itself while soft: the weapons are made of the same
  case, so a crab's claws are useless at precisely the moment it
  most needs them.
THREAD: making things (a factory shut for retooling), building (a
  shop closed during its own refit and earning nothing).
ASKED-AS: moulting shedding skin crab soft shell insect growing stages hiding vulnerable hardening cast

ESSENCE: a caterpillar and a butterfly are one animal doing two
  unrelated jobs — one eats leaves and does nothing else, the
  other drinks, travels and mates. Splitting the life means the
  young never compete with the adults for anything, and each body
  is built for one task instead of compromising between two.
ROOT: the living world / a body optimised for two jobs is worse at
  both, and dividing the life into stages escapes that trade.
CANNOT: no growth after the final change — an adult insect is the
  size it will always be, so a small fly is not a young one and
  never becomes a bigger one. And no feeding at all for some
  adults: several kinds emerge with no working mouthparts and live
  a few days entirely on what the larva banked.
THREAD: engineering (two machines beating one that switches
  modes), work (an apprenticeship resembling nothing of the trade).
ASKED-AS: caterpillar butterfly larva grub maggot changes pupa small flies young adults competing stages

ESSENCE: some insects hatch as miniatures and simply enlarge at
  each moult — grasshoppers, bugs, dragonflies. Others dissolve
  themselves almost entirely inside a case and rebuild from the
  material. The rebuild is expensive and defenceless, and the
  groups that pay for it are the ones that took over the world.
ROOT: the living world / a costly step buying a wholly different
  adult body pays wherever the two ways of life differ enough.
CANNOT: no radical redesign without a helpless interval, because a
  body cannot be dismantled and used at the same time. And no
  nymph living where its adult could not: a partial change keeps
  the two forms similar, so the young stay inside the adult's
  world and inside its competition.
THREAD: building (renovating while occupied against gutting and
  rebuilding), teaching (improving a method against starting over).
ASKED-AS: dragonfly nymph grasshopper young looks like adult cocoon chrysalis rebuild inside complete change

ESSENCE: the reason most animal species are insects comes down to
  four things standing together — small enough that a crumb is a
  meal and a crack is a house, waxed against drying, able to fly,
  and able to turn one summer into several generations. Remove any
  one and the group would be unremarkable.
ROOT: mathematics / advantages that multiply rather than add
  produce a difference of kind instead of a difference of degree.
CANNOT: no small land animal at all without waterproofing — at
  that size the surface would dry it out within an afternoon, so
  the wax had to come first and everything else after. And no
  insect dominance at sea: the group that owns the land is a bit
  player in salt water, where the crustaceans were already in.
THREAD: money (four small edges compounding into a monopoly), the
  living world (numbers dwarfing the variety of ideas behind them).
ASKED-AS: insects most species why many beetles successful small flying numbers waterproof generations dominate land

ESSENCE: flight is the dearest way to travel per minute and the
  cheapest per mile, and it buys three things nothing else does:
  escape upward, a search area a hundred times larger, and access
  to food and mates in patches too scattered to walk between.
  Where those stop paying it is dropped, fast and repeatedly.
ROOT: physics / staying up costs continuously while covering
  ground costs little more, so the sums depend entirely on distance.
CANNOT: no flying animal that is also heavily armoured or heavy
  with young — every gram is charged on every trip, which is why
  birds have hollow bones and lay rather than carry. And no flight
  retained where there is nothing to flee and nowhere to go:
  island birds and island insects lose it again and again.
THREAD: transport (an aircraft's economics on short and long
  hauls), money (a high fixed cost that only pays at volume).
ASKED-AS: flying birds insects energy expensive wings lost flightless island eggs light bones cost

ESSENCE: an ant nest makes more sense as one animal than as
  thousands. The queen is an ovary rather than a ruler, the
  workers are its hands and its gut, and what succeeds or fails is
  the colony and never the ant. A worker's death is a scratch, not
  a bereavement.
ROOT: the living world / what counts is copies passed on, and in
  these species reproduction happens at colony level and nowhere
  else at all.
CANNOT: no worker leaving to found her own nest — she has no route
  to offspring, so her loss costs the colony almost nothing, which
  is exactly what makes suicidal defence affordable. And no orders
  given: there is no ant anywhere in the nest that knows what the
  nest is doing.
THREAD: people together (an institution outliving every member),
  the body (a cell dying to nobody's grief but its own body's).
ASKED-AS: ants queen colony workers ruler orders nest one organism sacrifice sterile bees hive

ESSENCE: in a bee colony the same individual does different work
  at different ages — cleaning, then nursing, then building, then
  guarding, and finally foraging outside. The dangerous job comes
  last, to the workers with the least life left to lose, which is
  precisely how anyone would schedule it if anyone were
  scheduling.
ROOT: money / spend the asset with the least remaining value on
  the riskiest task, and the arithmetic comes out by itself.
CANNOT: no promotion by merit — it is a timetable and not a
  career, though it can be overridden when a colony loses a whole
  age class at once and has to backfill. And no experienced worker
  kept safe indoors: the schedule sends its most practised bees to
  the job most likely to kill them.
THREAD: money (writing an asset down before risking it), work (a
  rotation whose sequence carries the whole logic).
ASKED-AS: bees jobs workers nurse forager guard age changes roles hive tasks divided older

ESSENCE: a swarm choosing a new home has no chooser. Scouts visit
  sites and advertise them with an enthusiasm matching what they
  found, others go and inspect the better-advertised ones, and
  when enough scouts are standing at one site the swarm goes. It
  is a vote counted by attendance, and it reliably picks well.
ROOT: mathematics / independent samples pooled at a threshold beat
  any single sampler, provided nobody can shout down the count.
CANNOT: no such quality if the scouts copied each other's opinions
  instead of going to look — the accuracy comes from independent
  inspections, and imitation would destroy it. And no leader to
  persuade, so no way to steer a colony by convincing anybody of
  anything.
THREAD: people together (a good decision from a crowd that never
  conferred), evidence (independence as the source of accuracy).
ASKED-AS: bees swarm choose nest site dance scouts vote quorum decision leader group best

ESSENCE: a wasp that stings once and dies is doing arithmetic
  rather than bravery. A worker leaves no offspring of her own, so
  her whole value is what she does for the nest, and spending her
  life to save it is a straight profit. That is why social insects
  fight so far above their weight.
ROOT: the living world / a body with no reproductive future is a
  cheap asset, and cheap assets can be spent outright.
CANNOT: no suicidal defence by a solitary animal — its own
  survival IS its reproduction, so the identical act is a total
  loss. And no colony defence without something fixed to defend:
  the sums need a nest full of young in one place, which is why
  wandering species never fight this way.
THREAD: war (the economics of who is asked to hold a position),
  money (writing off an asset to protect the whole balance sheet).
ASKED-AS: bee sting dies wasps attack nest defend suicide soldiers ants termites swarm protect

ESSENCE: pollen only works if it lands on the same kind of flower
  it came from, and what makes that likely is a habit in the
  insect: a foraging bee sticks to one species for an entire trip
  even when better flowers stand open beside it. The plant is
  buying loyalty and not visits.
ROOT: evidence / a delivery service is worth paying for only if it
  delivers to the right address, and volume without accuracy is
  worth nothing.
CANNOT: no pollination from a visitor that samples everything —
  moving between species carries pollen to the wrong doors and the
  plant gets no return at all. And no loyalty for free: it costs
  the bee every better flower it walks past, and the plant is
  charged for that in sugar.
THREAD: money (paying a premium for reliability over price), craft
  (a specialist's speed coming from not switching tools).
ASKED-AS: bees one flower type stick loyal pollen wasted species visiting nectar reward foraging

ESSENCE: a flower pays in two currencies because it is buying two
  things. Nectar is sugar water, fuel for an adult's flight, and
  it buys a visit. Pollen is protein, the food of the young, and
  it buys a household that keeps coming back all season. A plant
  offering only one gets a completely different sort of visitor.
ROOT: the living world / an animal's fuel budget and its building
  budget are separate accounts, so a bribe must match the account.
CANNOT: no rearing young on sugar — it cannot build a body, so a
  nectar-only flower recruits passers-by instead of a workforce.
  And no free pollen either: it is the plant's own cargo, so every
  grain eaten is a grain not delivered, and the plant is paying
  with the very goods it wanted shipped.
THREAD: farming (feed for work against feed for growth), money
  (wages and capital coming out of one till).
ASKED-AS: nectar pollen larvae fed sugar protein flowers reward collect baskets grains young hive

ESSENCE: eyes at the front overlap their views and judge distance
  precisely, which is what landing a strike requires. Eyes at the
  sides see nearly all the way round and give up depth, which is
  what spotting an approach requires. Pick up a skull and you have
  read which of the two the animal was doing.
ROOT: light + seeing / depth from two eyes needs overlap, and
  overlap is bought by surrendering field of view.
CANNOT: no animal with both arrangements — they are mutually
  exclusive, so every species has already chosen. And no reading
  it as intelligence or ferocity: an owl's forward stare and a
  rabbit's sideways one are the same trade solved in opposite
  directions by the same physics.
THREAD: engineering (a camera's zoom against its coverage),
  attention (narrow focus and wide watch as one conflict).
ASKED-AS: eyes front side predator prey rabbit owl cat vision depth judge skull hunter

ESSENCE: a hunter must choose between waiting and chasing, and the
  energy budget decides. Waiting costs almost nothing and needs
  one accurate strike, so it favours stillness, camouflage and
  venom. Chasing costs enormously and needs stamina and cooling,
  so it favours long legs, big lungs, and the sense to abandon a
  run that is going badly.
ROOT: physics / energy spent has to be recovered from the kill, so
  a method's cost sets how often it is allowed to fail.
CANNOT: no ambush hunter that also runs prey down — the bodies are
  built oppositely, and a cheetah cannot lie in wait for weeks the
  way a spider does. And no pursuit hunter pressing every chase: a
  predator that will not quit a failing run starves with a full
  set of talents.
THREAD: money (a cheap strategy tolerating many misses), sport (a
  sprinter's race against a distance runner's).
ASKED-AS: predator hunting ambush chase cheetah spider crocodile wait energy stamina giving up strike

ESSENCE: an animal cannot watch and eat at the same time, so
  safety is paid for in mouthfuls. Every head lifted is a bite not
  taken — which is why hungry animals take more risks, why a
  grazer in a group looks up less often than one alone, and why a
  predator's real bill is counted in lost feeding, not in deaths.
ROOT: attention / one pair of eyes cannot be on the ground and the
  horizon at once, so vigilance and feeding trade directly.
CANNOT: no free watchfulness — a herd that must stay alert eats
  less and grows more slowly, so the cost arrives as thinner
  animals rather than as bodies. And no reading danger from how
  relaxed a group looks: the calm ones are usually the ones large
  enough to spread the watching thinly.
THREAD: work (attention divided between a task and interruption),
  money (a premium paid continuously out of output).
ASKED-AS: grazing animals looking up watching danger feeding time hungry risk herd alone vigilance

ESSENCE: a herd buys three separate things — more eyes on watch,
  worse odds of being the individual taken, and a moving mass that
  spoils a hunter's aim. It costs three: you are far easier to
  find, you compete with your own neighbours for food, and disease
  and parasites move between you unhindered.
ROOT: mathematics / risk shared among many falls for each, while
  detection and infection both rise with the size of the target.
CANNOT: no herd that is not also a market — the same crowd
  shielding you is eating your grass. And no equal safety inside
  it: the animals on the outside carry the risk for everybody in
  the middle, which is exactly why the middle is contested and why
  a frightened group tightens rather than scatters.
THREAD: money (a pool that lowers individual risk and creates a
  shared one), cities (the same crowding trade at human scale).
ASKED-AS: herd flock school safety numbers predators eyes watching crowd disease competing middle edge

ESSENCE: venom is delivered and poison is eaten, and that one
  difference sets everything else. Venom needs a needle, is
  expensive to manufacture, is metered out carefully, and is
  usually advertised — because an attacker warned off costs
  nothing. Poison needs no delivery and only works after the
  animal has already been bitten.
ROOT: chemistry / a substance that must reach the blood needs a
  route in, while one that acts from the gut needs only swallowing.
CANNOT: no poisonous animal defending itself from a first bite —
  the lesson is learned by the predator and paid for by the
  individual, so the defence protects the kind and not the
  specimen. And no free venom: an animal that has spent its dose
  is disarmed for days and knows it.
THREAD: law (a deterrent that works only after harm is done),
  money (an expensive weapon rationed because it is expensive).
ASKED-AS: venom poison difference snake frog bite eat toxic dangerous spider sting warning colours

ESSENCE: venom for hunting and venom for defence do different work
  and feel different. Hunting venom has to stop a meal running, so
  it paralyses and begins digesting, often with little pain.
  Defensive venom has to teach something too big to eat, so it is
  built for immediate agony and frequently does no lasting harm.
ROOT: the living world / a chemical is selected for the effect
  needed, and stopping prey and deterring a bear are unrelated
  effects with unrelated recipes.
CANNOT: no reading danger from pain — the stings that hurt worst
  are often the least harmful and the bites that kill can be
  almost painless going in. And no single antidote across species:
  each venom is a mixture shaped by one animal's own problem, so
  the treatment has to match the animal.
THREAD: medicine (severity of symptom as a poor guide to
  seriousness), law (a penalty meant to deter against one to stop).
ASKED-AS: sting hurts bee wasp scorpion snake bite painful deadly harmless venom defence hunting

ESSENCE: there are two entirely different ways to be missed. One
  is not being detected, which is the business of matching a
  background. The other is being seen perfectly and dismissed — a
  caterpillar that is a twig, a moth that is a bird dropping, a
  fish that is a dead leaf. That one beats recognition, in plain
  view.
ROOT: the mind / seeing runs a detection stage and an
  identification stage, and a disguise can be aimed at either.
CANNOT: no masquerade away from the thing imitated — a twig
  impostor on a bare wall is just an odd object, so this disguise
  has to carry its setting with it. And no masquerading as
  something edible: the model must be an item the predator has
  already learned to ignore, which is why the models are always
  litter, rubbish and inedible scraps.
THREAD: image (a logo built to be recognised, this craft
  inverted), war (hiding a vehicle against dressing it as a barn).
ASKED-AS: stick insect leaf looks like twig bird dropping disguise pretending object seen ignored

ESSENCE: bright warning colours work only on a predator that can
  learn, can remember, and survived the lesson. So the whole
  arrangement runs on individuals being attacked: the pattern's
  value is built out of the deaths of its own wearers, and it
  collapses wherever the local predators are short-lived or newly
  arrived.
ROOT: the living world / a signal means something only where its
  meaning has been taught, and the teaching costs the teacher.
CANNOT: no warning colour protecting anything among predators that
  are always naive — the lesson has to have been paid for locally
  and recently, or the colour is merely conspicuous. And no
  benefit to the individual that gives the lesson: it is the
  survivors of its own kind that collect on it.
THREAD: law (deterrence requiring enforcement that harms someone),
  teaching (a rule learned only by those who saw it applied).
ASKED-AS: bright colours warning wasp frog poison predators learn avoid pattern taught birds memory

ESSENCE: knowing which way is north is not knowing where you are,
  and animals carry both instruments. A sun compass corrected
  against an internal clock, a star pattern learned as a chick,
  and a magnetic sense supply the heading; smells, coastlines and
  remembered ground supply the position. Break one and the animal
  travels confidently in the wrong direction.
ROOT: navigation / a heading and a position are separate pieces of
  information needing separate instruments to obtain.
CANNOT: no sun compass without a working clock — shift a bird's
  day by six hours and it turns ninety degrees off, which is how
  the mechanism was proved. And no position sense in a first-year
  bird of many species: the young hold a fixed heading for a fixed
  time, and only experience turns that into knowing where it is.
THREAD: navigation (a compass and a chart being two purchases),
  the mind (direction and location as different kinds of memory).
ASKED-AS: birds navigate migration stars sun magnetic compass lost young first time route direction

ESSENCE: a small bird crossing an ocean does it by turning itself
  into a fuel tank — doubling its weight in fat, and shrinking its
  own gut, liver and unused muscle beforehand so as not to carry
  what it will not need. It arrives with organs to rebuild, which
  is why the stops on the way are repairs and not rests.
ROOT: physics / range is fuel divided by consumption, so every
  gram that is not fuel is a subtraction from the distance.
CANNOT: no long crossing without somewhere to fuel first — the fat
  is laid on at particular sites over weeks, so a stopover is part
  of the machine and not a convenience that can be moved. And no
  eating properly on arrival: a bird that lands with a shrunken
  gut needs days to rebuild it, and is at its most fragile then.
THREAD: flight (payload traded against range on any aircraft),
  money (borrowing against the body to fund a journey).
ASKED-AS: birds migration fat fuel weight doubling stopover feeding ocean crossing long distance exhausted

ESSENCE: a hibernating ground squirrel is not asleep. It lets its
  body fall to a few degrees above freezing and its fuel use drops
  to a fiftieth — because warmth is the expense, so a small animal
  facing a winter with no food survives by switching the heating
  off rather than by finding more fuel.
ROOT: chemistry / reaction rates fall steeply as temperature
  drops, so a cold body costs almost nothing to keep running.
CANNOT: no hibernation without a safe insulated place — an animal
  at four degrees cannot flee anything, so the burrow is a
  requirement rather than a comfort. And no unbroken winter
  either: hibernators rewarm every week or two at enormous cost,
  and why they must remains unsettled, so the cheap state is not
  entirely cheap.
THREAD: engineering (shutting a plant down rather than idling it),
  money (cutting fixed costs instead of chasing revenue).
ASKED-AS: hibernation squirrels winter sleep body temperature cold slow heart wake burrow fuel

ESSENCE: a bear in winter drops only a few degrees, stays alert
  enough to rouse, and gives birth and nurses in the den. It is a
  large animal, so it loses heat slowly and can carry months of
  fat — which buys it a different arrangement altogether: costly,
  shallow, and reversible on any afternoon that demands it.
ROOT: mathematics / a big body's surface is small against its
  bulk, so its heating bill per kilogram stays low enough to pay.
CANNOT: no deep hibernation in a large animal — rewarming a body
  that size would cost more than the saving, so the deep state
  belongs to small creatures only. And no quick waking for a true
  hibernator: a squirrel needs an hour to come back, and that hour
  is exactly what a bear is refusing to give up.
THREAD: engineering (idling a big plant against cold-starting it),
  money (paying a fee to keep an option open).
ASKED-AS: bears hibernate really den cubs born wake up deep true difference alert rousing

ESSENCE: a hummingbird would starve overnight. It is small enough
  that a night's heating costs more than a whole day's storage, so
  it shuts down every evening, falling to near air temperature and
  restarting at dawn. The smaller a warm animal is, the closer it
  lives to that edge, every single day of its life.
ROOT: mathematics / heat loss follows surface while fuel storage
  follows volume, so smaller means a worse ratio every hour.
CANNOT: no small warm-blooded animal going long without food — a
  shrew dies within hours, which is why nothing warm-blooded is
  much smaller than a couple of grams. And no torpor without a
  price: a torpid animal is defenceless and slow to restart, so it
  is a nightly gamble rather than a free saving.
THREAD: money (a business running with no cash buffer at all), the
  body (a reserve that has to be sized to the burn rate).
ASKED-AS: hummingbird overnight torpor bats small animals starve hours shrew morning warming energy

ESSENCE: staying warm costs roughly ten times the food and buys
  three things: working at night, working in the cold, and never
  waiting to warm up before you can move. A lizard eats a tenth as
  much and pays in mornings spent on a rock, and in a world it
  must hand over to the warm-blooded after dark.
ROOT: physics / holding a temperature above the surroundings costs
  continuously, and the bill is paid whether anything is being
  done or not.
CANNOT: no cold-blooded animal active on a cold night, which is
  why the far north has almost no reptiles at all. And no
  warm-blooded animal surviving a famine the way a snake does: a
  snake can go a year between meals, and the price of the quick
  responsive body is that it has to be fed constantly.
THREAD: money (fixed costs bought in exchange for capacity),
  engineering (a machine kept hot for instant response).
ASKED-AS: warm blooded cold reptiles lizards food eat less basking sun night active mammals

ESSENCE: a large reptile barely cools overnight because its bulk
  holds the day's heat — so size alone can deliver a steady
  temperature with none of the machinery and none of the food
  bill. Bulk and burning are alternative answers to one problem,
  which is why the biggest cold-blooded animals live where it is
  warm.
ROOT: mathematics / heat lost depends on surface while heat stored
  depends on volume, so bulk buys thermal steadiness for nothing.
CANNOT: no small animal getting steadiness this way — below a
  certain size the day's heat is gone within the hour, so small
  creatures must either burn fuel or accept the swings. And no
  large reptile in a cold country: bulk slows cooling and
  generates nothing, so a cool season defeats it regardless.
THREAD: building (a thick stone wall evening out the day),
  cooking (a big joint holding heat long after the oven is off).
ASKED-AS: crocodile large reptiles temperature steady overnight size bulk warm climate blooded heat

ESSENCE: shedding heat is harder than making it, and every large
  animal in a warm place is built around that. An elephant's ears
  are radiators laced with vessels, a dog pants because it cannot
  sweat through fur, a jackrabbit does the whole job with its
  ears. Overheating, not cold, is what kills a working animal.
ROOT: physics / heat produced follows body mass while heat lost
  follows surface, so cooling gets harder as the animal gets bigger.
CANNOT: no big furred animal working hard in heat without a
  dedicated radiator — the coat that saves it at night traps it by
  day. And no cooling by panting in humid air: evaporation is the
  entire mechanism, and humidity switches it off, which is why
  damp heat kills where dry heat merely exhausts.
THREAD: engineering (a cooling system sizing the whole design),
  the body (heatstroke as a failure of shedding, not of making).
ASKED-AS: elephant ears dogs panting sweating overheating heat animals cooling humid fur shade radiator

ESSENCE: how an animal eats matters more than what it is related
  to. Filterers, grazers, browsers, seed-crackers, nectar-drinkers,
  flesh-tearers and scavengers each have a toolkit forced on them
  by the food — so unrelated animals doing one job converge on the
  same jaws, the same guts and often the same working hours.
ROOT: physics / a task with narrow physical requirements forces
  the same equipment on everyone who attempts it.
CANNOT: no guessing an animal's family from its feeding gear — the
