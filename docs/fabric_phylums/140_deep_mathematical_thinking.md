# 140 DEEP MATHEMATICAL THINKING — how mathematics is actually done
Not the results but the working habits: what counts as settled, the
handful of moves that solve most problems, what a definition is for, and
why the subject grows by asking what would happen if one of its own
rules were quietly dropped.

ESSENCE: an example shows a thing can happen. A conjecture says it always
  happens. A proof settles it. These are three different objects with
  three different weights, and confusing them is the most common failure
  in the subject and outside it.
ROOT: mathematics / proof is chained certainty — the other two are
  evidence, and evidence is not the same currency.
CANNOT: no promoting a conjecture by piling up examples, however many. And
  no demoting one either — a conjecture unproved for centuries is not
  thereby false; it is simply unsettled.
THREAD: law (an allegation, a theory of the case, a verdict), science (an
  observation, a hypothesis, a confirmed theory), medicine (a case report
  against a trial).
ASKED-AS: example conjecture proof difference guess pattern proved always evidence settled claim

ESSENCE: patterns can hold for a very long time and then break. A famous
  one about circles cut by chords gives one, two, four, eight, sixteen —
  and then thirty-one. Five agreements and a wrong answer, which is what
  agreement alone is worth.
ROOT: this file / an example shows possibility, not necessity — and a
  string of examples is still only a string of examples.
CANNOT: no number of confirming cases making a general claim safe. And no
  intuition for where the break lies: some claims about whole numbers fail
  first at values too large to write down.
THREAD: money (a strategy that worked for years), medicine (a treatment
  effect that held until a larger trial), engineering (a design that never
  failed until the conditions it never met).
ASKED-AS: pattern holds then breaks first few cases sequence doubling then wrong assume

ESSENCE: one counterexample destroys a general claim completely. It is the
  cheapest and most decisive move available — no theory needed, no method,
  no argument beyond exhibiting the thing. Hunting for one should come
  before any attempt to prove.
ROOT: mathematics / a claim about all cases is refuted by one case, since
  "all" leaves no room for exceptions.
CANNOT: no counterexample supporting a claim, and none refuting a claim
  about SOME. And no repair of a broken universal by declaring the
  counterexample unusual — either the claim is narrowed in writing or it
  is dead.
THREAD: law (one contrary precedent), science (one repeatable
  disconfirming observation), engineering (one failure mode invalidating a
  safety argument).
ASKED-AS: counterexample disproves one case enough find break claim always true refute

ESSENCE: a direct proof walks from what is granted to what is claimed by
  steps nobody can refuse. Its whole discipline is that each step must be
  justified by something already standing — a premise, a definition, or a
  result already proved.
ROOT: mathematics / proof transports certainty without leaking it — so
  every link must be as strong as the ones before.
CANNOT: no step justified by plausibility, by the diagram, or by the word
  "clearly" — which in practice marks either the easiest step in the proof
  or the one that is wrong. And no proof stronger than its weakest link.
THREAD: law (a chain of reasoning from admitted facts), engineering (a
  load path traced from roof to foundation), accounting (a figure traced
  to its source documents).
ASKED-AS: direct proof steps justify each line clearly obviously assume show how write

ESSENCE: to prove "if this then that", you may instead prove "if not that,
  then not this". The two sentences say exactly the same thing — and one
  of them is frequently far easier to argue.
ROOT: mathematics / a claim and its contrapositive have identical truth in
  every case, because both forbid precisely the same combination.
CANNOT: no such equivalence for the CONVERSE — "if that then this" is a
  different claim entirely, and proving it proves nothing about the
  original. Confusing the two is the commonest logical error there is.
THREAD: law (a rule and the cases it excludes), medicine (a test that
  rules out against one that rules in), engineering (proving no failure
  mode reaches a state rather than tracing every path).
ASKED-AS: contrapositive if then not equivalent converse different prove instead easier logic

ESSENCE: from "all thieves are people" nothing follows about people being
  thieves, and nothing follows about non-thieves being non-people. The
  converse and the inverse are separate claims, each needing its own
  evidence.
ROOT: this file / only the contrapositive is guaranteed — and it is the
  one people rarely reach for, while the two that are not are the ones
  they reach for constantly.
CANNOT: no deriving the converse from the original, ever. And no
  conclusion at all about a case the rule does not cover; a rule about
  thieves is silent about everyone else.
THREAD: law (a statute's silence about uncovered cases), medicine (a
  symptom common in a disease against a disease common with the symptom),
  news (every reversal of a finding in a headline).
ASKED-AS: converse inverse not the same reverse if then backwards fallacy logic implies

ESSENCE: assume the opposite of what you want, follow it faithfully, and
  arrive at something impossible. The impossibility convicts the
  assumption — and since the assumption was the denial, the claim stands.
  It is how the root of two was shown to be no fraction.
ROOT: mathematics / a contradiction kills whatever produced it — so
  producing one from an assumption is a verdict on that assumption.
CANNOT: no such proof if the contradiction came from anywhere but the
  assumption, so every other step must be beyond doubt. And no information
  about WHY the claim is true: the method proves without explaining, which
  is why some proofs feel unsatisfying.
THREAD: law (an alibi that leads to an impossibility), engineering (a
  failure analysis assuming a state and finding it unreachable), medicine
  (excluding a diagnosis by its necessary consequences).
ASKED-AS: proof by contradiction assume opposite impossible reductio absurdum how works example

ESSENCE: when the possibilities are finite and few, prove the claim
  separately for each. It is inelegant and completely valid — and modern
  proofs sometimes leave a computer to grind through billions of cases,
  which raised a genuine question about what a proof is for.
ROOT: mathematics / proof is chained certainty — and a finite list of
  chains is still a chain.
CANNOT: no case analysis covering an infinite set of possibilities, which
  is exactly where induction takes over. And no valid analysis with a case
  missing — the completeness of the list is itself part of the proof.
THREAD: law (a rule tested against every category it might cover),
  engineering (an exhaustive failure mode review), computing (checking
  every input where the input space is small enough).
ASKED-AS: proof by cases exhaustion split into possibilities computer checked all covered complete

ESSENCE: "without loss of generality" means a symmetry has been noticed —
  the cases are interchangeable, so proving one proves the rest. It is a
  labour-saving device and it is also where a proof most often quietly
  cheats.
ROOT: mathematics / symmetry is sameness under change — if relabelling the
  cases leaves the problem unchanged, one case stands for all.
CANNOT: no such move without an actual symmetry to point at. Assuming the
  first number is the larger is fine; assuming it is even is not, and the
  phrase is used to smuggle both.
THREAD: law (treating like cases alike, where the likeness must be
  argued), engineering (testing one of a symmetric pair), physics
  (choosing coordinates to simplify without changing the problem).
ASKED-AS: without loss of generality wlog assume symmetry cases same argument cheat justified

ESSENCE: the strongest way to prove something exists is to build it. A
  construction settles existence, supplies an example, and usually shows
  how to build more — three results for one argument.
ROOT: mathematics / an example shows possibility — and existence claims
  are precisely claims of possibility.
CANNOT: no construction needed to disprove existence; that takes an
  impossibility argument instead, and the two are entirely different kinds
  of work. And no construction proving uniqueness — building one says
  nothing about whether there is another.
THREAD: engineering (a working prototype settling feasibility), law (a
  precedent established by an actual decided case), cooking (a recipe that
  demonstrably produces the dish).
ASKED-AS: proof by construction build example show exists explicit method find one settles

ESSENCE: some proofs show something must exist while giving no way
  whatever to find it. There must be two people in London with the same
  number of hairs, and no argument here names either of them. The
  knowledge is real and completely unusable.
ROOT: mathematics / a counting argument can force a conclusion without
  visiting the objects it is about.
CANNOT: no extracting a construction from such a proof; the information
  was never in it. Which is why some things are known to exist and have
  never been exhibited — including numbers proved to have properties
  nobody can demonstrate on a single example.
THREAD: computing (knowing a solution exists while the search remains
  hopeless), law (proof that a document must exist without locating it),
  economics (an equilibrium proved to exist with no way to reach it).
ASKED-AS: exists but cannot find nonconstructive proof knows there is one no example

ESSENCE: to prove a thing is the only one of its kind, suppose there are
  two and show they must be the same. Uniqueness is a separate claim from
  existence and needs its own argument — a fact that half the working in
  the world quietly skips.
ROOT: mathematics / a proof must exclude the alternatives, and "there is
  another" is the alternative here.
CANNOT: no uniqueness following from existence, and none from having
  failed to find a second. And no meaningful "the" before a thing whose
  uniqueness has not been established — the word is a claim.
THREAD: law (whether a rule admits one interpretation or several),
  engineering (a solution that is optimal against one that is merely
  feasible), navigation (a fix from two circles, which may give two).
ASKED-AS: uniqueness proof only one suppose two show same the versus a exists

ESSENCE: whole numbers cannot decrease forever. So if an assumption lets
  you build a smaller case with the same property, and then a smaller one
  again, the assumption is dead — there is no bottom for the descent to
  reach.
ROOT: mathematics / the counting numbers have a smallest member, so no
  strictly falling chain of them can be endless.
CANNOT: no infinite descent among positive whole numbers, which is what
  makes the method work. And no such argument among fractions or
  measurements, where halving forever is perfectly possible.
THREAD: computing (proving a program terminates by finding a count that
  always falls), games (showing a game must end), engineering (a process
  that provably runs out of steps).
ASKED-AS: infinite descent smallest counterexample cannot keep getting smaller whole numbers terminates

ESSENCE: count one collection two different ways and the two answers must
  agree. That single observation proves a great many identities without
  any algebra — you are not manipulating symbols, you are describing one
  pile from two directions.
ROOT: mathematics / no honest recount disagrees — so two honest counts of
  one thing are an equation.
CANNOT: no disagreement between two correct counts, so a discrepancy is
  proof that one of the countings double-counted or missed. And no such
  proof unless both counts really cover the same collection.
THREAD: accounting (a total reconciled from two independent directions),
  engineering (a mass budget checked by parts and by weighing), law (two
  independent routes to the same figure).
ASKED-AS: counting two ways proof identity combinatorial same total both directions equals

ESSENCE: to show two collections are the same size, pair them off. No
  counting required, and it works where counting is impossible — which is
  how endless collections are compared at all.
ROOT: mathematics / counting is the first abstraction, and matching
  one-to-one is what counting was standing in for.
CANNOT: no size comparison of endless collections by any other means, and
  no reliance on one being contained in the other — the whole numbers pair
  off perfectly against the even ones, and containment settles nothing.
THREAD: farming (matching sheep to pebbles, the oldest version), computing
  (an encoding that proves two data formats carry the same content), music
  (two rhythms shown identical by pairing beats).
ASKED-AS: one to one correspondence same size pair off matching count without counting bijection

ESSENCE: the pigeonhole principle is trivial to state and hard to use,
  because the whole art is choosing what the pigeons are and what the
  holes are. The conclusion is free; the modelling is the work.
ROOT: mathematics / more things than places forces sharing — with the
  content being what you decide to call a thing and a place.
CANNOT: no pigeonhole argument without a genuine count on both sides, so
  no vague "there are lots" version. And no telling WHICH hole is shared —
  the principle is entirely non-constructive.
THREAD: computing (proving any compressor must fail on some inputs), the
  body (two people sharing a hair count), scheduling (a clash forced by
  counting rooms against classes).
RULE: to use the pigeonhole principle — decide what you want two of;
  invent a property that every object has exactly one value of, and count
  how many values there are; count the objects; if the objects outnumber
  the values, two objects share a value, and that shared value is what the
  problem was asking for.
ASKED-AS: pigeonhole principle how to use choose boxes items two share prove exists

ESSENCE: to find an invariant, ignore the goal and stare at the moves. Ask
  what every single move leaves untouched — a colour, a parity, a total, a
  remainder. Whatever survives all of them is the thing that decides what
  is reachable.
ROOT: mathematics / an invariant is what the moves cannot touch — and
  finding one is a search through the moves rather than through the
  positions.
CANNOT: no invariant found by studying the target position; it lives in
  the rules of movement. And no proof of possibility from an invariant —
  it only ever forbids, never permits.
THREAD: physics (conservation laws found by asking what the equations
  preserve), accounting (a control total unchanged by any correct entry),
  computing (a loop invariant, chosen to be what the loop protects).
RULE: to hunt an invariant — write out the moves and, for each, list what
  it changes; then look for a simple quantity none of them alters, trying
  parity, a colouring, a sum, or a remainder first; compute it at the
  start and at the target; if the two differ, the target is unreachable
  and you have your proof.
ASKED-AS: find invariant puzzle impossible what stays same moves parity colouring remainder how

ESSENCE: a monovariant is a quantity that only ever moves one way. It does
  not prove impossibility; it proves ENDING — since it cannot move forever
  in a bounded range, the process must stop, and often you can say where.
ROOT: this file / no infinite descent among whole numbers — a monovariant
  is that principle attached to a process.
CANNOT: no endless process with a whole-number monovariant that is
  bounded. And no conclusion about WHERE it stops from the monovariant
  alone; that needs a separate argument about what a stopped state looks
  like.
THREAD: computing (proving a program terminates), economics (a trading
  process that must settle), physics (a quantity that only increases, and
  the arrow of time it defines).
ASKED-AS: monovariant always decreases process must stop terminate bounded proof ending puzzle

ESSENCE: when a problem resists, consider the extreme case — the largest,
  the smallest, the leftmost, the first to fail. Extremes have properties
  ordinary members do not, because there is nothing beyond them, and that
  nothing is usually the lever.
ROOT: premise — a finite collection has a largest and a smallest member,
  and being at the boundary is itself a strong piece of information.
CANNOT: no extremal argument on a collection with no extreme — an open
  range or an unbounded set may have none, and the method silently fails
  there rather than announcing it.
THREAD: engineering (designing to the worst case), law (the hardest case a
  rule must still handle), economics (the marginal buyer, who is an
  extremal object doing all the work).
ASKED-AS: consider the largest smallest extreme case argument first last boundary lever technique

ESSENCE: start at the answer and ask what would have to be true just
  before it. Working backwards turns a search with many forward branches
  into a walk with few, because the goal usually has far fewer ancestors
  than the start has descendants.
ROOT: premise — a search is cheaper from whichever end has the narrower
  fan-out, and that is very often the end you are trying to reach.
CANNOT: no working backwards through a step that is not reversible without
  checking the forward direction afterwards — the same duty as any one-way
  move in algebra.
THREAD: chess (calculating from the mating position), engineering (a
  design derived from the required output), planning (a schedule built
  from the deadline back).
ASKED-AS: work backwards from answer goal what before reverse solve puzzle strategy end

ESSENCE: if the problem has a big number in it, solve it with a small one.
  Do three instead of a hundred, two instead of n — and either the pattern
  appears or the reason it does not becomes the real question.
ROOT: premise — the structure of a problem is usually independent of its
  size, so a small instance carries the same shape at a cost you can
  afford.
CANNOT: no guarantee the small case shows the general behaviour — some
  problems are genuinely different at small sizes, and the first two or
  three cases can be misleadingly special. Test one more than feels
  necessary.
THREAD: engineering (a scale model or a pilot run), computing (debugging
  on the smallest input that still fails), cooking (a test batch).
RULE: to attack an unfamiliar problem — do the smallest case by hand, then
  the next, then the next, writing the answers out in a row; look for a
  pattern and state it as a definite guess; test the guess on one further
  case chosen to be awkward rather than convenient; then try to prove it,
  and if the proof resists, look at what the proof keeps needing, because
  that is usually the better guess.
ASKED-AS: try a smaller case simpler version small numbers pattern instead of general

ESSENCE: sometimes the general problem is EASIER than the particular one.
  Proving a statement for every n can be simpler than proving it for a
  hundred, because the general version has structure the particular one
  hides, and induction becomes available.
ROOT: this file / a proof about all cases may use machinery a single case
  cannot — the extra generality supplies the extra grip.
CANNOT: no such gain when the generalisation is false; broadening a claim
  can break it, and then the labour is wasted. The move must be tried, not
  assumed.
THREAD: engineering (a general mechanism cheaper than a bespoke one), law
  (a principle easier to defend than a particular exception), computing (a
  general algorithm simpler than a special-cased one).
ASKED-AS: generalise easier stronger statement prove for all n instead specific inventor paradox

ESSENCE: to test a claim, specialise it. Put in nothing, put in one, make
  two things equal, take the case that is obviously degenerate — and see
  whether the claim survives. Most false claims die at their own special
  cases.
ROOT: this file / a counterexample is the cheapest refutation, and the
  cheapest place to look for one is where the structure collapses.
CANNOT: no confidence from a claim surviving typical cases only; the
  typical case is where a claim was invented and is the last place it will
  fail. And no complete test either — surviving specialisation is not a
  proof.
THREAD: computing (testing with empty input, one item, and duplicates),
  engineering (checking a formula at zero load and at the limit), medicine
  (a protocol tested on the atypical patient).
ASKED-AS: test a formula special cases zero one equal degenerate check does it work

ESSENCE: two subjects that behave the same way are usually the same
  subject. When adding angles behaves like multiplying lengths, or when a
  circuit obeys the equations of a spring, the resemblance is a promise:
  every theorem carries across.
ROOT: mathematics / a structure is defined by how its parts behave, so two
  things behaving identically are the same structure wearing different
  clothes.
CANNOT: no transferring a result across an analogy that has not been
  checked to the level of the rules — a resemblance in a few cases is not
  a shared structure, and this is the standard way analogy misleads.
THREAD: engineering (electrical and mechanical systems solved with one set
  of equations), music (rhythm and pitch as the same wave arithmetic at
  different speeds), economics (models borrowed wholesale from physics).
ASKED-AS: analogy between subjects same equations transfer results structure resemblance behaves like

ESSENCE: two structures are isomorphic when there is a perfect translation
  between them that respects every operation. Then they are not merely
  similar — they are the same object under two names, and no statement in
  the language can tell them apart.
ROOT: this file / a structure is defined by behaviour — so a translation
  preserving all behaviour preserves everything there is.
CANNOT: no property distinguishing isomorphic structures from inside the
  structure. Anything that seems to is a fact about the naming, not the
  thing — which is exactly what makes the notion useful.
THREAD: language (a perfect translation, which never quite exists),
  computing (two data formats carrying identical information), music (a
  tune transposed, which is the same tune).
ASKED-AS: isomorphic same structure different names translation preserves operations identical relabelled distinguish

ESSENCE: the strongest move in problem solving is to turn your problem
  into one already solved. Nothing new is invented; the whole work is the
  translation, and the answer arrives from somebody else's labour.
ROOT: mathematics / proof transports certainty — and a translation
  transports a whole result rather than a single step.
CANNOT: no reduction without checking the translation both ways — a map
  that turns your problem into a solved one is useless unless the solved
  answer maps back. Half the failures of the method are here.
THREAD: computing (proving a problem hard by reducing a known hard one to
  it), engineering (recasting a design problem into a standard form), law
  (arguing a case falls under an established category).
ASKED-AS: reduce problem to another already solved translate transform standard form use known

ESSENCE: in some subjects every statement has a twin, made by swapping a
  pair of ideas throughout — points for lines, and for or, largest for
  smallest. Prove one and the twin is free, which halves the work of a
  whole field.
ROOT: mathematics / symmetry is sameness under change — here the change
  acts on the vocabulary itself rather than on any object.
CANNOT: no duality without an actual symmetry in the axioms; it must be
  established once and then it holds forever. And no self-dual statement
  gaining anything — those prove only themselves.
THREAD: geometry (points and lines interchangeable in the projective
  plane), computing (the two logical operations swapping under negation),
  economics (a maximisation problem and its shadow-price twin).
ASKED-AS: duality two theorems for one swap points lines and or free proof

ESSENCE: abstraction is throwing away everything that does not matter,
  deliberately. What remains is a shape that many different situations
  share, and a result about that shape is a result about every one of them
  at once.
ROOT: mathematics / counting is the first abstraction — number is what
  remains when the things are thrown away, and the subject keeps doing
  that same move at higher floors.
CANNOT: no abstraction without loss; the discarded detail is genuinely
  gone, so anything depending on it is unanswerable in the abstract
  version and must be brought back in.
THREAD: engineering (an interface hiding an implementation), law (a
  principle abstracted from the facts of the cases), language (a common
  noun standing for every instance).
ASKED-AS: abstraction general throw away details what matters common structure applies many

ESSENCE: the price of abstraction is that intuition does not come with it.
  A general statement is harder to picture, harder to check, and harder to
  teach than any of the concrete cases it covers — which is why every
  abstraction should be carried alongside an example.
ROOT: this file / abstraction discards detail, and intuition is built
  almost entirely out of detail.
CANNOT: no working understanding of an abstraction with no example in
  hand. And no abstraction worth making before there are several concrete
  cases to abstract FROM — a generalisation invented ahead of its
  instances usually generalises nothing.
THREAD: teaching (the concrete before the general, always), engineering (a
  framework built before its second use case), writing (a principle stated
  without an illustration).
ASKED-AS: abstract too general hard to understand example needed intuition lost premature generality

ESSENCE: nearly every named structure in mathematics is the same sentence:
  a collection of things, one or more ways of combining them, and a short
  list of laws those combinations obey. The variety comes entirely from
  which laws are kept.
ROOT: mathematics / a system stands on chosen premises — and a structure
  is a chosen set of premises about an operation.
CANNOT: no structure without its laws stated; a collection with an
  operation and no rules supports no theorems at all. And no theorem of a
  structure applying to a system that fails even one of the laws.
THREAD: music (scales as sets with a combining rule), computing (types
  with their permitted operations), law (an institution defined by what it
  may do rather than what it is made of).
ASKED-AS: group ring field what is structure set operation rules axioms abstract algebra

ESSENCE: the first question to ask about any operation is whether it stays
  inside. Add two whole numbers and you get a whole number; divide two and
  you may not. Closure is what makes a collection safe to work in without
  leaving it.
ROOT: this file / a structure is a set with operations obeying laws — and
  closure is the law that makes the operation an operation on that set at
  all.
CANNOT: no arithmetic confined to a collection the operation escapes — and
  every widening of number in history was a closure failure being
  repaired.
THREAD: cooking (an ingredient set that stays a cuisine), computing (a
  type whose operations return the same type), law (an institution whose
  decisions stay within its own jurisdiction).
ASKED-AS: closed under operation stays inside set whole numbers division escapes closure why

ESSENCE: two questions describe almost every undoing there is: is there a
  do-nothing element, and does each thing have a partner that cancels it.
  Nothing added, minus for plus, upside-down for times, backwards for
  every rotation — one shape, many costumes.
ROOT: this file / a structure is a set with operations obeying laws — and
  these two laws are what make an operation reversible.
CANNOT: no undoing without an inverse, so no solving equations in a
  structure that lacks them. Zero has none for multiplying, and that
  single absence is the whole of why dividing by it fails.
THREAD: money (a reversing entry in a ledger), computing (an undo
  operation and what has none), physics (a symmetry and its opposite
  transformation).
ASKED-AS: identity element inverse undo cancels do nothing zero one opposite reverse operation

ESSENCE: the payoff of abstraction is proving something once and using it
  everywhere. A theorem about any structure obeying four laws is a theorem
  about integers, rotations, permutations, and codes — none of which the
  prover had to think about.
ROOT: this file / a structure is defined by its laws — so a proof using
  only the laws applies to everything satisfying them.
CANNOT: no such transfer to a system that fails one law, however similarly
  it behaves otherwise. The laws are load-bearing, not decorative, and
  checking them is the price of admission.
THREAD: engineering (a component certified once against a standard),
  computing (a routine written against an interface and used by every
  implementation), law (a precedent binding every case in its class).
ASKED-AS: prove once applies everywhere general theorem many examples power abstraction why bother

ESSENCE: a relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive carves a
  collection into non-overlapping piles. Nothing is left out and nothing
  is in two piles — which is what "same in some respect" always turns out
  to mean.
ROOT: premise — being the same as, in any respect, must be honest about
  itself, mutual, and chainable, and those three demands force the
  partition.
CANNOT: no such relation leaving anything unclassified or in two classes
  at once. And no partition from a relation missing one of the three — "is
  a friend of" is not transitive, so friendship does not carve people into
  groups.
THREAD: law (categories that must be exhaustive and exclusive), biology
  (species boundaries and where they fail to be transitive), computing
  (grouping records by a key).
ASKED-AS: equivalence relation same as classes partition groups reflexive symmetric transitive carve

ESSENCE: the function is the central idea of modern mathematics — not the
  numbers, the mappings between them. Almost everything above elementary
  level is the study of functions, of families of functions, or of the
  spaces functions live in.
ROOT: premise — what a thing DOES is a fuller description than what it is,
  so relationships turn out to be more fundamental than objects.
CANNOT: no describing a structure without describing the maps that
  preserve it — the objects alone are inert. Which is why the maps became
  the subject and the objects became the setting.
THREAD: computing (programs as functions, and the whole functional
  tradition built on it), physics (a field is a function on space), music
  (a transformation of a theme rather than the theme).
ASKED-AS: functions central idea mappings between things more important objects modern mathematics why

ESSENCE: three questions describe any map. Does it ever send two things to
  one place; does it reach everything; does it do neither. A map that
  collides loses information, one that misses leaves room, and one that
  does neither is a perfect translation.
ROOT: this file / an inverse exists only when nothing collided — so these
  three questions are exactly the questions about reversibility.
CANNOT: no map both colliding and reversible. And no map from a smaller
  collection onto a larger one without missing something, nor from a
  larger into a smaller without colliding — which is the pigeonhole
  principle in the language of functions.
THREAD: computing (a hash that collides against an encoding that does
  not), language (translation that merges distinct words), engineering (a
  measurement that cannot distinguish two states).
ASKED-AS: injective surjective bijective one to one onto collides misses reversible map

ESSENCE: a set is a collection with no order and no repeats — a membership
  question and nothing else. Union, intersection and difference are the
  only operations, and almost every classification problem in any field is
  those three used carefully.
ROOT: premise — the most basic thing that can be said about a thing is
  whether it belongs, and everything else can be built on top of that one
  question.
CANNOT: no asking a set how many times it holds something or in what order
  — those are different objects and need different names. Confusing a set
  with a list is a standing source of error.
THREAD: computing (sets against lists, and choosing wrongly), law
  (categories of persons covered by a rule), library work (subject
  classification and its overlaps).
ASKED-AS: set union intersection difference belongs member no order duplicates versus list

ESSENCE: the empty case is not an awkward exception; it is the case that
  tests whether a definition was written properly. A rule that breaks on
  nothing at all is a rule with an unstated assumption in it.
ROOT: premise — a definition must decide every case, and the case with no
  members is the one nobody pictures while writing.
CANNOT: no sum of nothing but nought, no product of nothing but one, and
  no exceptions to either — those conventions are forced, because they are
  what keep the general laws working when a collection runs empty.
THREAD: computing (an empty list crashing code written for the typical
  case), law (a rule with no qualifying members), accounting (a period
  with no transactions, which must still balance).
