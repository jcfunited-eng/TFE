# 157 DEEP GEOGRAPHY — why places are the way they are
A place is not a list of facts about the ground. It is a position:
how much sun falls on it, what it sits between, what has to cross
it, and what it costs to reach. Almost everything else about a
place follows from that position, and most of it follows without
anybody having decided anything.

ESSENCE: latitude is a ration of sunlight. Near the equator the sun
  comes down almost straight and the year barely changes; far north
  it arrives at a slant and the year swings hard. Everything a place
  can grow, and how many days it has to grow it in, is spent out of
  that ration.
ROOT: astronomy / a ball lit from one side spreads the same beam of
  light over more and more ground the further it is tilted away.
THREAD: farming (planting windows written by the tilt), deep earth
  (the desert belts), the body (skin making a vitamin only from a
  steep enough sun).
ASKED-AS: why hot near equator cold north sun angle seasons growing crops climate

ESSENCE: water forgives and land does not. A coast keeps a narrow,
  late year because the sea takes forever to warm and forever to
  cool; the middle of a continent bakes and freezes and its seasons
  arrive on time. The same latitude holds a mild port and a place
  where the rivers go solid.
ROOT: physics / water stores far more heat per degree than rock or
  soil, and it stirs downward, so it smooths out what dry land
  amplifies.
THREAD: weather (the sea breeze is this in miniature), farming
  (frost dates set by the nearest water), building (a house designed
  for a swing rather than for a level).
ASKED-AS: coast milder inland extreme summer winter same latitude sea moderate continental

ESSENCE: a mountain range wrings the air out. Wind pushed uphill
  cools and drops its water on the near side; over the top the same
  air comes down warm and empty. One ridge can put a rainforest and
  a desert within sight of each other.
ROOT: physics / rising air expands and cools, and cold air cannot
  hold the water that warm air was carrying.
THREAD: weather (why one valley farms and the next irrigates),
  rivers (nearly all the flow born on one face), the living world
  (species split apart by a wall of dryness).
ASKED-AS: one side mountain rains other dry desert behind range wind wet slope shadow

ESSENCE: mountains do not stop people, they funnel them. A range is
  crossed at a handful of passes, and those few gaps decide where
  the roads, the tolls, the forts, the market towns and the
  invasions go. A whole region's history can hang on four or five
  holes in a wall of rock.
ROOT: movement / cost rises so steeply with slope that a longer flat
  route beats a short steep one nearly every time, which collapses
  all the traffic onto the same few lines.
THREAD: war (a chokepoint on land), trade (the toll town that
  outlives its toll), language (dialects kept apart by walls).
ASKED-AS: mountain pass road crossing armies border valleys blocked route trade forts through

ESSENCE: a river has already cut the only gentle line through rough
  country, so the footpath, the road, the railway, the canal and the
  cable all end up in the same narrow strip, stacked beside each
  other. The valley is not a place where things happen to be; it is
  the one place they can be.
ROOT: water has spent millions of years surveying the flattest route
  to the sea, and anything that dislikes gradients inherits that
  survey for nothing.
THREAD: transport (a survey nobody had to pay for), war (a defile is
  a valley read as a trap), infrastructure (every service in one
  trench).
ASKED-AS: roads follow rivers valley railway canal same route gorge flat path transport corridor

ESSENCE: the first great states sat on rivers that flooded on a
  schedule. The flood laid down fresh silt every year and carried
  the salt away, so the same fields could be farmed forever without
  anyone knowing why — and a surplus that arrives on a calendar is a
  surplus you can build a kingdom around.
ROOT: farming / soil is spent by cropping unless something replaces
  it, and a river carrying ground-up mountains replaces it free.
THREAD: soil (fertility as a delivery rather than a property), power
  (a state built on a countable harvest), water (irrigation as the
  flood repeated by hand).
ASKED-AS: nile flood silt fertile farming valley ancient egypt mesopotamia rivers soil renewed

ESSENCE: before engines, water carried freight for almost nothing
  and land carried it for its own value in about a week. A navigable
  river reaches inland like a free road, so places on one grew rich
  and places fifty miles off did not — and a country cut by usable
  rivers behaves like a much smaller country.
ROOT: physics / floating removes nearly all the friction, so the
  cost of shifting a ton drops enormously the moment it leaves the
  axle.
THREAD: money (transport cost as a wall around a market), cities
  (the head of navigation as a town-maker), engineering (canals as
  rivers built where none was).
ASKED-AS: river transport cheap boats freight barges inland trade navigable water shipping goods

ESSENCE: towns grow where a cargo has to change vehicles. Where the
  boats can go no further, everything is unloaded, stored, argued
  over and reloaded — and the people doing that need houses, food,
  rope, banks and courts. A great many old cities are simply a place
  where a load had to be put down.
ROOT: money / handling is the expensive part of moving anything, so
  wherever handling is forced, business collects around the stop.
THREAD: trade (the toll follows the transfer), logistics (cost
  driven by the number of touches), history (a place outliving the
  reason it exists).
ASKED-AS: town grew river rapids waterfall port unloading cargo transfer trade why here

ESSENCE: a good harbour is a rare accident — deep water close in,
  shelter from the usual wind and swell, a plain or a river behind
  it to trade with, and no bar of sand across the mouth. Most of the
  world's coastline has none of these, which is why an enormous
  coast can hold almost no ports.
ROOT: several unrelated requirements must all hold at the same spot,
  and independent requirements multiply into rarity.
THREAD: chance (rarity built from stacked conditions), trade (the
  sea as a road needing a doorway), building (breakwaters as
  harbours bought rather than found).
ASKED-AS: natural harbour port why city here bay sheltered deep water anchorage coast ships

ESSENCE: for most of history the sea was the fast road and the land
  was the obstacle. Two towns on opposite shores of a gulf were
  nearer each other than either was to the village over the hill
  behind it — which is why so many old worlds are rings of coast
  around a water, and why their maps look inside out to us.
ROOT: transport / cost per distance, not distance, decides who is
  near whom, and water cost a fraction of what land cost.
THREAD: trade (a route is a price, not a line), language (tongues
  spreading along shores rather than inland), war (sea power as the
  ability to be anywhere on one coast).
ASKED-AS: sea travel easier than land ancient coast trade islands connected water road neighbours

ESSENCE: an island gets less of everything — fewer species, fewer
  diseases, fewer invasions, fewer new ideas — and holds what it
  gets for longer. Isolation is a filter on the way in and a
  preservative once inside, so islands are full of odd survivals and
  missing ordinary things at the same time.
ROOT: the living world / arrival across a barrier is rare, and a
  small population then changes and loses things fast on its own.
THREAD: heredity (small populations drifting), medicine (an
  unexposed population meeting a common disease), power (a state
  that is an island only while it rules the water).
ASKED-AS: island species few isolated culture different invasion protected sea barrier unique animals

ESSENCE: some wealth sits in a spot and some is smeared over a
  province, and the difference decides the politics of a place. Oil
  in a field, diamonds in a gravel bed, a copper seam — every one of
  them can be held by a few hundred armed men standing on a few
  square miles. A wheat harvest across a whole region cannot be held
  without holding everybody in it.
ROOT: geography / where value is concentrated on the ground, the
  cost of capturing all of it is small, and anything cheap to
  capture is eventually captured.
THREAD: war (a chokepoint is this same idea applied to a route),
  power (who can take a thing decided by where it physically sits),
  economy (an industry's shape following its geology).
ASKED-AS: oil diamonds mines fought over resources war rebels seize spread crops region wealth

ESSENCE: a landlocked country's trade is not its own. Every export
  crosses somebody else's border, waits at somebody else's customs
  post, rides somebody else's railway, and stops entirely if that
  neighbour has a war or a grudge. The cost is not the extra miles;
  it is the extra sovereignties.
ROOT: movement / every handover between authorities is a place where
  a shipment can be taxed, delayed or refused, and each such place
  multiplies the risk of the whole journey.
THREAD: logistics (cost driven by the number of joins), power (a
  transit route as leverage), law (treaties of access as the real
  infrastructure).
ASKED-AS: landlocked country no coast trade harder neighbours ports transit borders exports access

ESSENCE: good farmland needs several unrelated things in the same
  place — workable soil, dependable rain during the growing season,
  ground level enough to plough, and a long enough warm spell. Each
  is common by itself. All four together cover a small fraction of
  the land, gathered into a handful of belts.
ROOT: farming / a crop grows only as far as whatever it has least
  of, so the useful ground is the overlap of every requirement
  rather than the sum of them.
THREAD: farming (irrigation as the purchase of exactly one missing
  condition), trade (a world fed out of a few regions and the
  shipping that follows from it), chance (a combination that is rare
  because its parts are unrelated).
ASKED-AS: farmland good land where crops grown rain soil flat warm rare belts world

ESSENCE: warmth without a winter lets parasites work all year and
  lets their carriers breed without a pause. So the same effort at
  farming, schooling or building buys less in a hot wet belt,
  because a share of every year's health and labour is being paid
  out to things too small to see.
ROOT: the living world / a cold or dry season halts or kills most
  carriers, and a place with neither gets no annual reset.
THREAD: medicine (drainage and screens beating any cure), history
  (settlement stopped at a disease line rather than a mountain),
  money (a tax on everything, collected by insects).
ASKED-AS: tropical diseases malaria warm climate year round mosquitoes winter kills parasites hot

ESSENCE: climbing is walking north. Every thousand metres up takes
  about as much warmth out of the air as a very long journey toward
  the pole, so a tropical mountain holds temperate fields halfway up
  and ice on top — and great cities sit at altitudes that would be
  absurd anywhere else, because that is where the climate they
  wanted was kept.
ROOT: physics / air cools as it rises and thins, and it does so at a
  rate that dwarfs any change over the same distance sideways.
THREAD: farming (crops sorted into bands up a hillside), the body
  (thin air and the lungs that adapt to it), weather (the same lapse
  rate that builds clouds).
ASKED-AS: mountain cooler higher altitude tropical highlands cities crops bands climate zones up

ESSENCE: the world has only a handful of climates and they turn up
  in the same positions on every continent — dry west coasts at the
  same latitudes, wet east coasts at the same latitudes, a mild
  strip with wet winters at the edge of each desert belt. Which is
  why wine, olives and the same weeds keep appearing on five
  separate continents.
ROOT: weather / the wind belts and ocean currents are arranged by
  the planet's spin and tilt, so their pattern repeats wherever
  there is land for it to land on.
THREAD: farming (a crop's range is a climate and not a country), the
  living world (introduced species that arrive and find home), trade
  (rivals in the other hemisphere harvesting six months out of
  phase).
ASKED-AS: mediterranean climate california chile wine same latitudes world pattern continents repeats crops

ESSENCE: dealings fall off with distance, steeply and reliably. Most
  trips, calls, marriages, trades and moves happen close to home,
  and the number drops away far faster than the miles climb.
  Nearness is not a mild preference; it is the single strongest fact
  about who deals with whom.
ROOT: every crossing costs time, money and attention, and those
  costs are paid per journey — so the far option loses a thousand
  small decisions rather than one big one.
THREAD: trade (a market shaped like the cost of reaching it), people
  together (friendship built out of repeated accident), transport
  (traffic predicted from sizes and separation alone).
ASKED-AS: people travel near home distance matters trips shorter nearby friends trade local fewer

ESSENCE: settlements sort themselves into sizes. Many villages with
  a shop, fewer towns with a doctor, a few cities with a hospital
  and a university — because each service needs a minimum number of
  customers, and that minimum decides how far apart the places
  offering it can stand.
ROOT: money / a specialty needs a market big enough to keep it busy,
  and the market is whatever population lies inside a trip people
  will actually make.
THREAD: history (density as specialisation made physical), economy
  (scale deciding how many suppliers fit), transport (the tolerable
  journey as the real boundary).
ASKED-AS: small towns big cities services hospital shops spacing why some places bigger settlements

ESSENCE: some countries have one city many times larger than the
  next, and it is usually a country that was run from a single
  place — a colonial port, or a capital that hoarded the ministries,
  the licences and the only airport. Once everything is there, being
  anywhere else costs you access, so everybody keeps arriving.
ROOT: power / when permissions are held in one building, distance
  from that building is a business cost, and firms will pay to avoid
  paying it.
THREAD: government (the capital as a place and not only an office),
  economy (crowding paying for its own congestion), history (a port
  built to face outward rather than to serve inland).
ASKED-AS: capital city huge rest country small dominant one place everything government jobs concentrated

ESSENCE: a place has two addresses. Its site is the ground itself —
  the hill, the spring, the harbour, the marsh — and its situation
  is what it sits between. Site decides whether you can build there;
  situation decides whether anybody wants to. Situation changes
  without the ground shifting an inch.
ROOT: value comes out of relations, and a relation can be altered by
  events a thousand miles away while the soil stays exactly where it
  has always been.
THREAD: money (an asset repriced by something it does not contain),
  history (empires ending where their routes end), war (a pass that
  matters only while somebody wants through it).
ASKED-AS: why city here location crossroads ground hill river position between changed importance place

ESSENCE: the reason a city was founded almost never outlives it. The
  mine closes, the ford is bridged, the fort is scrapped — and the
  city stays, because by then the reason to be there is everyone
  else who is already there. A settlement outlives its founding
  logic the way a language outlives its first speakers.
ROOT: people together / once the schools, roads, pipes, customers
  and neighbours exist, the cost of the place has been paid, and
  leaving means paying it again somewhere else.
THREAD: history (early accidents locked in), economy (sunk cost
  working as a real force rather than a fallacy), infrastructure
  (pipes and lines nobody would lay again today).
ASKED-AS: town survived mine closed factory gone city stayed why still there industry decline

ESSENCE: every centre has a catchment, and the boundary between two
  catchments sits wherever the trip to each becomes equal. It is a
  real line, it is invisible, and it moves the day a road opens. A
  city's true size is not its population but the ground that sends
  things to it.
ROOT: distance decay / people use the nearest adequate option, so a
  centre's territory is decided jointly by its own pull and by its
  rivals'.
THREAD: economy (a market's real extent), transport (a bypass moving
  trade rather than easing it), government (service boundaries drawn
  on a map fighting boundaries drawn by behaviour).
ASKED-AS: which town people shop catchment area boundary between cities pull road opened trade

ESSENCE: a border drawn on a river looks obvious and works badly —
  rivers move, and both banks want the water. A border on a
  watershed ridge divides the rain and leaves nobody upstream of
  anybody. A border drawn as a straight line across a map divides
  nothing at all, and that is very often exactly why it was chosen.
ROOT: a boundary is a promise about where authority stops, and the
  ground offers no line that is naturally a promise.
THREAD: law (a title needing a describable edge), war (the frontier
  as an expense), rivers (one water claimed by two authorities).
ASKED-AS: border river changed course straight lines map ridge watershed why drawn there dispute

ESSENCE: an enclave is a piece of one country with another country
  all the way round it. Every ordinary thing — a hospital trip, a
  school run, a delivery of bread — becomes an international
  crossing, so the place lives on somebody else's tolerance and dies
  the moment relations sour.
ROOT: government / sovereignty claims a continuous inside, and a
  fragment has no inside for it to be continuous with.
THREAD: government (sovereignty as a claim needing daily consent),
  logistics (a supply line running through a foreign authority),
  history (a leftover of an old settlement nobody can tidy).
ASKED-AS: enclave country inside another border strange piece surrounded crossing daily life exclave

ESSENCE: world trade squeezes through a handful of narrow places — a
  strait a few miles across, a canal, a mountain gap, a single
  landing point for an undersea cable. Nobody designed this. It is
  what happens when everybody independently takes the cheapest
  route: a very large system ends up with very few throats.
ROOT: transport / the cheapest path is the same cheapest path for
  everyone, so optimising concentrates traffic instead of spreading
  it.
THREAD: war (the same map read as a target list), logistics (single
  points of failure in a network), money (an insurance premium that
  prices a strait).
ASKED-AS: suez canal strait blocked shipping route narrow passage trade world chokepoint closed cargo

ESSENCE: the open ocean has roads on it. Ships follow a few worn
  tracks set by wind, current, ice, depth and the ports at each end,
  so a map of shipping looks like a map of motorways — and enormous
  areas of water carry nothing at all.
ROOT: navigation / fuel and time are saved by riding the wind and
  the current, and everybody's arithmetic returns the same answer.
THREAD: war (sea control means holding a few lines, not an ocean),
  weather (the trade winds as a timetable), trade (a route that is a
  habit as much as a geography).
ASKED-AS: shipping routes ocean lanes ships follow same paths wind currents map trade sea

ESSENCE: people move for a reason at both ends — something makes
  staying worse and something makes going look better. But the ones
  who actually go are rarely the poorest: moving costs money,
  information and nerve, so departures rise as a place gets slightly
  richer, not as it gets poorer.
ROOT: a decision needs a motive and a means, and the means is the
  part that fails first.
THREAD: money (a lump sum needed before an investment can be made),
  people together (a network doing the recruiting), history
  (migration as the normal condition of populations).
ASKED-AS: why people migrate leave country move abroad jobs poor richer family already there

ESSENCE: a city makes its own weather. Dark surfaces soak up sun,
  stone and concrete hold it into the night, rain runs off instead
  of evaporating, and every engine and cooling unit adds more — so a
  big city sits several degrees above the fields around it, most
  sharply after dark.
ROOT: physics / heat leaves a surface by evaporating water and by
  radiating to the open sky, and a city has replaced the water with
  pavement and the sky with walls.
THREAD: weather (a local climate made out of surfaces), health
  (night heat as the killer, not day heat), building (materials
  chosen for cost deciding a city's temperature).
ASKED-AS: city hotter than countryside concrete night heat trees shade temperature urban summer streets

ESSENCE: the flat land beside a river floods because it is the
  river's overflow room. It is also the best soil, the cheapest to
  build on and the closest to the water and the wharf. So people
  build there knowing, and the flood is less a surprise than an
  instalment on a bargain they took.
ROOT: money / the benefits arrive every year and the bill arrives
  once a lifetime, and a rare large loss is reliably undervalued
  against a steady small gain.
THREAD: chance (rare big events discounted by everybody),
  infrastructure (defence as probability management), law (who bears
  a risk that everyone knew about).
ASKED-AS: why build floodplain houses flooded again river valley cheap land defences knew risk

ESSENCE: a delta is not solid ground. It is a pile of mud held up by
  fresh mud arriving. Dam the river upstream and the deliveries
  stop; pump water or gas out from below and the pile squeezes down.
  Great deltas are now sinking several times faster than the sea is
  rising, and the sea gets the blame.
ROOT: deep earth / loose sediment compacts under its own weight
  forever, so a delta keeps its height only by continuous resupply.
THREAD: rivers (a river's real cargo is dirt), water (extraction
  dropping the ground it is pumped from), power (a harm caused
  upstream by people who will never see it).
ASKED-AS: delta sinking land subsidence dams sediment sea level rising flooding city ground dropping

ESSENCE: deserts do not march. Dry land degrades in place — too many
  animals in a good year, ploughing that bares the soil, wells that
  let herds stay where they should have moved on — and then a
  perfectly normal drought finds nothing left holding the ground.
  The desert does not arrive; the resistance leaves.
ROOT: the living world / plants hold soil, shade it and return water
  to the air, so stripping the cover shifts the local water balance
  in the direction of dryness.
THREAD: farming (the plough as the beginning of erosion), water (a
  well concentrating damage in a ring around it), weather (a drought
  as a test rather than a cause).
ASKED-AS: desert spreading sahara grazing overgrazing dryland soil lost drought degradation land ruined

ESSENCE: a river is one thing and its basin holds several
  governments, so the upstream state's dam is the downstream state's
  drought. There is no umpire above them and the water does not wait
  for the argument — which is why river treaties are among the most
  durable agreements in the world and among the most quietly
  resented.
ROOT: government / sovereignty claims everything inside a line, and
  a river refuses to stay inside any line.
THREAD: law (a right to a flow rather than to a thing), power
  (leverage held by position alone), farming (irrigation as the
  largest user by far and the least visible).
ASKED-AS: river shared countries dam upstream downstream water treaty dispute nile mekong supply

ESSENCE: every map leaves nearly everything out, and what it keeps
  is a decision. What is named, what is centred, which line is solid
  and which is dashed, whose spelling is used — all of it is
  asserted rather than observed, and a map is believed in a way that
  a sentence saying the same thing would not be.
ROOT: a picture is taken as a report of what is there, so a claim
  drawn as a picture slips past the part of a reader that argues.
THREAD: evidence (a definition doing the work a measurement is
  credited with), power (naming and claiming as one act), image (a
  picture read faster than it can be questioned).
ASKED-AS: maps biased projection greenland size borders disputed names different countries show truth

ESSENCE: change a map's scale and things appear and vanish. At one
  scale a village is a dot, at another it is streets, at another it
  is not there at all; a coastline measured with a shorter ruler
  gets longer without limit. There is no true length of a coast and
  no true number of islands — only answers at a stated grain.
ROOT: measurement / a reading is a question asked at a chosen
  resolution, and the resolution is part of the answer rather than a
  detail of the equipment.
THREAD: measurement (resolution inside every number), computing
  (sampling deciding what can be represented at all), evidence (a
  statistic whose definition is doing the arguing).
ASKED-AS: coastline length measured ruler longer map scale detail zoom islands counted how many

ESSENCE: regions are drawn, not found. Almost everything on the
  ground changes gradually — speech, crops, rainfall, religion,
  building style — and each of those gradients turns at a different
  place. A region is a decision to treat one bundle of gradients as
  an edge, and every such edge is arguable.
ROOT: the mind / thinking works by sorting, and continuous ground
  offers nothing to sort by unless somebody supplies a cut.
THREAD: language (a dialect shading into the next valley's), law (an
  administrative line pretending to be a fact of the ground),
  statistics (results that change when the districts change).
ASKED-AS: regions defined where does south begin middle east boundary vague area names disagree

ESSENCE: things near each other resemble each other — soil, income,
  accent, illness, voting. This is so reliable that any patch of map
  looks meaningful, and most apparent geographic patterns are just
  that: neighbours being neighbours, rather than something about the
  place acting on the people in it.
ROOT: whatever spreads, spreads outward from wherever it started, so
  similarity falls off with distance for reasons that have nothing
  to do with the trait itself.
THREAD: statistics (correlated samples inflating confidence),
  medicine (a disease cluster that is really a population cluster),
  the living world (a species range as a spread rather than a fit).
ASKED-AS: map pattern clusters neighbours similar area income accent spread real coincidence nearby places

ESSENCE: what a piece of land gets used for is decided by what it
  costs to get its product to market. Heavy or perishable things are
  grown close in, light and storable things travel from far out, and
  the cheapest land ends up under whatever survives the longest
  journey. Rent and transport cost trade off against each other,
  ring by ring.
ROOT: money / land near a market can charge more precisely because
  using it saves the journey, and the saving is collected as rent.
THREAD: economy (rent as a captured advantage), transport (a cost
  falling and re-making every decision it touched), cities (land use
  as a price map with soil underneath it).
ASKED-AS: farms near city vegetables dairy far away wheat land use rent transport market

ESSENCE: firms of the same kind pile into the same few places even
  though land and wages there are dear. What they are buying is a
  pool of people who already know the trade, suppliers reachable in
  an afternoon, and overhearing — and none of that can be bought
  anywhere else at any price.
ROOT: people together / knowledge that lives in practice moves by
  contact, and contact needs proximity.
THREAD: economy (returns that increase with scale), teaching (skill
  carried by practitioners and not by documents), history (an early
  accident deciding where an industry sits forever).
ASKED-AS: industries cluster same city expensive why firms together silicon valley suppliers workers skills

ESSENCE: before engines, the winds and currents were a one-way
  system, and the empires that grew across oceans grew along the
  loops that came back. A sailing route is a circuit and not a line,
  which is why three or four great turns in the ocean carried nearly
  all long-distance trade for centuries.
ROOT: navigation / a ship under sail cannot choose its path freely,
  so the achievable routes are set by a wind pattern nobody
  controls.
THREAD: weather (the trade winds and the belts), trade (a season
  deciding a market's calendar), history (which coasts met which,
  and when).
ASKED-AS: sailing ships wind currents routes monsoon voyage return trade winds seasons ocean explorers

ESSENCE: the sea's food is not spread evenly. A few narrow strips
  where deep cold water is dragged to the surface hold a tiny
  fraction of the ocean's area and a large share of its fish — so
  the great fishing grounds, and the towns and quarrels that go with
  them, sit exactly where the wind pushes surface water off a coast.
ROOT: the living world / life at sea is limited by nutrients, and
  nutrients sink, so production happens only where something lifts
  them back up into the light.
THREAD: oceans (currents hauling heat and nutrients together),
  farming (a harvest nobody planted and everybody counts on), money
  (a common pool that nobody owns).
ASKED-AS: fishing grounds rich fish cold water upwelling coast peru why some seas empty

ESSENCE: there is a line across every dry continent where farming
  just about works in a wet run of years and fails in an ordinary
  one. Settlement always pushes past it during the good years,
  because the good years look like the truth — and then a drought
  that is entirely unremarkable empties the country again.
ROOT: chance / a short run of a variable thing is not its average,
  and people take the run they happened to live through as the
  description of the place.
THREAD: chance (a sample too short to contain its own tail), farming
  (a crop as a bet placed on a season), history (settlement pushed
  by policy against arithmetic nobody checked).
ASKED-AS: dry farming failed drought settlers abandoned rainfall average variable good years land marginal

ESSENCE: some of the world's driest, most productive farmland is
  drinking water that fell as rain thousands of years ago and is not
  being replaced. The pumping looks like ordinary irrigation and is
  actually mining — and when the level drops past the reach of the
  pumps, the farms, the towns and the reason for the towns all stop
  together.
ROOT: money / a stock being drawn down looks exactly like a flow
  until it is gone, and nothing in the annual accounts marks the
  difference between the two.
THREAD: money (a store spent as though it were income), farming
  (yields bought from the past), water (rain that fell in a
  different climate entirely).
ASKED-AS: desert farming irrigation wells water table dropping aquifer ancient rain pumping running out

ESSENCE: a very large share of the world's people live within a
  short walk of a sea or a great river, on a very small share of its
  land. Ports, fish, flat ground, cool air and a view all want the
  same strip — so the most valuable, most contested and most exposed
  ground on earth is a thin ribbon at the edge.
ROOT: several unrelated advantages happen to sit on the same narrow
  band, and demand for a fixed strip cannot be answered by making
  more of it.
THREAD: money (fixed supply meeting rising demand), weather (storm
  surge finding the crowd), infrastructure (defences protecting the
  most expensive land first and the poorest last).
ASKED-AS: people live near coast crowded seaside expensive land storms erosion population edge sea

ESSENCE: being in the middle of everything is not a gift. A flat
  plain between two strong neighbours has no defensible edge, so it
  is crossed, fought over, partitioned and reassembled — and the
  same openness that makes a place rich in trade makes it cheap to
  invade.
ROOT: war / defence is bought from terrain, and a place with no
  terrain must buy it with people and money instead, every year,
  forever.

  the same ground).

ESSENCE: several countries have built a capital in an empty place on

  the country belongs to, and a new city on neutral ground is the

ESSENCE: a narrow neck of land between two seas is worth far more
  than the land is worth. Cutting it saves not miles but weeks, and
  the saving is collected as a toll forever — which is why canals
  across isthmuses have been dug at enormous cost in money and lives
  by people who would never have farmed an acre of that ground.
ROOT: money / a saving repeated by every ship forever adds up to a
  very large sum in the present, and that sum is what justifies the
  digging.
THREAD: trade (chokepoints made by hand), engineering (a lock as a
  staircase built for water), power (a strip of land governed
  separately because of what passes through it).
ASKED-AS: panama suez canal built why isthmus ships saved weeks toll locks water shortcut

ESSENCE: ships grew because bigger is cheaper per ton, and every
  increase left some ports behind. A harbour that cannot be dredged
  deep enough, a bridge too low, a lock too narrow — any one of them
  drops a port out of the network permanently, and its trade moves
  to a rival that could dig.
ROOT: money / a cost per ton that falls with vessel size makes the
  largest usable ship the standard, and the standard then sets the
  minimum a port has to supply.
THREAD: logistics (scale economies deciding an industry's shape),
  history (the container moving the docks downriver), engineering
  (maintenance as the true cost of a channel).

ESSENCE: people site towns using the worst thing anybody remembers,
  and the ground's worst is far worse than anybody's memory. A
  river's biggest flood, a coast's biggest wave, a slope's biggest
  slide are all events with gaps of centuries between them — so the
  safest-looking place is often the one that has not had its turn.
ROOT: chance / rare events are common over long spans and absent
  over short ones, and human records are short.
THREAD: chance (rare large events), deep earth (the rock keeping a
  much longer memory than the archive), infrastructure (design
  standards set from a series that is too short).

ESSENCE: in cold country the ground itself is a building material
  that melts. A heated house warms the soil beneath it, the ice in
  that soil turns to water, and the whole thing sinks and tilts — so
  northern towns stand on stilts, on gravel pads or on refrigerated
  foundations, and a warming climate does the same damage to all
  three.
ROOT: physics / ice inside a soil carries load and water does not,
  so the strength of that ground is a function of its temperature.
THREAD: building (the ground working as part of the structure),
  infrastructure (a whole town's assets sharing one failure mode),
  deep earth (frozen ground storing carbon as well as strength).

ESSENCE: rough country holds more languages, more crop varieties and
  more customs per square mile than flat country — not because the
  people there are different, but because the next valley costs
  enough to reach that everything drifts apart and nothing gets
  standardised. The ridges are doing the work.
ROOT: distance decay / contact is what merges things, so terrain
  that raises the price of contact preserves differences that would
  otherwise blend away.
THREAD: language (dialects shading valley to valley), farming (many
  local varieties instead of a few national ones), government (a
  state's real border is its reach).

ESSENCE: every crop, tree, pest and disease lives inside a band of
  temperature and moisture, and those bands are moving — toward the
  poles and up the hillsides. The plant moves only as fast as its
  seeds travel, the pest usually moves faster, and the farm, the
  vineyard and the forest cannot move at all.
ROOT: the living world / a range is a climate, so when the climate
  walks the range must walk with it or the population dies where it
  is standing.
THREAD: farming (a variety bred for a climate that is leaving), the
  living world (a range shifting faster than the soil under it),
  money (a long-lived asset priced on an assumption).
ASKED-AS: crops moving north warming vineyards pests spreading trees range shifting climate farming zones

ESSENCE: places get closer without moving. When a journey that took
  a week takes a day, every decision that respected that distance is
  re-made — where to buy, where to live, who counts as a neighbour —
  and whatever the connected places gained, the places off the line
  lost in exactly the same measure.
ROOT: transport / what matters is time and cost rather than miles,
  so improving the vehicle moves the map without touching the
  ground.
THREAD: history (a message outrunning the messenger), economy (a
  market's extent set by reach), cities (a bypass moving trade away
  from a town rather than easing it).

ESSENCE: a frontier is a zone and a boundary is a line, and the
  world has moved from one to the other. For most of history
  authority faded outward into ground nobody administered; the
  modern map has no such gaps, so every acre is claimed, and
  disagreements that could once simply be ignored now have to be
  settled or held open.
ROOT: government / a state claims every acre inside a line, and a
  world made of such states leaves no unclaimed space for ambiguity
  to live in.
THREAD: government (sovereignty as a claim to have no superior),
  history (empires ending where their logistics did rather than at a
  line), law (a title needing an edge to be a title).

ESSENCE: big cities line up. Once a route exists, every place along
  it grows for the same reason, and eventually their edges touch —
  so the largest settled areas on earth are not blobs but strings,
  running along a coast, a valley, or the line somebody laid a
  railway on.
ROOT: transport / growth follows access, and access is delivered
  along a line rather than spread over an area.
THREAD: infrastructure (systems crossing many authorities),
  government (boundaries that no longer match the thing they
  govern), economy (a labour market larger than any city on it).

ESSENCE: the same feature changes sides. A river is a barrier to an
  army on foot and a highway to one with boats; a forest hides a
  rebel and blinds an aircraft; a desert stops a wagon and welcomes
  a truck. Terrain has no fixed meaning — it has a meaning per
  vehicle.
ROOT: an obstacle is defined by what it costs whoever is crossing
  it, and that cost belongs to the technology rather than to the
  ground.
THREAD: war (terrain multiplying force), transport (each mode with
  its own cost surface laid over one landscape), history (a
  technology remaking geography without moving a stone).
ASKED-AS: river barrier or road desert crossing trucks forest cover terrain changed technology obstacle
