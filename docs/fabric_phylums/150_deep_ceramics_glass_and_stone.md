# 150 DEEP CERAMICS, GLASS AND STONE — the hard crafts
Three trades that work with rock: one that grinds it, wets it and
bakes it back; one that melts it and races it while it cools; one
that takes it as found and removes everything that is not the thing.
All three are one-way from the first move, and all three are ruled by
what heat does to a body that cannot flow. These are the laws under
the pot, the pane and the block, and every one of them forbids
something.

ESSENCE: clay is not a substance, it is a shape. It is rock that
  weather has broken down into flakes so thin that water films
  between them, and the flakes slide over each other while holding
  together by that same water — which is exactly what we mean by
  something being plastic. Fire drives the water out and the flakes
  fuse where they touch.
ROOT: chemistry / properties come from the arrangement of the
  innards, and here the arrangement is a stack of plates with liquid
  between them.
THREAD: the body (joints sliding on a fluid film), building (a
  lubricated slope that slips), snow (packing only in a narrow
  wetness band).

ESSENCE: clay works only inside a narrow window of wetness. Too wet
  and it sags under its own weight and sticks to everything; too dry
  and it tears rather than bends. Every stage of pottery is a
  different point in that window, and moving down it is easy while
  moving back up it is nearly impossible without ruin.
ROOT: craft / a material has one state in which the work of the hour
  is possible, and the state is moving all the time.
THREAD: cooking (dough at the right temperature and no other),
  building (plaster's working window between too wet and gone off),
  metals (forging inside a temperature band).

ESSENCE: there are two kinds of water in clay and only one of them
  can be given back. The water between the flakes leaves when the
  pot dries, and can be replaced. The water locked inside the
  mineral itself leaves at a few hundred degrees, and the moment it
  has gone the clay is no longer clay — it will never soften in
  water again, however long it soaks.
ROOT: chemistry / a reaction that consumes part of the material is
  one-way, while evaporation is not.
THREAD: cooking (an egg that has set), medicine (a burn as against a
  bruise), keeping knowledge (writing carved as against writing in
  sand).

ESSENCE: a pot shrinks twice, for two different reasons. It shrinks
  while it dries, as the water between the flakes leaves and they
  close up. Then it shrinks again in the fire, as the body begins to
  melt at its contact points and pulls tight. Together that is
  commonly a tenth or more of the size, so a potter making a lid for
  a jar is working in a size that does not yet exist.
ROOT: mathematics / a proportional change applies to every
  dimension at once, so a small percentage on a length is a large
  one on a volume.
THREAD: metals (a casting pattern cut oversize by the shrink rule),
  building (a floor laid to a size the wood does not yet have),
  tailoring (cutting for cloth that will shrink at the first wash).
ASKED-AS: pottery shrinks drying firing lid fits jar size percentage measuring clay allowance

ESSENCE: a pot cracks in drying because one part of it got there
  first. A rim dries faster than a base, an edge faster than a
  middle, and the part that has already shrunk is held by the part
  that has not — so the drying is slowed and evened deliberately,
  under plastic, with the thin parts covered and the thick ones
  open.
ROOT: physics / a difference is what does damage; a whole body
  changing size together does no harm at all.
THREAD: metals (a casting cracking where thick meets thin), building
  (a slab cured wet to stop it drying at the top first), the body (a
  wound healing at different speeds along its length).

ESSENCE: wedging is not mixing the clay, it is emptying it —
  squeezing out trapped air and, at the same time, lining up the
  flakes so that the body behaves the same in every direction. An
  air pocket in a wall becomes steam in the fire and takes the wall
  with it, and unaligned clay pulls off centre on the wheel.
ROOT: craft / preparation decides the ceiling of the work, and its
  effects are invisible until the stage where nothing can be done.
THREAD: this file (annealing glass to remove the stress that was put
  in earlier), cooking (kneading against folding — the same motion
  for a different end), building (compacting a base before pouring).
ASKED-AS: wedging clay kneading air bubbles pottery preparing spiral bursting kiln wheel centring

ESSENCE: the wall of a pot must be even in thickness, and not
  because it looks better. A thick section still holds water when a
  thin one has dried, still holds heat when a thin one has cooled,
  and expands and contracts on its own schedule — so unevenness is
  the mechanism of nearly every crack from the drying shelf to the
  kiln shelf.
ROOT: physics / a body's response to heat and to drying goes with
  its thickness, so a body of two thicknesses is two bodies tied
  together.
THREAD: metals (a weld between two thicknesses cooling unequally),
  cooking (a joint of meat with a thin end), building (a foundation
  of two depths settling differently).
ASKED-AS: pot base thick heavy wall thin uneven cracking trimming turning foot weight

ESSENCE: any sealed hollow form must be given a hole before it is
  fired. Air inside expands with heat and steam is produced from
  whatever moisture is left, and there is no wall thick enough to
  hold that — a closed sphere becomes a bomb on the kiln shelf, and
  it takes the neighbours with it.
ROOT: physics / a gas heated in a fixed volume raises its pressure
  without limit, and a fired body has no give at all.
THREAD: cooking (an egg or a chestnut bursting in a hot oven), the
  body (a sealed cavity under pressure), engineering (any vessel
  needing a relief path by law).
ASKED-AS: hollow sculpture exploded kiln pinhole vent sealed form firing burst air pressure

ESSENCE: a pot must be genuinely bone dry before it meets the fire.
  Water left in the wall turns to steam at boiling point and leaves
  faster than the pores can pass it, and the piece blows apart —
  which is why the first hours of a firing are spent below the boil,
  with the door ajar, doing nothing that looks like progress.
ROOT: physics / a liquid becoming a gas expands enormously, and a
  solid with narrow pores cannot pass that much gas at once.
THREAD: welding (damp or painted metal boiling into the weld),
  cooking (a wet surface steaming rather than browning), building
  (a screed that must be dry before a floor goes on).
ASKED-AS: firing kiln explode pots damp candling slow drying bone dry cold cheek water

ESSENCE: throwing begins with making the lump perfectly symmetrical
  about the axis of the wheel, and nothing afterwards can rescue a
  failure at that step. An off-centre lump presents a different
  amount of clay to the hand once every revolution, so the hand is
  being asked to correct a rhythm rather than to hold a position —
  and the wall thins wherever the correction lags.
ROOT: physics / a spinning body meets the tool at a rate set by its
  own turning, so any asymmetry becomes a repeating disturbance.
THREAD: engineering (balancing a wheel before it is used), music
  (tuning before playing), building (setting out from one true line
  before anything is built).
ASKED-AS: centring clay wheel wobbling throwing pottery lump off centre arms bracing hands

ESSENCE: on the wheel a wall only ever gets thinner. Clay is moved
  by squeezing it between two points and letting the squeeze travel
  upward, so every pull adds height and takes thickness, and there
  is no operation that puts thickness back. The whole craft is
  therefore a budget: a fixed amount of wall, spent upward, once.
ROOT: physics / matter is conserved, so height comes out of
  thickness and out of nothing else.
THREAD: money (a fixed budget spent forward), craft (cutting to the
  waste side because wood cannot be added back), writing (a first
  draft's material redistributed but never increased).
ASKED-AS: throwing pull walls thin collapsed too tall pottery wheel height clay thickness rim

ESSENCE: water on the wheel is both the lubricant that makes
  throwing possible and the poison that ends the pot. Every minute
  of throwing puts more water into the clay, and the wall softens
  until it can no longer stand — so a pot has a working life
  measured in a few minutes, and the skilled potter is not the one
  with the better hands but the one who needs fewer pulls.
ROOT: craft / the material's condition is running out while the work
  is being done, so speed is a property of the design and not of the
  worker's haste.
THREAD: cooking (a pastry warming in the hands as it is worked),
  building (a mortar going off in the board), surgery (tissue
  deteriorating while the operation runs).

ESSENCE: clay remembers being handled. A wall stretched hard on one
  side, a rim pulled crooked and pushed back straight, a slab rolled
  always in one direction — none of it shows on the drying shelf, and
  all of it comes back in the fire as a warp, because heat lets the
  body relax toward the shape its particles were left lying in.
ROOT: chemistry / working a material leaves its particles lined up,
  and heat gives them the freedom to act on that alignment.
THREAD: metals (a machined part moving as its locked-in stress is
  released), wood (a board that cups when one face is planed), the
  body (a posture learned young and returned to under fatigue).

ESSENCE: leather-hard is the state a potter waits for and the state
  most of the work happens in. The clay has lost enough water to
  hold its own shape and take a knife, and has kept enough to be
  cut, carved, turned and joined. It is a window that closes on its
  own, and the whole workshop is arranged around timing pieces into
  it.
ROOT: craft / the useful state of a material is a passing condition,
  so the schedule of the work is set by the material rather than by
  the worker.
THREAD: cooking (a cake turned out of its tin at the right moment),
  building (concrete floated when it has stiffened but not set),
  farming (a hay crop cut in the only fine week).
ASKED-AS: leather hard clay trimming turning handles attaching carving stage timing wrapped plastic

ESSENCE: two pieces of clay only join if they are at the same
  wetness. A wet handle on a dry pot dries and shrinks after the pot
  has stopped, and pulls itself off along the joint — usually weeks
  later, or in the fire. The scoring and the slip are how the join
  is made, but the matched moisture is why it holds.
ROOT: physics / two bodies changing size at different times tear at
  the surface between them.
THREAD: building (a new extension moving against an old wall),
  medicine (a graft mismatched to its bed), materials (two metals
  bonded and swelling at different rates in the sun).
ASKED-AS: handle fell off pot joining slip scoring crack join dry wet clay attaching

ESSENCE: adding pre-fired grit to a clay body opens it up — the
  grains give water a road out, cut the shrinkage, and hold the wall
  against sagging, which is what lets a large piece or a cooking pot
  survive at all. It is paid for in plasticity and in a rough
  surface, so the body that will hold a big form is the body that
  will not throw a fine one.
ROOT: engineering / a design buys a virtue with a virtue, and here
  the virtue sold is the very slipperiness that made the clay
  workable.
THREAD: building (aggregate in concrete for the same three
  reasons), cooking (bran in a dough shortening the gluten), metals
  (an alloying element blocking growth at the cost of workability).

ESSENCE: the clays that are purest are the least workable. A clay
  that has stayed where its rock decayed is clean, white and coarse;
  a clay that a river has carried and sorted is finer, far more
  plastic, and stained with iron and organic matter. So whiteness
  and workability are opposites in nature, and every white body is a
  blend that puts back by recipe what the purity took away.
ROOT: geology / transport sorts particles by size and picks up
  passengers, so the same journey that refines a material
  contaminates it.
THREAD: metals (ore purity and ease of working rarely coinciding),
  food (refined flour losing what made it nutritious), water (a
  spring clean because it travelled through rock and poor because of
  it).
ASKED-AS: white clay porcelain red terracotta iron plastic throwing ball clay kaolin body

ESSENCE: the first firing has nothing to do with beauty. It is done
  to turn a fragile dried pot into something that can be handled and
  dipped, and — crucially — to leave it porous, so that when it is
  dipped in glaze the pot sucks the water out and the glaze coat
  stays put. A pot fired high first cannot be glazed by dipping at
  all.
ROOT: engineering / a process step exists to create the condition
  the next step requires, not to advance the visible product.
THREAD: painting (a primer coat that exists to be absorbed into),
  building (a scratch coat keyed for the one after it), teaching (a
  foundation year that teaches nothing anyone came for).

ESSENCE: a glaze is a glass that is manufactured on the pot. Ground
  rock and ash are painted on as a slurry, and in the fire they melt
  into a thin layer of true glass that must do four things at once:
  melt at the temperature this body is fired to, stay where it was
  put, wet the body enough to bond, and end up the same size as the
  body when everything is cold.
ROOT: chemistry / a mixture of rocks has a melting behaviour set by
  its recipe, so melting temperature is something that is designed.
THREAD: welding (a filler chosen to melt with its parent metal),
  building (a render matched to the wall behind it), cooking (a sauce
  that must set at the temperature the dish is served).
ASKED-AS: glaze recipe melting temperature cone pottery bucket powder rock glass shiny body

ESSENCE: a melted glaze is a liquid and it obeys gravity. It thins
  at the rim, thickens where the form turns, pools in the bottom of
  a bowl and runs off a vertical wall — and if it reaches the foot
  it welds the pot to the kiln shelf, where removing it destroys
  both. Every foot ring in the world is wiped clean for this one
  reason.
ROOT: fluids / a liquid on a slope flows until it is too cold or too
  stiff to flow, so the only questions are how runny and how long.
THREAD: painting (a coat running on a vertical surface), cooking
  (cheese leaving the top of a dish and welding to the tin), metals
  (solder finding its way to where it was not wanted).
ASKED-AS: glaze run kiln shelf stuck foot wiping bowl pooling thick drips vertical

ESSENCE: how thick a glaze goes on is not decided by the dipping
  hand but by how thirsty the pot is. A dry biscuit pot drinks fast
  and takes a thick coat in two seconds; a damp one, or one still
  warm from the kiln, takes almost nothing. And glaze colour and
  surface change completely with thickness — so the same bucket
  gives three different results in one afternoon.
ROOT: physics / a porous body pulls water in at a rate set by how
  much water it already holds.
THREAD: painting (a porous wall drinking the first coat), cooking (a
  batter clinging or sliding by how dry the surface is), farming
  (soil taking up water at a rate set by how wet it already is).
ASKED-AS: glaze thin patchy thick dipping seconds pot dry damp double dipping colour uneven

ESSENCE: every kind of decoration has its own firing temperature,
  and they are all different — so a decorated pot goes through the
  kiln several times, always downward: the glaze first at its full
  heat, then enamels, then gold and lustre at the lowest of all. Fire
  them in any other order and each firing destroys the work of the
  one before it.
ROOT: engineering / a process whose steps happen at different
  temperatures can only run in one direction, because every step
  revisits everything already done.
THREAD: metals (brazing before soldering an assembly and never
  after), cooking (the order set by what each stage survives),
  building (trades sequenced by what buries what).
ASKED-AS: decorating pottery enamel lustre gold firing order twice temperature underglaze onglaze transfer

ESSENCE: glaze and body must end up the same size when cold, and
  they almost never do on their own. If the glaze shrinks more than
  the body it is stretched over it and cracks into a net — crazing.
  If it shrinks less it is squeezed and peels off the edges in
  flakes — shivering. Both are faults of the pair, not of either one
  alone.
ROOT: physics / two materials joined face to face and cooled
  together load each other by whatever difference there is in their
  shrinking.
THREAD: materials (two bonded metals loaded by every change of
  weather), building (a hard render on a soft wall), dentistry (a
  filling expanding differently from the tooth).
ASKED-AS: crazing glaze cracks lines pottery shivering flaking fit body craze network cold

ESSENCE: the whole problem of glazing is one number: how much the
  glaze changes size with heat compared with how much the body does.
  Everything else — the colour, the gloss, the recipe, the beauty —
  sits on top of a match that either exists or does not. A little
  squeeze in the glaze is actually the aim, because glass is strong
  in squeeze and weak in stretch, so a well-fitted pot is stronger
  than a bare one.
ROOT: physics / a material's response to temperature is a fixed
  property, so a joint between two materials carries their
  difference for ever.
THREAD: this file (tempered glass built on the same asymmetry),
  building (pre-stressed concrete squeezed so it is never stretched),
  metals (shot-peening a surface into compression).
ASKED-AS: glaze fit expansion crazing silica alumina adjusting recipe body strength squeeze stretch

ESSENCE: a crazed glaze means opposite things on two pots. On dense
  stoneware the body itself is waterproof, so a craze net is only a
  look and a place for tea to stain. On porous earthenware the body
  holds water, and the crack is a road into it — so the pot seeps,
  smells, harbours bacteria and eventually splits along a craze line
  in the freezer or the dishwasher.
ROOT: engineering / the same defect is trivial or fatal according to
  what lies behind it, so a fault must always be read together with
  its substrate.
THREAD: medicine (the same rash meaning nothing or everything by
  what is under it), building (a hairline crack in render against
  one in a beam), computing (a warning that is noise in one system
  and an alarm in another).

ESSENCE: a glaze can look perfect and still give up metal into food.
  If the glass was not fired to full maturity, or the recipe is out
  of balance, or the colourant is one of the heavy metals, then acid
  food and hot drink dissolve a little of the surface every time —
  and the brightest low-fired colours have always been the worst
  offenders.
ROOT: chemistry / a glass dissolves in acid at a rate set by its own
  composition, so stability is a property of the recipe and not of
  glass in general.
THREAD: pharmacy (a dose built from many small ones), plumbing (lead
  pipe dissolving into soft water), food (a container chosen by what
  it is going to hold).

ESSENCE: the silica inside a clay body suddenly changes its crystal
  form at a particular temperature on the way up and back again on
  the way down, and jumps in size as it does so. So a kiln has a
  band it must be walked through slowly in both directions — and the
  cooling half is the one that cracks pots, because a firing that
  went perfectly can be ruined by opening the door early.
ROOT: chemistry / a crystal has more than one way to stack, and a
  change of stacking is a change of volume that happens all at once.
THREAD: metals (iron restacking itself on heating, which is the
  whole of steelmaking), water (ice expanding as it forms), geology
  (minerals changing form with depth and shifting the rock).
ASKED-AS: kiln cracked cooling opening early quartz inversion dunting ping ware ring firing schedule

ESSENCE: a hard brittle thing does not break because it is hot or
  cold, but because one part of it is hotter than another and they
  are tied together. So the three cures are all the same cure: make
  it thin, make it out of something that changes size less, and heat
  or cool it evenly. This is why ovenware is thin, why cheap glass
  splits under hot water, and why a thick handmade mug is the
  fragile one.
ROOT: physics / stress comes from a difference, and heat entering a
  solid always arrives at the outside first.
THREAD: this file (drying and firing a pot of uneven thickness),
  building (a stone step split by a frost after sun), the body (a
  tooth cracked between hot and cold in one mouthful).
ASKED-AS: mug cracked boiling water pyrex casserole thermal shock thick thin heat sudden cold

ESSENCE: a kiln is not controlled to a temperature, it is controlled
  to an amount of cooking. A body carries on maturing the longer it
  is held hot, so the same result comes from hotter and quicker or
  cooler and slower — which is why potters trust a small pyramid
  that melts and bends when the right combination has been reached,
  and treat the thermometer as a rough guide.
ROOT: chemistry / a reaction rate climbs steeply with heat, so time
  and temperature trade against each other along a known exchange.
THREAD: cooking (safety as time at temperature rather than a
  number), medicine (dose as strength times duration), building
  (concrete gaining strength on warmth and time together).

ESSENCE: a kiln is never one temperature. Heat arrives from
  particular places and leaves through the walls, so there is always
  a hot spot and a cold one, and the difference can be a whole cone
  or more. That means the same glaze on the same body gives
  different pots on different shelves, and a kiln has to be mapped
  by its owner rather than read from a manual.
ROOT: physics / heat flows down a gradient, and any box that is
  heated at one place and loses heat at its skin must have a spread
  inside it.
THREAD: cooking (the back and front of a domestic oven), farming (a
  field with a wet corner), computing (one machine in a cluster
  running hotter and failing first).

ESSENCE: almost all the cost of a firing is heating the kiln and its
  shelves, not the pots — so a half-empty kiln costs nearly what a
  full one costs, and the rhythm of the whole workshop is set by
  filling it. Work waits on the shelf for company, and the one urgent
  piece is the most expensive object in the building.
ROOT: money / where the cost falls per batch rather than per item,
  the sensible batch is as large as the box will take.
THREAD: logistics (a lorry dispatched half full costing the same as
  a full one), baking (an oven lit for one loaf), computing (a job
  run on a machine that had to be started anyway).

ESSENCE: what the kiln is breathing changes what comes out of it.
  Starve the fire of air and it takes oxygen from wherever it can,
  including from the metal oxides in the glaze — and copper turns
  from green to blood red, iron from tan to a soft grey-green, the
  clay itself from buff to grey. The recipe is unchanged; only the
  air was.
ROOT: chemistry / a metal's colour depends on how many electrons it
  is carrying, and a hungry flame is a chemical agent that takes
  them away.
THREAD: cooking (browning as a chemistry of the surface, not of the
  ingredient), metals (a furnace atmosphere deciding whether a part
  scales), photography (the developer deciding what the same
  exposure becomes).

ESSENCE: in some firings the glaze arrives as a gas. Salt or soda
  thrown into a hot kiln vaporises, and wood ash flies with the
  flame; both land on the ware and combine with the clay's own
  surface into a glaze made in place. So the pot is glazed where the
  vapour could reach and bare in its own shadows, and the kiln
  itself is glazed too, a little more with every firing.
ROOT: chemistry / a reaction between a vapour and a surface happens
  wherever the two meet, so the pattern of the result is a map of
  the flow.
THREAD: weather (frost settling only where the air reached), light
  (a photograph as a record of what the light could see), farming (a
  spray drifting and landing by wind rather than by intention).

ESSENCE: earthenware, stoneware and porcelain are not three
  materials but three distances along one road. As the firing goes
  higher, more of the body melts and the melt fills the holes
  between the grains — so earthenware stays porous and needs a glaze
  to hold water, stoneware closes up and holds water by itself, and
  porcelain becomes so nearly all glass that light passes through
  it.
ROOT: chemistry / a mixture begins to melt at the temperature its
  most fusible parts allow, and the melt fills space by capillary
  pull.
THREAD: metals (sintering powder into a solid part by the same
  mechanism), building (fired brick from soft brick by the same
  ladder), geology (rock partly melted and welded at depth).

ESSENCE: porcelain is unforgiving for exactly the reasons it is
  beautiful. The particles that make it white and translucent are
  fine, pure and non-plastic, so the body has little to hold it
  together while wet; it goes floppy at a moment's notice, it
  remembers every distortion, and it moves in the fire. The
  difficulty is not a tradition, it is the same three facts that
  produce the material.
ROOT: engineering / one set of properties produces both the prize
  and the price, so the difficulty cannot be separated from the
  result.
THREAD: cooking (a delicate ingredient with a short window),
  materials (a high-performance alloy with a narrow process range),
  music (an instrument that rewards and punishes the same touch).

ESSENCE: the inside of a kiln has to be made of something that will
  not melt and also does not mind being heated and cooled again and
  again — and those are two different requirements pulling apart. A
  dense refractory brick is strong and stores heat, so it costs fuel
  and holds it; a light insulating brick saves the fuel and crumbles
  if you look at it. Every kiln is a compromise between the two.
ROOT: engineering / insulation is trapped air and strength is solid
  material, so the two cannot be maximised in one body.
THREAD: this file (grog opening a body at the cost of its
  plasticity), building (a wall that must insulate and carry load),
  clothing (a fabric warm because it holds air and weak for the same
  reason).

ESSENCE: glass has no melting point. Its atoms are frozen in the
  disorder of a liquid because it was cooled too fast to organise
  into a crystal, so instead of a temperature at which it turns to
  liquid it has a long range over which it grows gradually softer —
  and that single fact is what makes it workable at all, since every
  other hard material goes from solid to liquid with nothing usable
  in between.
ROOT: chemistry / a crystal has a fixed melting temperature because
  its structure is fixed, and glass has no structure to break.
THREAD: cooking (sugar going through the same states — thread, ball,
  crack), materials (a plastic softening rather than melting), the
  mind (a gradual change with no single moment of transition).

ESSENCE: a glassblower works inside a window of stiffness. Too hot
  and the piece flops and pulls itself out of shape; too cool and it
  will not move and it cracks. The window lasts seconds to a minute,
  so the piece is carried back to the furnace mouth again and again,
  and the design of the object is really a plan for what can be done
  in each short visit.
ROOT: craft / where the workable state is a passing condition, the
  work must be broken into what fits inside it.
THREAD: cooking (a sugar or chocolate window), surgery (a step that
  must be finished before the tissue changes), music (a live
  performance with no stopping).
ASKED-AS: glassblowing reheating glory hole too stiff floppy timing working window pipe turning seconds

ESSENCE: a finished glass object has to be cooled on a schedule or
  it destroys itself. Cooling fast leaves the outside solid while
  the inside is still shrinking, and the trapped disagreement stays
  locked inside for ever — until a knock, a warm drink or nothing at
  all releases it, sometimes days later. Annealing means holding the
  piece warm and letting it down slowly, and it takes far longer
  than making it.
ROOT: physics / a solid that is cooled unevenly freezes a
  disagreement about size into itself, and nothing removes stress
  except letting the material move.
THREAD: metals (stress relieving a welded frame), building (a
  concrete pour cured slowly to avoid cracking), the mind (an
  unresolved tension surfacing long after the event).

ESSENCE: tempered glass is a pane with an enormous argument stored
  inside it — the skin cooled and set first, then the middle
  shrank and pulled the skin into squeeze. That squeeze is its
  strength, and it also means the whole pane is one connected
  system: break the skin anywhere and the argument is released
  everywhere at once, and the pane becomes a heap of small pieces in
  a moment.

ESSENCE: glass is not cut, it is cracked to order. The wheel does
  not remove anything — it presses a shallow crack into the surface,

  within minutes, so an old score will not run true, and a line can
  never be scored twice.

ESSENCE: blowing and casting make two different materials out of one

  makes solid and detailed things slowly — and because the piece is

ESSENCE: fired clay is the most durable thing ordinary people ever
  made. It is chemically finished, so nothing rots it; it is
  worthless, so nobody melted it down; and when it breaks it becomes
  many pieces of exactly the same material rather than dust. So the
  record of most of human life survives as broken pots, and their
  styles change fast enough to be a calendar.
