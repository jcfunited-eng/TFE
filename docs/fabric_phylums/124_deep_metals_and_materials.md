# 124 DEEP METALS AND MATERIALS — what stuff is and why it behaves
Every material behaves the way it does because of how its insides are
arranged, and that arrangement can be changed without changing the
recipe. These are the laws underneath the catalogue — what the
insides decide, what no treatment can undo, and what you have agreed
to the moment you choose one material over another.

ESSENCE: a metal is not a smooth substance, it is atoms stacked in a
  repeating pattern like oranges in a crate. Which pattern they use
  decides almost everything the metal will do — how easily it bends,
  whether it stays tough in the cold, and how much of another element
  it can dissolve.
ROOT: chemistry / properties come from the innards, and in a metal the
  innards are a stacking arrangement rather than a molecule.
THREAD: salt and sugar crystals with their flat faces, ice, geology
  (minerals sorted by how their atoms pack), chocolate tempered into
  the right crystal form.
ASKED-AS: metal atoms crystal structure arrangement inside why bends iron heated pattern

ESSENCE: a piece of metal is not one crystal but millions of small
  ones packed against each other at random angles, like a dry stone
  wall. The joins between them are as important as the crystals — most
  of what happens to a metal, good and bad, happens at those joins.
ROOT: chemistry / a metal freezes from many separate starting points
  at once, so the crystals grow until they collide and stop.
THREAD: dry stone walls, a frozen puddle showing its pattern, cracked
  mud, a paved road of many slabs behaving unlike one poured slab.
ASKED-AS: grains metal structure crystals boundaries etched pattern microscope broken surface

ESSENCE: the smaller the crystals, the stronger and the tougher the
  metal — this is the one improvement that raises both at once, and
  almost nothing else in materials does. It is bought by cooling fast,
  by working the metal, or by adding an element that gets in the way
  of growth.
ROOT: chemistry / bending happens by slip inside a crystal, and every
  boundary is a wall that slip has to stop at, so more boundaries
  means more walls.
THREAD: concrete with well-graded aggregate, packed snow against loose
  snow, cooking (fast-frozen food keeping its texture because its ice
  crystals stayed small).
ASKED-AS: grain size fine strong tough overheated weld coarse cooling fast metal ruined

ESSENCE: metals bend instead of shattering because their crystals do
  not slide as whole blocks. A tiny fault in the stacking moves
  through instead, shifting one row of atoms at a time — like moving a
  heavy carpet by walking a ripple across it rather than dragging the
  whole thing.
ROOT: chemistry / breaking every bond in a plane at once needs an
  enormous force, while breaking a few at a time along a travelling
  fault needs very little.
THREAD: moving a rug by a ripple, a caterpillar's walk, a queue
  shuffling forward one person at a time, zips.
ASKED-AS: metal bends why not shatter ductile dislocation slip permanent deformation atoms rows

ESSENCE: bending a metal makes it harder to bend again — the faults
  that carry the bending pile up and jam each other. That is why a
  wire coathanger gets stiff where you worked it and then snaps there,
  and why metal that is being formed has to be softened partway
  through.
ROOT: chemistry / the travelling faults are what allow bending, and
  once they are tangled they cannot move, so the metal has spent its
  own means of giving way.
THREAD: bending a paperclip back and forth, kneaded dough going
  springy, work-hardened brass shell cases, jewellery hammered stiff.
ASKED-AS: coathanger snapped bending stiff work hardening cold worked wire brittle spot metal

ESSENCE: heating a worked metal and letting it cool slowly lets the
  atoms rearrange, the jammed faults clear out, and new fresh crystals
  grow — the metal is soft again and can be worked again. It is the
  reset button, and it erases the strength that the working built.
ROOT: chemistry / atoms move when warm, and given enough warmth and
  time they will always rearrange toward the lower-energy, tidier
  state.
THREAD: copper pipe softened in a flame to be bent, silversmithing,
  glass annealed slowly so it does not fly apart, sleep and repair.
ASKED-AS: annealing soften metal heat slow cool copper pipe bend anneal reset

ESSENCE: a part can be under great stress with nothing whatever
  hanging on it. Uneven cooling, welding, machining and bending all
  leave the inside pulling against itself — so a block cut in half can
  spring out of shape, and a part can crack sitting on a shelf.
ROOT: physics / when one region cools or is stretched more than its
  neighbours and then sets, the mismatch has nowhere to go and stays
  locked in.
THREAD: toughened glass exploding weeks later, a warped board after
  ripping it lengthways, a bent shaft that was straight until it was
  machined, welded frames pulling themselves crooked.
ASKED-AS: warped after machining stress relief distortion cut sprang cracked shelf welded moved

ESSENCE: an alloy is a pure metal with a named weakness deliberately
  patched — chromium against rust, carbon for hardness, nickel for
  toughness in the cold, magnesium to stiffen aluminium. Nobody
  invents an alloy in general; each one is an answer to one specific
  complaint.
ROOT: chemistry / a foreign atom in the stacking distorts it locally,
  and that distortion blocks the slip that would otherwise let the
  metal give way.
THREAD: cooking (salt does more than flavour), medicine (a drug with
  its side effects), concrete admixtures, glass with lead or boron
  added for a purpose.
ASKED-AS: alloy stainless brass bronze added element why mixed metals pure improve property

ESSENCE: a pure metal melts at one temperature; most alloys melt over
  a range, going slushy before they go liquid. That range governs
  casting, welding, and soldering, and it is why an alloy can be half
  frozen and completely useless while still looking solid.
ROOT: chemistry / two kinds of atom freeze out at different points, so
  the mixture separates as it cools rather than setting all at once.
THREAD: solder that stays pasty and can be wiped, salted roads, ice
  cream freezing over a range, cheese behaving differently as it warms.
ASKED-AS: melting point range alloy solder pasty mushy casting hot short weld

ESSENCE: some alloys get stronger simply by sitting there. Heated,
  quenched, and then left warm for hours, tiny particles form
  throughout and block the slip — aluminium aircraft alloys arrive
  soft, are shaped, and then harden over days without being touched.
ROOT: chemistry / a fast cool traps more of one element dissolved than
  the metal can hold, and it comes back out as fine particles when
  given a little time and warmth.
THREAD: honey crystallising out of solution, kidney stones, glass
  ceramics grown deliberately cloudy, a cake setting after it comes
  out.
ASKED-AS: aluminium hardens over time age heat treated t6 aircraft alloy rivets soft

ESSENCE: steel is iron with a little carbon in it, and the carbon sets
  the ceiling while the heat treatment decides where under that
  ceiling the part lands. Below about a quarter of a per cent, no
  amount of quenching will harden it — which is why structural steel
  cannot be hardened and tool steel can.
ROOT: chemistry / hardening depends on trapping carbon in the wrong
  stacking as the iron restacks itself, so with almost no carbon there
  is nothing to trap.
THREAD: cooking (the same flour, different bread depending on what is
  in it), files and springs cut from one bar, rebar against a chisel.
ASKED-AS: steel carbon content mild tool harden bolt file spring grade quench

ESSENCE: quenching and tempering are two separate operations doing
  opposite things. The quench freezes the steel into a hard, strained,
  brittle state that is nearly useless on its own; the tempering
  reheats it just enough to let some strain out — and the temperature
  chosen is where you decide how much hardness to trade for toughness.
ROOT: chemistry / a fast cool traps carbon where it does not fit, and
  gentle reheating lets a controlled amount of it move back out again.
THREAD: cooking (searing and then resting), glass annealed after
  forming, a knife's edge and its spine wanting different answers.
ASKED-AS: quench temper oil water hardness brittle knife heat treatment cracked colours

ESSENCE: the quench only reaches so far in. A thin part cools right
  through and hardens throughout; a thick one hardens at the skin and
  stays soft in the middle whatever the recipe says. So the same steel
  in two sizes is two different parts.
ROOT: physics / heat leaves through the surface, so the middle of a
  thick section cools slowly no matter how cold the bath is.
THREAD: cooking (a thick steak, seared outside and raw within),
  concrete pours generating heat in their middles, freezing a large
  joint of meat.
ASKED-AS: thick section hardened through middle soft hardenability alloy steel size quench

ESSENCE: many parts want two different materials in one body — hard
  where they rub and tough underneath. So the surface is treated
  separately: carbon or nitrogen driven into the skin, a plated layer,
  or the surface hammered with shot to squeeze it. The properties that
  matter live in the outermost fraction of a millimetre.
ROOT: engineering / wear and cracking both start at the surface, so a
  skin treatment buys most of the benefit for a small fraction of the
  material.
THREAD: gears and camshafts, anodised aluminium, springs peened to
  hold their surface in compression, bread with a crust.
ASKED-AS: case hardened nitrided plated shot peened surface skin depth wear reground

ESSENCE: stainless steel does not resist rust by being noble — it
  rusts instantly, in a layer a few atoms thick, and that invisible
  film then seals the surface. Scratch it and it re-forms in seconds,
  provided there is oxygen there to do it with.
ROOT: chemistry / chromium's own oxide is dense and sticks fast, unlike
  iron's oxide, which is loose and flakes off and lets the next layer
  be attacked.
THREAD: aluminium's identical trick, a wound scabbing over, paint that
  works by sticking rather than by being tough, galvanised coatings.
ASKED-AS: stainless steel rust proof passive layer scratched chloride pitting seaside pool oxygen

ESSENCE: stainless fails where oxygen cannot reach — under a washer,
  inside a crevice, beneath a deposit, in a tight joint that stays
  damp. The film cannot rebuild there, and the corrosion then runs
  fast in a place nobody can see, while every visible surface stays
  bright.
ROOT: chemistry / the protection is a repair process that needs a
  supply, so anywhere the supply is cut the material is defenceless.
THREAD: swimming pool roof failures, boat rigging cracking at the
  swage, teeth decaying between rather than on top, damp under a mat.
ASKED-AS: crevice corrosion under washer stainless failed hidden joint damp pitting rigging bolt

ESSENCE: aluminium is a very reactive metal that behaves like an inert
  one, because it grows a tough oxide skin the instant it meets air.
  That skin is the entire reason it survives — and it is also why
  welding it is difficult and why glue and paint need the surface
  prepared within minutes.
ROOT: chemistry / refined metal wants to return to ore, and here the
  first stage of returning happens so fast and so tightly that it
  stops the rest.
THREAD: stainless steel's identical answer, anodising (the same skin
  grown thicker on purpose), a sliced apple browning, ladders that
  chalk your hands.
ASKED-AS: aluminium oxide skin anodised welding difficult corrosion white powder paint adhesion

ESSENCE: galvanising is not paint. The zinc is there to be eaten
  first — it is less noble than the steel, so as long as any zinc
  remains nearby, the steel underneath does not rust even where the
  coating is scratched right through.
ROOT: chemistry / two different metals in wet make a battery, and the
  poorer one dissolves; galvanising is that fact turned deliberately
  against itself.
THREAD: zinc lumps bolted to boat hulls, sacrificial anodes in water
  heaters, a bodyguard, farming (a trap crop planted to be eaten).
ASKED-AS: galvanised zinc coating scratched rust anode sacrificial hot dip protection consumed

ESSENCE: when two different metals touch in wet, one dissolves — and
  how fast depends on their relative areas. A small stainless screw in
  a big steel plate is nearly harmless; a small steel screw in a big
  stainless plate is eaten in a season. The little one always loses,
  and the bigger its partner, the faster.
ROOT: chemistry / the whole current of the battery flows through both
  parts, so a small dissolving area carries the same total attack
  concentrated into very little metal.
THREAD: aluminium boats with the wrong fittings, copper pipe joined to
  steel, gutters and their nails, dentistry (two metals in one mouth).
ASKED-AS: dissimilar metals corrosion screws aluminium stainless steel contact wet area ratio

ESSENCE: some metals crack under a load they could carry forever, and
  in a chemical they could sit in forever, because they meet both at
  once. Neither the stress nor the fluid alone would do it — the
  cracking exists only where they overlap, which is why it surprises
  everybody.
ROOT: chemistry / a chemical attack that would be harmless on a flat
  surface concentrates at the tip of a crack, where the stretched
  bonds are already weakest.
THREAD: brass cartridge cases cracking in ammonia, stainless in hot
  chlorides, polymers crazing under stress in a solvent, teeth again.
ASKED-AS: cracked suddenly chemical stress corrosion brass ammonia chloride no warning shiny broke

ESSENCE: hydrogen is small enough to walk into steel and make it
  brittle from the inside. It arrives from plating, pickling, welding
  with damp rods, or cathodic protection — and the part usually breaks
  hours or days later, sitting still, with nothing having happened to
  it.
ROOT: chemistry / a hydrogen atom fits between the iron atoms, gathers
  where the metal is most stretched, and stops the slip that would
  otherwise let the metal give way.
THREAD: welders keeping their rods in ovens, gas cylinders, a bruise
  that shows the next day, delayed failures generally.
ASKED-AS: bolt snapped later hydrogen embrittlement plating baking high strength delayed brittle

ESSENCE: copper conducts electricity better than anything affordable,
  and its price is why the world's power lines are aluminium instead —
  aluminium conducts worse per volume and far better per pound, and
  a cable hanging between towers is paying for weight, not for volume.
ROOT: chemistry / conduction depends on how freely electrons move
  through the stacking, and that is fixed by the metal itself and
  cannot be much improved by anything else.
THREAD: heat conduction following the same order, silver in contacts,
  gold on connectors (poor conductor, perfect at not corroding).
ASKED-AS: copper wire aluminium cable conductivity power lines cost weight house wiring joints

ESSENCE: metal feels cold and wood at the same temperature does not.
  Nothing about the temperature differs — the metal simply pulls heat
  out of your hand faster than your body replaces it, so what a hand
  reports is a rate of loss rather than a temperature.
ROOT: physics / touch senses heat leaving the skin, and how fast it
  leaves depends on the material's conductivity, not on how warm it is.
THREAD: tiled bathroom floors, a metal bench in winter, cooking (a
  thick pan spreading heat), the body (wind carrying heat away faster).
ASKED-AS: metal feels cold touch wood same temperature conducts heat handle floor tiles

ESSENCE: two materials joined face to face swell at different rates
  when they warm, so the joint is loaded by the weather every single
  day of its life. Where the mismatch is large enough, nothing else
  matters — the bond simply works itself apart over years.
ROOT: physics / heat makes atoms jostle wider, and each material has
  its own amount of widening, fixed by its insides.
THREAD: a bimetal strip in a thermostat — this fault used on purpose,
  fillings cracking teeth, tiles lifting off a heated slab, enamel on
  steel, coatings crazing.
ASKED-AS: coating cracked peeled expansion mismatch glass metal seal bimetal tiles lifted

ESSENCE: steel that bends all day at room temperature can shatter like
  glass in the cold. The change is not gradual — there is a
  temperature below which the material stops giving way and starts
  breaking, and it can sit inside the range a structure will actually
  meet.
ROOT: chemistry / the travelling faults that carry bending need thermal
  jostling to move, so cooling far enough leaves the metal with no way
  to give except by cracking.
THREAD: Liberty ships splitting in North Atlantic winters, the Titanic
  plate argument, plastic garden furniture snapping in frost, rubber
  seals failing on a cold launch morning.
ASKED-AS: cold brittle steel shattered winter transition temperature ductile toughness impact freezing

ESSENCE: the notch is what kills. File 15's law says a sharp corner
  multiplies the force at its tip — what decides whether that matters
  is whether the material can flow a little at the tip and blunt it. A
  tough metal shrugs off a scratch that would split a hard one in half.
ROOT: chemistry / a material that can give way locally spreads the
  crowded force over a wider area, and one that cannot must carry all
  of it on a few bonds.
THREAD: cutting glass by scoring it, tearing packaging along a nick,
  aircraft windows rounded after square ones tore, keyways and
  splines placed where the bending is least.
ASKED-AS: notch sensitive scratch crack sharp corner brittle tough shattered stamped mark

ESSENCE: hard means resisting a scratch or a dent; tough means
  absorbing a blow without breaking. They are different questions and
  they usually pull against each other — glass is hard and shatters, a
  soft copper pipe is easily dented and very hard to break.
ROOT: chemistry / hardness comes from stopping slip and toughness comes
  from allowing it, so the same feature that supplies one denies the
  other.
THREAD: a glass-hard file that snaps when dropped, ceramic knives,
  body armour built in layers because no one material does both, boxing
  (a chin against a jaw).
ASKED-AS: hard tough difference hardness shatter dent scratch brittle impact strength material

ESSENCE: how strong a material is and how stiff it is are two separate
  properties, and people mix them up constantly. Every steel — cheap
  mild bar and the finest alloy — is exactly as stiff as every other,
  so a beam that sags is not cured by a better grade, only by more
  depth or a different material.
ROOT: chemistry / stiffness comes from the strength of the bonds
  themselves, which alloying and heat treatment barely touch, while
  strength comes from what stops the slip, which they change enormously.
THREAD: making (stiffness is geometry, not material), aluminium being a
  third as stiff and a third the weight, bicycle frames, a diving board.
ASKED-AS: stronger steel still bends stiffness flex sagging grade upgrade beam bouncy floor

ESSENCE: a big piece of a brittle material is weaker than a small piece
  of the same stuff, because strength is set by the worst flaw in it
  and a bigger piece has had more chances to contain one. Strength in
  such materials is not a number, it is a distribution with a size
  attached.
ROOT: chance / a failure begins at the single worst defect, so the more
  material there is, the more likely a bad one is present somewhere.
THREAD: a long chain and its worst link, rope, timber graded by its
  defects, quality control by sampling.
ASKED-AS: big part weaker strength scatter flaws statistics test coupon glass ceramic size

ESSENCE: metal dies by repetition, and for steel there is a stress low
  enough that it never dies at all — below that line the cycles simply
  do not count. Aluminium has no such line: every cycle costs
  something, so an aluminium structure has a life, and the life is
  counted in flights and landings rather than in years.
ROOT: engineering / fatigue is a crack growing a hair at a time, and in
  some metals a low enough stress cannot start one in the first place.
THREAD: aircraft retired by flight count, bicycle frames in aluminium
  against steel, springs designed to sit below the line, bridge hangers.
ASKED-AS: fatigue limit aluminium life cycles steel endurance aircraft flights frame cracked

ESSENCE: "hot" is not a thermometer reading, it is a fraction of the
  material's melting point. Lead is hot at room temperature and creeps
  in your hand; tungsten is cold at the glow of a lamp filament. Every
  question about slow sagging under load is really a question about how
  near the melting point the thing is living.
ROOT: chemistry / atoms rearrange under a steady load once they have
  enough thermal jostling, and how much is enough is set by the
  temperature that unsticks them entirely.
THREAD: making (creep counted in hours where fatigue counts cycles),
  lead roofs that ripple downhill, glaciers, turbine blades built with
  a single crystal to slow it down.
ASKED-AS: creep sagging over time plastic clip lead roof turbine hot melting fraction

ESSENCE: pouring a metal into a mould lets you make almost any shape at
  once, and it gives you the metal at its weakest — the crystals grow
  inward from the cold mould walls and meet in the middle, and gas and
  shrinkage leave holes exactly where the last liquid was.
ROOT: chemistry / a casting freezes from the outside in, so the metal's
  structure is a record of how the heat left the mould.
THREAD: ice forming on a pond from the edges, bread with a hollow
  crumb, chocolate moulding, geology (crystals growing into a cavity).
ASKED-AS: casting porosity shrinkage mould shape complex weak cast iron aluminium bubbles

ESSENCE: forging beats and squeezes hot metal into shape, and the
  crystal structure flows around the form like grain in a tree that
  grew that way. A forged hook has its fibres following its curve; a
  machined one has them cut clean through, and it breaks at a fraction
  of the load.
ROOT: chemistry / working hot metal both refines the crystals and
  aligns their pattern along the direction of flow.
THREAD: wood grain following a branch, a rope's fibres running its
  length, crankshafts and connecting rods, hand-forged tools.
ASKED-AS: forged versus cast machined grain flow strength crankshaft hook billet die

ESSENCE: machining is the only method that starts from a solid piece
  and takes away — so it is the most accurate, the most wasteful, and
  the one that cuts through whatever structure the material already
  had. It reveals the inside and it does not improve it.
ROOT: making / material removal cannot add a property, so a machined
  part is exactly as good as the bar it came from and no better.
THREAD: sculpture from a block, a cut log showing knots that were
  invisible, cooking (trimming does not improve the meat), quarrying.
ASKED-AS: machined billet cut from solid waste accuracy expensive stock quality moved finishing

ESSENCE: bending sheet metal has two rules that beat every intention —
  it cracks if the bend is tighter than the material allows, and it
  springs partway back when released. So the tool has to be made to
  overbend by an amount that depends on the exact batch of metal.
ROOT: chemistry / the outside of a bend is stretched past yield while
  the material near the middle is only sprung, so the sprung part pulls
  the bend open again when the force goes.
THREAD: folding cardboard along a scored line, bending a paperclip
  further than you want it, hemming, plaster that cracks on a tight
  curve.
ASKED-AS: bending sheet metal cracked radius springback press brake angle overbend thickness

ESSENCE: a weld is a small casting poured into a groove, and beside it
  is a band of metal that got hot enough to change and was never meant
  to. That band — not the weld itself — is where most welded structures
  fail: it has coarser grains, different hardness, and stresses locked
  in as it cooled.
ROOT: chemistry / heat rearranges the innards, and a welding torch
  applies a full heat treatment to a strip of metal nobody specified.
THREAD: making (a joint being where things break), a scar's different
  texture, soldered electronics warping a board, forging repairs.
ASKED-AS: weld failed beside seam heat affected zone soft hard cracked aluminium annealed

ESSENCE: welding pulls things crooked. The molten pool shrinks as it
  freezes and drags everything toward itself, so a frame welded in the
  wrong order comes out of the jig bent, with the stresses that bent it
  still inside.
ROOT: physics / cooling metal contracts, and a joint cooling against
  material that is already cold has nowhere to take up the shortening.
THREAD: sewing a long seam and finding the fabric puckered, a road
  slab curling, drying timber, tightening wheel nuts in a star pattern.
ASKED-AS: welded frame bent distortion pulled sequence tack clamps straighten warped shrinkage

ESSENCE: brazing and soldering join metals without melting them — a
  different, lower-melting filler is drawn into a close gap and it
  sticks by wetting the surfaces. The joint's strength comes from the
  fit and the cleanliness, and a wide gap makes a weak joint however
  much filler is added.
ROOT: chemistry / a liquid climbs into a narrow gap by itself, and a
  thin layer of a soft metal between two stiff faces is far stronger
  than a thick one.
THREAD: capillary action in a paper towel, plumbing joints, bicycle
  frames brazed at their lugs, jewellery, glue lines that work the same
  way.
ASKED-AS: soldering brazing flux capillary gap fit clean joint plumbing filler wetting

ESSENCE: some parts are never melted at all — a powder is pressed into
  shape and heated until the particles weld to each other where they
  touch. It makes complicated shapes cheaply and in materials that
  cannot be cast, and it leaves fine holes throughout by its nature.
ROOT: chemistry / atoms move across a contact point when hot, so
  touching particles fuse without any of them becoming liquid.
THREAD: firing pottery, ice grains bonding into a snowball, tungsten
  filaments and carbide tips made no other way, self-lubricating
  bushes.
ASKED-AS: sintered powder metal pressed porous bearing carbide bush oil filled ceramic

ESSENCE: a part built up in layers is strongest along its layers and
  weakest across them — the layer lines are a grain the machine put
  there, and a printed piece will split along them like wood. Which way
  up it was printed is a structural decision, not a convenience.
ROOT: making / each layer bonds to the one below through a partial
  re-melt, so that bond is always weaker than the material within a
  layer.
THREAD: plywood (grain crossed on purpose to cure this), laminated
  timber, wood splitting along its grain, brickwork courses.
ASKED-AS: 3d printed layers split orientation weak direction print bed strength anisotropic

ESSENCE: ceramics are magnificent under squeeze and hopeless under
  stretch — often ten times stronger one way than the other. Their
  bonds cannot slip at all, so there is no bending and no warning: they
  carry the load and then they are in pieces.
ROOT: chemistry / the bonds are rigid and directional, so nothing can
  shift a row of atoms sideways, and the only response left to an
  overload is a crack running.
THREAD: arches in stone and brick, engine liners and brake discs,
  concrete's identical problem, teeth (hard enamel over a giving core).
ASKED-AS: ceramic brittle compression tension tile cracked no warning shatter porcelain strong

ESSENCE: glass is not a slow liquid — the old story about cathedral
  windows thickening at the bottom is wrong. It is a solid whose atoms
  never got the chance to line up as they cooled, and it breaks not
  because it is weak but because every surface has invisible scratches
  and a crack starts at the worst of them.
ROOT: chemistry / a fast cool freezes the disorder of the liquid in
  place, and with no crystal structure there are no travelling faults,
  so nothing can blunt a crack tip.
THREAD: cutting glass by scoring and snapping, optical fibre coated the
  instant it is drawn, a chipped windscreen spreading, obsidian.
ASKED-AS: glass flows liquid myth cathedral windows scratch breaks strength surface amorphous

ESSENCE: toughened glass is ordinary glass with its outer skin
  deliberately squeezed. A crack cannot start while the surface is in
  compression, so it takes far more to break — and once something
  reaches past the skin, the whole stored squeeze is released at once
  and the sheet turns to gravel in a moment.
ROOT: physics / cracks only run under stretch, so pre-loading the
  surface with squeeze means an applied load must first cancel that
  before it can do any harm.
THREAD: prestressed concrete (identical idea in another material), shot
  peening a spring, a strung bow, laminated windscreens choosing the
  opposite trade.
ASKED-AS: toughened tempered glass shattered pieces drilling cut cannot safety prestressed skin

ESSENCE: a composite is stiff fibres held in a soft glue, and it is
  strong only along the fibres. Across them it has nothing but the
  glue. Every composite design is therefore a decision about which
  directions matter, taken layer by layer, and getting the directions
  wrong wastes the material entirely.
ROOT: building / two jobs split between two materials — the fibre takes
  the pull and the matrix holds the fibres in line and passes load
  between them.
THREAD: wood, bone, fibreglass hulls, carbon bicycle frames, plywood
  and its crossed layers, a rope with all fibres in one direction.
ASKED-AS: carbon fibre glass composite layers direction weave strong across resin failed

ESSENCE: composites hide their damage. A knock that would leave a
  visible dent in metal leaves a composite looking untouched while its
  layers have separated inside — and the part may have lost most of its
  compression strength without a mark on it.
ROOT: chemistry / the fibres survive the blow and the matrix between the
  layers splits, and that split is internal, flat, and invisible from
  the surface.
THREAD: a bruise under unbroken skin, delaminated plywood, concrete
  spalling from within, medicine (internal injury with no external
  sign).
ASKED-AS: composite damage invisible impact delamination carbon inspection tap test bruise repair

ESSENCE: concrete does not dry — it reacts. Water is an ingredient that
  is chemically consumed as the cement grows crystals through the mix,
  which is why fresh concrete is kept wet rather than allowed to dry,
  and why it goes on gaining strength for years.
ROOT: chemistry / cement and water form a new mineral, so removing the
  water stops the reaction rather than completing it.
THREAD: bread proving and needing time, plaster setting hot, epoxy
  curing rather than drying, geology (minerals forming from solution).
ASKED-AS: concrete curing wet drying strength time frost early water reaction sets

ESSENCE: reinforced concrete is designed to be cracked. The concrete
  splits wherever it is stretched — that is the point at which the
  steel takes over — so the design controls how wide the cracks get,
  not whether they happen. What actually protects the steel is not the
  concrete's tightness but its chemistry: fresh concrete is strongly
  alkaline, and steel does not rust in it.
ROOT: chemistry / the alkalinity holds a protective film on the steel,
  exactly as chromium does in stainless, until something neutralises it.
THREAD: stainless steel's passive film, spalling car park decks in
  salted cities, teeth and enamel, timber preservative depth.
ASKED-AS: concrete cracks rebar cover depth spalling carbonation salt rust reinforcement alkaline

ESSENCE: a polymer is a plate of tangled spaghetti — very long
  molecules with strong bonds along them and weak ones between. Almost
  every property comes from what those chains can do: how tangled they
  are, whether they can slide, and whether they are tied to each other.
ROOT: chemistry / the innards decide behaviour, and here the innards
  are long chains rather than a stacking pattern.
THREAD: cooked spaghetti against dry, wool fibres, a ball of string, DNA
  and proteins built on the same long-chain trick.
