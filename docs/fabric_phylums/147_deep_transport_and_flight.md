# 147 DEEP TRANSPORT AND FLIGHT — moving people and things
Every vehicle is an argument with a medium. Road, rail, water and air
each set their own toll, and the shape of the machine, the cost of
the journey and the size of the network all fall out of which medium
was chosen. These are the laws under all of it.

ESSENCE: one number decides almost everything about transport: how
  much energy it takes to move a tonne one kilometre. Rank ships,
  trains, lorries, cars and aircraft by that figure and you have
  predicted their costs, their routes, their emissions and which goods
  can afford them.
ROOT: energy / moving a mass against resistance costs work, and the
  price per unit of useful transport is what a whole industry is
  organised around.
THREAD: energy (fuel per kilogram deciding what can fly), money (unit
  cost driving structure), farming (why grain travels and strawberries
  do not), the living world (an animal's cost of travel).
ASKED-AS: energy per tonne kilometre ship train lorry plane cheapest freight cost move

ESSENCE: two resistances fight every vehicle, and they swap places
  with speed. Rolling resistance is roughly steady, so it dominates
  when slow; air drag grows with the square of speed, so it takes over
  and then runs away with everything. That crossover sets every
  sensible speed limit ever chosen.
ROOT: flight and fluids / drag is the fluid's toll and grows with the
  square of speed, while a rolling contact's loss barely depends on it.
THREAD: cycling (a rider's own body being most of the drag), transport
  (why lorries are governed), energy (a turbine's cube law), the body
  (swimming's cost rising sharply with pace).
ASKED-AS: rolling resistance air drag speed square cube fuel motorway slow town streamlining crossover

ESSENCE: steel wheel on steel rail is the lowest rolling resistance in
  practical transport — a train's wheels barely deform, so a modest
  engine can pull thousands of tonnes. Everything good and everything
  awkward about rail comes from that one fact.
ROOT: motion and force / rolling loss comes from a contact deforming
  and springing back, and two hard steel surfaces deform almost not at
  all.
THREAD: motion and force (rolling against sliding), building (a
  prepared path bought once and used forever), farming (a cart track
  against a made road), transport (canals for the same reason).
ASKED-AS: rail efficient steel wheel low friction heavy trains gradient grip slip track

ESSENCE: rail and water are cheap because the path is prepared and
  shared; road and air are flexible because the path is free. That
  trade never resolves — the efficient modes must be fed by the
  flexible ones, so almost every real journey uses both.
ROOT: money / a fixed cost paid once must be spread over traffic,
  which rewards concentration; a variable cost rewards going straight
  to where the thing is wanted.
THREAD: computing (a backbone and the last hop), energy (a grid and
  the street), people (a motorway and a lane), farming (a mill and the
  farms feeding it).
ASKED-AS: rail flexible road door to door network combined transfer efficient path prepared

ESSENCE: the last mile costs the most because it is where the load
  stops being a load. A container crosses an ocean as one item and
  arrives as a thousand items, each with its own address, its own
  door, its own person who is out — and no vehicle on that leg is ever
  more than a fraction full.
ROOT: mathematics / the number of separate destinations explodes at
  the end of a network, and a vehicle serving many addresses spends
  most of its time not moving.
THREAD: computing (fan-out at the edge of a network), energy (the
  distribution network costing more than the transmission), farming (a
  market against a delivery round), the body (capillaries).
ASKED-AS: last mile delivery expensive door van stops addresses not home branching final leg

ESSENCE: on a short journey the vehicle is barely the point. Loading,
  securing, paperwork, waiting for a slot and unloading swallow the
  time, so making the vehicle faster changes almost nothing — and the
  whole competitive game is about handling rather than speed.
ROOT: mathematics / a total is the sum of its parts, and speeding up
  the part that is not the largest cannot change the total much.
THREAD: engineering practice (accelerating work that is not on the
  critical path), flight (a short flight where the airport dominates),
  cooking (preparation against the cooking time), medicine (a
  procedure's real duration being the room turnaround).
ASKED-AS: loading unloading time short journey terminal handling faster vehicle pointless waiting slot

ESSENCE: the shipping container's invention was not a steel box. It
  was the abolition of touching the contents: once a load is sealed at
  the factory and not opened until the shop, the ship, the crane, the
  train and the lorry can all be redesigned around one shape, and
  handling collapses from days to minutes.
ROOT: making / once parts are made to a shared size, work stops
  needing to be fitted, and a standard's value grows with the number
  of parties who keep it.
THREAD: computing (a protocol everyone implements), making (standard
  fasteners), money (a shared currency), language (a common tongue for
  trade).
ASKED-AS: shipping container standard box ports cranes handling system agreed everyone unloading days

ESSENCE: a timetable is not a plan, it is a solved puzzle. Every
  vehicle, crew, platform, berth and slot must be in the right place
  at once, and each is a constraint on all the others — so a schedule
  that satisfies everything is often barely possible and never unique.
ROOT: mathematics / a set of simultaneous constraints has a solution
  space that shrinks fast as constraints are added, and the shape of
  that space is not obvious from any one rule.
THREAD: engineering practice (a project schedule and its critical
  path), teaching (a school timetable), computing (scheduling work on
  one processor), farming (a harvest with shared machinery).
ASKED-AS: timetable schedule constraints crews platforms slots puzzle adding service reshuffles connections robust

ESSENCE: a tight schedule with no slack is a schedule that cannot
  recover. One delay meets a connection, the connection waits, the
  vehicle is late for its next job, and by the afternoon a two-minute
  problem has become a network-wide collapse — which is why real
  timetables are deliberately padded.
ROOT: chance / independent small variations occur constantly, and a
  system with no absorbing capacity passes each one straight along.
THREAD: engineering practice (schedule pressure turning weaknesses
  on), money (a buffer that looks like waste until it is needed),
  computing (a queue at high utilisation), the body (reserve capacity).
ASKED-AS: delay cascade knock on late connection padding slack recovery timetable collapse buffer

ESSENCE: congestion is not caused by too many vehicles. It is caused
  by a system running close to its capacity, where the smallest
  disturbance takes longer to clear than the gaps between
  disturbances. Below that point delay is negligible; above it, delay
  climbs towards infinity while traffic barely rises.
ROOT: chance / a queue's waiting time depends on utilisation in a way
  that is nearly flat and then vertical, so the last few per cent of
  capacity costs almost all the delay.
THREAD: computing (a server at high load), the body (a blocked
  passage), engineering practice (a critical path with no float),
  people (a queue at one till).
ASKED-AS: traffic jam congestion capacity queue sudden vertical delay ninety percent flow slower

ESSENCE: build a wider road and it fills. People choose journeys
  against a rough budget of travel time, so any improvement in speed
  is spent on going further, more often, or at a worse hour — and the
  new road returns to the same speed with more people on it.
ROOT: money / lowering the price of something raises the amount
  demanded, and travel time is the price of a journey.
THREAD: energy (efficiency raising consumption), computing (more disk
  filling with more files), building (more storage producing more
  clutter), money (a subsidy absorbed by prices).
ASKED-AS: induced demand wider road fills traffic same speed more journeys travel time budget

ESSENCE: the four-stroke engine takes four strokes to do one useful
  thing. Suck, squeeze, bang, blow — and only the bang pushes. About a
  quarter of the fuel's energy reaches the wheels; the rest leaves as
  hot exhaust, hot coolant, friction and pumping the air in and out.
ROOT: heat and electricity / an engine harvests a fall from hot to
  cold and cannot take all of it, and the best possible share is fixed
  by the two temperatures alone.
THREAD: heat and electricity (the heat engine's ceiling), energy (the
  chain of conversions each keeping part), cooking (heat lost around
  the pan), the body (metabolism's own efficiency).
ASKED-AS: four stroke engine efficiency exhaust heat wasted quarter compression radiator losses fuel

ESSENCE: an engine is only good over a narrow band of speeds, and a
  vehicle needs to move from stationary to fast. Gears exist to keep
  the engine in its happy band while the wheels do something else
  entirely — which is why an electric motor, happy nearly everywhere,
  mostly does without them.
ROOT: motion and force / gears trade turns for twist at the ratio of
  their teeth, so the same power can be delivered as much torque
  slowly or little torque quickly.
THREAD: motion and force (a lever's bargain), cycling (a rider's
  cadence being the thing kept constant), making (a drill's speed
  selector), the body (stride length against rate).
ASKED-AS: gears gearbox why needed engine band torque speed ratio electric motor cadence

ESSENCE: braking is deliberate destruction. All the energy put in by
  the engine is turned to heat in the discs and thrown away — so a
  vehicle that stops often burns fuel to make warmth. Regeneration
  runs the motor backwards and puts some of it back, and only some.
ROOT: heat and electricity / a generator makes the shaft harder to
  turn in exact proportion to what is drawn, so slowing a vehicle can
  charge a battery instead of a brake disc.
THREAD: heat and electricity (induction read backwards), the body
  (eccentric muscle work absorbing energy), motion and force (energy
  as a currency that changes pocket), money (a refund at a discount).
ASKED-AS: braking heat discs wasted regenerative recovers battery city driving motorway pointless energy

ESSENCE: a tyre has one budget of grip and it can be spent on turning,
  on accelerating, or on stopping — in any mixture, but never more
  than the total. Ask for full braking and full cornering at once and
  you get neither, which is the whole of vehicle handling in one
  sentence.
ROOT: motion and force / friction has a maximum set by how hard the
  surfaces are pressed together, and that maximum is shared across
  every direction at once.
THREAD: motion and force (friction's two settings), the body (a foot
  planting in a turn), sport (an athlete changing direction), making
  (a clamp's holding force shared between loads).
ASKED-AS: tyre grip circle braking cornering same time slides limit traction friction budget

ESSENCE: which end of a car loses grip first is a design decision, and
  it decides how the car behaves when a driver asks too much.
  Understeer means the nose washes wide, which feels alarming and is
  survivable; the other way round spins the vehicle, which feels
  dramatic and is not.
ROOT: engineering practice / a designer nominates not only where a
  system fails but how it behaves as it approaches, and the edge
  behaviour is where the safety lives.
THREAD: engineering practice (graceful degradation and warning before
  failure), flight (an aircraft designed to drop its nose in a stall),
  sailing (a boat that rounds up rather than capsizes).
ASKED-AS: understeer oversteer car slides front rear spins limit handling safe designed washes

ESSENCE: air lifts cars as well as wings. At speed, the flow over a
  body generates forces the designer did not necessarily want — lift
  at the front, a wandering rear, a lorry pulling a car towards it —
  so fast vehicles are shaped as much to press themselves down as to
  slip through.
ROOT: flight and fluids / faster flow means lower pressure, so any
  body forcing air to go further over one side than the other is
  pushed toward the fast side whether or not it was meant to be.
THREAD: flight and fluids (lift as deflection), sport (a cyclist in
  another's wake), building (wind loads on a tall structure), sailing.
ASKED-AS: downforce lift car spoiler drag racing wing overtaking lorry buffeting fast shape

ESSENCE: a boat that floats by displacing water makes waves as it
  goes, and those waves get longer as it speeds up. When the wave is
  as long as the hull, the boat is sitting in its own trough and
  climbing a hill it made — which is a wall, not a gradual limit.
ROOT: physics / a wave's speed is set by its length, so a hull pushing
  a wave of its own length is tied to that wave's pace.
THREAD: physics (wave speed and wavelength), the living world (why
  fast swimmers are long), transport (why big ships are economical),
  sport (rowing eights being longer than singles).
ASKED-AS: hull speed boat wall length long ships faster wave trough power displacement limit

ESSENCE: there are two ways to be a boat. Displacement means pushing
  water aside and being held up by buoyancy — efficient, slow, and
  seaworthy. Planing means going fast enough to ride on top of the
  water on a flat bottom — quick, thirsty, and rough in a sea.
ROOT: flight and fluids / a fluid pushes back on a surface deflecting
  it downwards, so above a certain speed the dynamic push exceeds what
  buoyancy was providing.
THREAD: flight and fluids (deflection making lift), transport (speed
  costing energy steeply), the living world (a duck against a
  water-skating insect), sport (powerboats against sailing hulls).
ASKED-AS: planing displacement speedboat rides on top fuel slams waves fast hull bottom

ESSENCE: a propeller is a rotating wing throwing water backwards. Push
  it too hard and the pressure on the front of the blade falls below
  the point where water boils at ambient temperature — bubbles form
  and collapse, the thrust falls away, and the collapsing bubbles eat
  the metal.
ROOT: flight and fluids / faster flow means lower pressure, and water
  boils whenever its pressure falls below its vapour pressure,
  whatever the temperature.
THREAD: flight and fluids (pressure falling where flow speeds up),
  making (material eaten by repeated tiny impacts), medicine
  (ultrasound bubbles used deliberately), plumbing (pump damage).
ASKED-AS: propeller cavitation bubbles boiling low pressure noise damage thrust falls submarine quiet

ESSENCE: an aircraft in steady flight is a balance of four things:
  lift against weight, thrust against drag. Nothing is left over. A
  climb is not extra lift — it is extra thrust, spent on gaining
  height, which is why a heavy aeroplane on a hot day cannot climb
  rather than cannot fly.
ROOT: motion and force / a body in steady motion has all its forces in
  balance, so any change in one must be answered by another.
THREAD: motion and force (balanced books for a thing that stays put),
  cycling (climbing bought from power, not from gear), the body
  (steady effort against accumulating fatigue), sailing.
ASKED-AS: four forces lift weight thrust drag balance climb power heavy hot day

ESSENCE: how far an aircraft can go is decided by how many units of
  lift it gets per unit of drag. That ratio is the aircraft's whole
  character: it sets the glide angle with the engines off, the fuel
  per mile with them on, and the shape of the wings.
ROOT: flight and fluids / lift is bought by deflecting air and drag is
  the price charged, so the ratio between them is what the design is
  actually competing on.
THREAD: flight and fluids (drag as the fluid's toll), the living world
  (an albatross against a sparrow), energy (efficiency compounding
  along a chain), sailing (a rig's drive against its heeling force).
ASKED-AS: lift drag ratio glide range fuel per mile wings long slender glider efficiency

ESSENCE: a wing stalls at an angle, not at a speed. Tilt it past the
  point where the airflow can follow the curve and the flow lets go —
  which can happen fast, slow, level or diving. The speed people quote
  is only the speed at which level flight requires that angle.
ROOT: flight and fluids / flow separates from a surface when asked to
  turn too sharply, and the turning demand is set by the angle to the
  oncoming air.
THREAD: flight and fluids (flow letting go past a critical tilt),
  sailing (a sail luffing when set too close), sport (a swimmer's hand
  losing grip on the water), the mind (a limit misremembered as a
  number).
ASKED-AS: stall angle not speed wing airflow separates turn steep recovery lower nose power

ESSENCE: an aircraft turns about three axes and needs a control for
  each — roll with the ailerons, pitch with the elevator, yaw with the
  rudder. They are not independent: roll makes yaw, yaw makes roll, and
  pitch changes with speed, so flying is a coordination rather than
  three separate levers.
ROOT: mathematics / a rigid body's orientation needs three numbers,
  and rotations about different axes do not simply add.
THREAD: motion and force (spin as an amount and a direction), sailing
  (a rudder needing flow), the body (a coordinated turn while
  running), photography (a gimbal's three axes).
ASKED-AS: ailerons elevator rudder roll pitch yaw three axes turn coordinated flat control

ESSENCE: an aircraft can be built to return to its attitude when
  disturbed, or to hold whatever it is pointed at. Stability makes a
  docile machine that resists the pilot; instability makes an agile
  one that must be flown every second — and modern fighters are the
  second, kept flyable only by computers.
ROOT: motion and force / a resting state is stable if a nudge produces
  a restoring effect, and a designer can place the centre of gravity
  and the tail so that it does or does not.
THREAD: motion and force (stability as whether a nudge lifts the
  weight), control (a balancing machine that falls when its loop
  stops), sailing (a hull that rounds up), engineering practice.
ASKED-AS: stability aircraft returns level unstable agile fighter computer fly by wire tail

ESSENCE: a jet engine faces one trade every time: throw a little air
  very fast, or a lot of air gently. The same thrust results — but the
  gentle version wastes far less energy in the air it leaves behind,
  which is why airliners have enormous fans and fighters do not.
ROOT: flight and fluids / everything that moves through fluid moves by
  throwing fluid backwards, and the energy wasted grows with the
  square of how fast it is thrown.
THREAD: flight and fluids (thrust by throwing mass), the living world
  (a whale's slow flukes against a fish's fast tail), transport
  (propellers efficient and speed-limited), energy (waste as squared).
ASKED-AS: jet engine bypass fan thrust efficient fast air thrown backwards airliner fighter

ESSENCE: aircraft climb because the air thins. Thin air means less
  drag for the same speed, so the same fuel goes further — and it also
  means less air to burn, so the engine's power falls. The cruising
  altitude is where those two curves cross for that aircraft.
ROOT: flight and fluids / drag is proportional to the density of the
  fluid, and an engine's thrust falls with the same density that helps
  the airframe.
THREAD: energy (a machine's efficiency depending on its environment),
  the body (thin air and performance at altitude), weather (density
  falling with height), cars (a turbocharger restoring lost air).
ASKED-AS: altitude thin air cruise higher less drag engine power ceiling coffin corner

ESSENCE: an aircraft has one weight limit, and fuel and payload share
  it. Take a full load and you cannot take full fuel; take full fuel
  for a long journey and you must leave cargo behind. Range and
  payload are the same currency, and every route is a point on that line.
ROOT: energy / a vehicle carries its own energy, so fuel is a load and
  a longer journey means less of the load can be anything else.
THREAD: energy (a vehicle's own supply as a load), survival (carrying
  water against carrying distance), making (weight spirals), farming
  (seed eaten against seed sown).
ASKED-AS: range payload tradeoff fuel weight limit cargo long flight less freight curve

ESSENCE: navigation away from landmarks is a chain of substitutions.
  Speed and heading and time give a position that drifts; a star, a
  radio beacon or a satellite corrects it. Every method ever used is
  one of those two, and the whole art is knowing how much your
  estimate has decayed since the last fix.
ROOT: evidence / small errors accumulate without bound, and only an
  external reference resets them.
THREAD: control (localisation and drift), astronomy (position from
  angles to known objects), the mind (losing track of time), farming
  (pacing a field).
ASKED-AS: navigation ocean no landmarks dead reckoning drift fix stars gps beacon error

ESSENCE: an aeroplane needs a strip of prepared ground long enough to
  reach flying speed and to stop again if something fails halfway — so
  the runway, not the aircraft, sets what can operate where. Heat,
  altitude and weight all lengthen the requirement, sometimes by a
  great deal.
ROOT: motion and force / a change of speed takes force multiplied by
  time, so the distance needed grows with the square of the speed to
  be reached.
THREAD: transport (the network's constraint being the terminal),
  building (a fixed asset limiting what can use it), farming (a barn
  door sizing the machinery), engineering practice (the envelope).
ASKED-AS: runway length take off abort stopping distance hot high heavy airport limits

ESSENCE: a helicopter's rotor is a wing that carries its own airflow,
  so it can hover — and pays for it in every other way. Hovering costs
  enormous power, the advancing and retreating blades meet the air at
  wildly different speeds, and that asymmetry caps the forward speed
  of the whole machine.
ROOT: flight and fluids / lift comes from airflow over a surface, and
  a rotor in forward flight has one side moving into the wind and the
  other away from it.
THREAD: flight and fluids (thrust by throwing mass down), the living
  world (a hummingbird's cost of hovering), transport (a mode chosen
  for access rather than efficiency), control (deliberate instability).
ASKED-AS: helicopter hover expensive fuel forward speed limit retreating blade stall rotor asymmetry

ESSENCE: when the engine stops, a helicopter is not a brick. Let it
  descend and the upward flow through the rotor keeps it turning,
  storing energy in the spinning blades, which is then spent in one
  flare at the bottom to arrive gently. It is a glide with the energy
  kept in rotation instead of in height.
ROOT: motion and force / height and speed are the same money in two
  pockets, and a spinning mass is a third pocket that can be filled
  and emptied.
THREAD: motion and force (rotational energy stored and released),
  flight (a glide as an exchange of height for distance), cycling
  (freewheeling down to carry momentum up), sailing.
ASKED-AS: autorotation helicopter engine fails glide rotor spins flare land dead mans curve

ESSENCE: the medium shapes the machine. Water is dense and slow, so
  ships are enormous and unhurried; air is thin and fast, so aircraft
  are light and quick and cannot pause; land is solid, so vehicles are
  small and can stop anywhere. Every difference between the modes
  descends from the stuff they push through.
ROOT: flight and fluids / drag depends on the density of the medium
  and the square of speed, so density decides what speeds and what
  sizes are affordable.
THREAD: the living world (a fish, a bird and a horse built by the same
  logic), making (a design as a settlement with its conditions),
  building (materials chosen by their climate).
ASKED-AS: medium shapes vehicle water air land dense thin ships aircraft amphibious compromise

ESSENCE: bigger ships are cheaper per tonne because the cargo grows
  with the cube of size while the hull's wetted surface grows with the
  square. That single ratio has driven every vessel larger for a
  century, until the ports and canals could not take any more.
ROOT: mathematics / make a shape twice as big in every direction and
  its volume grows eightfold while its skin grows fourfold.
THREAD: motion and force (make a shape bigger and its bones fall
  behind), the living world (why large animals have different
  proportions), building (economies of scale in structures), farming.
ASKED-AS: bigger ships cheaper per container cube square surface canal port draught limits size

ESSENCE: a vehicle's route and its heading are different things. A
  ship in a current and an aircraft in a wind must point away from
  where they are going in order to arrive there, and the amount of
  offset is a triangle nobody can skip.
ROOT: motion and force / motion relative to a moving medium adds to
  the medium's own motion, so the path over the ground is the sum of
  two vectors and not one.
THREAD: navigation (set and drift), the living world (birds
  compensating for wind), swimming (crossing a river), the mind
  (aiming off for a known bias).
ASKED-AS: crosswind drift crab heading course current triangle aim off arrive downwind ship

ESSENCE: a ship is stable because its weight and its buoyancy pull
  against each other with a lever arm that grows as it leans. Load it
  high and the arm shrinks; let a liquid slosh across a wide deck and
  the arm can vanish — which is why a partly filled tank is more
  dangerous than a full one.
ROOT: motion and force / a resting thing is stable if tilting it lifts
  its weight, and the geometry of a hull decides whether it does.
THREAD: motion and force (stability and the centre of gravity), the
  home (a tall bookcase), transport (a loaded lorry rolling), farming
  (a tanker trailer's slosh).
ASKED-AS: ship stability capsize top heavy loading free surface tank half full sloshing lever

ESSENCE: an accident is an energy problem. What injures is the energy
  that has to be absorbed in the stop, and that energy grows with the
  square of speed — so a small increase in speed makes a
  disproportionately larger crash, and every survivable design is
  about stretching the stop out in time.
ROOT: motion and force / kinetic energy goes with the square of speed,
  and the force in a stop is that energy divided by the distance over
  which it is absorbed.
THREAD: motion and force (impulse and time), sport (rolling with a
  fall), making (a design that yields deliberately), the body (a
  helmet's liner crushing once).
ASKED-AS: crash energy speed squared crumple zone stopping distance force survivable stretch impact

ESSENCE: stopping a vehicle takes two distances added together — the
  ground covered while a person notices and reacts, which is roughly
  constant in time, and the braking distance, which grows with the
  square of speed. Doubling the speed far more than doubles the total.
ROOT: the mind / a reaction takes a fixed fraction of a second, and
  during it the vehicle covers a distance proportional to speed.
THREAD: the mind (reaction time as a fixed cost), sport (anticipation
  substituting for reaction), medicine (a delayed response), control
  (delay as the enemy of every loop).
ASKED-AS: stopping distance reaction time thinking braking speed doubles four times wet tyres

ESSENCE: everything a car does passes through four contact patches
  about the size of a hand. All the steering, all the acceleration,
  all the braking and all the stability are transmitted through that
  small area — so tyre condition and pressure matter more than almost
  anything else on the vehicle.
ROOT: motion and force / friction is what couples a wheel to the road,
  and it acts only where the rubber actually touches.
THREAD: motion and force (friction depending on pressing force), the
  body (feet as the only contact in running), making (a joint carrying
  everything), sailing (a keel as the whole grip on the water).
ASKED-AS: tyres contact patch small grip pressure tread aquaplaning water film steering gone

ESSENCE: a suspension's real job is not comfort. It is keeping the
  tyres pressed to a road that is not flat — so the lighter the parts
  that move with the wheel, the faster they can follow a bump, and the
  more of the time the tyre is actually touching anything.
ROOT: motion and force / a mass takes force and time to change its
  motion, so a heavy wheel assembly cannot follow a sharp bump and
  leaves the surface.
THREAD: motion and force (damping fighting speed rather than
  position), the body (a knee absorbing a landing), building
  (isolating a machine from a floor), photography (a stabiliser).
ASKED-AS: suspension grip bumps unsprung weight wheel follows road soft ride firm handling

ESSENCE: weight moves around a vehicle as it accelerates, brakes and
  turns. Braking presses the front tyres down and lightens the rear;
  cornering loads the outside. So the grip available at each wheel is
  changing constantly, and a driver's smoothness is really the
  management of that shifting.
ROOT: motion and force / a force applied at the ground and resisted at
  a centre of gravity above it makes a turning effect, which presses
  one end down and lifts the other.
THREAD: motion and force (turning effect as force times distance), the
  body (leaning into a stop), sport (a rider shifting weight),
  transport (a laden lorry's different behaviour).
ASKED-AS: weight transfer braking front dips rear light cornering outside loaded smooth driver

ESSENCE: a network can be built as everybody-to-everybody or as
  everybody-through-a-hub. The hub fills the vehicles and makes rare
  routes possible; it also adds a change, a wait, and a single place
  where a bad day ruins the whole system.
ROOT: mathematics / connecting many places directly needs a number of
  links growing with the square of the places, while a hub needs one
  link each.
THREAD: computing (a switch against a mesh), the body (blood returning
  through the heart), farming (a market town), people (a broker).
ASKED-AS: hub spoke direct flights connections transfer wait network routes thin empty vehicles

ESSENCE: freight rarely balances. More goes one way than the other, so
  a fleet spends a large fraction of its life moving empty containers,
  empty wagons and empty lorries back to where they will be needed —
  and that repositioning is paid for by the loaded direction.
ROOT: money / a market clears at a price, so an imbalance in flow
  appears as a price difference between the two directions rather than
  as an equal flow.
THREAD: money (asymmetric trade), farming (a lorry that brought feed
  returning empty), people (a commute full one way), energy (a grid
  built for peak flow in one direction).
ASKED-AS: empty containers backhaul return leg imbalance one way freight repositioning cheap direction

ESSENCE: a train cannot stop quickly, so it must not be allowed to
  reach anywhere unsafe. That is why railways run on blocks and
  signals rather than on drivers seeing danger: the safe braking
  distance is far longer than the visible distance, so the protection
  has to be information rather than eyesight.
ROOT: this file / stopping distance grows with the square of speed and
  steel wheels grip poorly, so the distance is measured in kilometres.
THREAD: control (safety as a separate circuit rather than a rule),
  engineering practice (an operator blamed for a design trap),
  transport (a road's sight lines), the mind (attention lapses).
ASKED-AS: train braking distance signals blocks cannot stop sight driver misses automatic protection

ESSENCE: a track gauge is an accident that became a constraint.
  Whatever width the first lines chose, every later wagon, tunnel,
  platform and bridge was built to match — so a country's gauge is
  now effectively permanent, and a neighbour with a different one has
  a border that goods cannot cross without being lifted.

ESSENCE: air traffic is a separation problem, not a speed problem.
  Aircraft must be kept apart in three dimensions and in time, and
  each one leaves a rotating wake behind it that a following aircraft

  may safely be, and nothing else.

ESSENCE: near the ground an aircraft's wing works better. The ground
  interrupts the swirling air that a wing normally sheds, cutting the
  drag that comes with making lift — so a landing aeroplane floats,
  and a heavily loaded one can get airborne in ground effect and be
  unable to climb out of it.

ESSENCE: near the speed of sound the air stops getting out of the way

  swept wings, and why the region just below the sound barrier is
  where the design difficulty lives.

ESSENCE: nothing about a vehicle is more decisive than where the

  ships and not a matter of putting things where they fit.
