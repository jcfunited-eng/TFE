# 152 DEEP ANIMAL HUSBANDRY AND VETERINARY — keeping animals well
The knowledge of people who keep animals alive and of the people
called when that fails. Almost none of it is about medicine: it is
about air, water, footing, space, timing and the eye of whoever walks
the shed at six in the morning. Every entry is a law about a living
thing in a place someone else chose for it, and every one forbids
something.

ESSENCE: a wild animal meets its own needs — it finds water, chooses
  where to lie, walks away from its own dung, leaves a sick
  companion, moves on when the grass is gone. A kept animal can do
  none of these things, so every one of them has become a job that
  somebody must do on its behalf, and the whole trade of husbandry
  is that list of transferred jobs.
ROOT: farming / confinement removes an animal's ability to solve its
  own problems, and the problems do not go away with the ability.
THREAD: the human journey (a hospital patient who cannot fetch a
  glass of water), building (a sealed house needing mechanical
  everything the open one did for free), governance (a duty of care
  arising from having taken control).
ASKED-AS: keeping animals needs penned confined welfare responsibility care wild versus kept shed basics

ESSENCE: every kept animal needs five things, and four of them are
  easy to remember. Enough of the right food and water. A place
  suited to it. Health, or treatment when it is not. Freedom from
  fear and distress. And the fifth, which is the one that gets
  missed: the room and the company to behave the way its kind
  behaves — to root, to perch, to graze, to lie down properly, to be
  with others of its sort.
ROOT: the living world / a behaviour that evolved is a need and not
  a preference, so an animal prevented from performing it is under
  strain even when it is fat and free of disease.
THREAD: the human journey (a prisoner well fed and still harmed),
  the mind (boredom producing real illness), medicine (a patient
  whose numbers are fine and whose life is not).
ASKED-AS: animal welfare five freedoms needs behaviour boredom pacing biting space company happy checklist

ESSENCE: disease is a contest between how much pathogen arrives and
  how well the animal can meet it — and crowding pushes both the
  wrong way at once. More animals in a space means more of whatever
  is circulating, more dung, more damp air, and less room to get
  away from any of it, while the same crowding is itself a stress
  that lowers the resistance meeting it.
ROOT: the living world / an infection depends on dose, and a dose is
  produced by other animals in proportion to how many and how close.
THREAD: public health (crowding and epidemic disease in the same
  relation), farming (a monoculture handing a pest its whole life),
  computing (load and failure rising together).
ASKED-AS: overcrowding stocking density disease outbreak shed pen space per animal coughing spreading treating

ESSENCE: in any animal building, air comes before heat. Animals
  produce a great deal of warmth and an astonishing amount of water
  vapour, ammonia and dust, and shutting the building up to keep
  them warm traps all of the second lot with them. A cold dry shed
  with moving air grows healthy stock; a warm damp still one grows
  pneumonia.
ROOT: weather / warm air holds more water, so a heated closed space
  fills with moisture that then condenses on everything in it.
THREAD: building (condensation inside a sealed wall), the body
  (breathing rate rising to clear waste rather than to gain air),
  ships and mines (ventilation as the first engineering problem).
ASKED-AS: shed ventilation cold draughts calves pneumonia coughing closing doors warm damp air inlet

ESSENCE: the air that matters is the air at the animal's head, and
  the animal's head is near the floor. Ammonia rises off wet bedding
  and sits in the bottom foot of the building, where it burns the
  lining of the airway and opens the door for every virus in the
  shed — and the person inspecting is standing five feet up in
  perfectly good air.
ROOT: chemistry / a gas produced at floor level in a still room
  reaches its highest concentration at floor level.
THREAD: medicine (a workplace exposure limit measured where the
  worker breathes), building (radon and carbon monoxide pooling low),
  the body (an insult to the airway lining preceding infection).
ASKED-AS: ammonia smell shed eyes stinging bedding wet poultry litter floor level air quality

ESSENCE: cold on its own kills very few animals. Cold with wet, or
  cold with a draught, kills a great many — because a wet coat and
  moving air both destroy the layer of still warm air that the
  animal's own hair or feathers were holding. A dry animal out of
  the wind copes with weather that would kill it lying in a puddle
  in a doorway.
ROOT: physics / insulation is trapped still air, so anything that
  wets it or blows it away removes the insulation and not merely
  some of the comfort.
THREAD: survival (the outdoor rule about wet clothing), textiles (a
  compressed or soaked insulating layer being no layer at all),
  building (a cold wall that is only a problem when it is damp).
ASKED-AS: cold weather lambs calves shelter wind wet dying hypothermia dry bedding draught barn

ESSENCE: bedding has four jobs and they are all mechanical. It keeps
  the animal dry, it keeps it warm off a cold floor, it cushions the
  joints of something that lies down for half the day, and it gives
  grip. Wet bedding does the opposite of all four and becomes a warm
  culture for the organisms that cause the udder and foot diseases
  that fill a vet's day.
ROOT: engineering / a surface an animal spends half its life against
  decides its comfort, its temperature and the bacteria at its skin.
THREAD: medicine (pressure sores from a bed rather than a disease),
  cleaning (a damp surface as a growing medium), building (a floor
  as the coldest surface in a room).
ASKED-AS: bedding straw sawdust wet dirty cows lying mastitis hock sores cubicles clean deep

ESSENCE: a floor an animal cannot trust changes everything it does.
  On slippery concrete a cow will not show heat, will not stride out
  and will not turn quickly — so she is missed at breeding, walks
  short and eats less, and one day she does the splits and is gone
  in an afternoon. Grip is not a comfort item, it is the difference
  between a normal animal and a cautious one.
ROOT: the body / an animal that fears falling changes its gait
  first, and every other behaviour that depends on movement changes
  with it.
THREAD: the human journey (an older person's whole life shrinking
  after one fall), transport (a road surface deciding driver
  behaviour), sports (footwear changing what a player will attempt).
ASKED-AS: slippery floor concrete cows slipping grooving heat detection standing legs splits injury grip

ESSENCE: how much feed an animal turns into food differs enormously
  by species, and it is not a matter of quality of stockmanship.
  Poultry and farmed fish convert best, pigs middling, cattle worst
  — and within any species the young convert far better than the
  old, so the last stretch of finishing is always the dearest food
  produced on the farm.
ROOT: the living world / an animal spends feed on staying alive
  before it spends any on growing, so the share left for growth
  falls as the body it must maintain gets bigger.
THREAD: money (rising marginal costs deciding when to stop), the
  body (a trained athlete's last percent of improvement), engineering
  (efficiency falling as a machine approaches its limit).
ASKED-AS: feed conversion ratio pigs chickens cattle efficiency finishing weight cost gain growth expensive last

ESSENCE: you do not feed a cow, you feed the several kilos of
  microbes in her front stomach, and they feed her. Everything
  follows from that: the ration must suit the bugs, changes must be
  slow enough for the bug population to shift, and a sudden load of
  grain lets the acid-makers take over, the pH crashes, the fibre
  digesters die and the animal is ill from a feed that was perfectly
  good.
ROOT: the living world / a population takes time to change its
  makeup, so a change faster than the population can follow is a
  shock rather than a diet.
THREAD: the body (gut flora disrupted by a sudden change or a
  course of antibiotics), farming (soil life needing gradual change),
  people together (a team's culture taking longer to turn than its
  instructions).
ASKED-AS: cow grain acidosis sudden diet change rumen bloat introducing feed slowly bugs scouring concentrate

ESSENCE: fibre in a ruminant's diet is not filler and not roughage
  in the dismissive sense. Long fibre is what makes the animal chew
  again and produce the saliva that keeps its front stomach from
  going acid, and what keeps the mat of digesting material moving.
  Take it out in favour of richer feed and the whole vessel stops
  working properly, however good the numbers on the ration look.
ROOT: the body / a digestive organ is a machine with mechanical
  requirements as well as chemical ones.
THREAD: the body (human digestion needing bulk it gains nothing
  from), engineering (a mixture needing a carrier that does no work
  itself), cooking (a structure that holds the rest together).
ASKED-AS: fibre roughage straw hay cows chewing cud ration concentrate acidosis chopped length rumination

ESSENCE: a pig or a chicken has no fermentation vat to build protein
  for it, so it must be handed every building block ready-made — and
  a ration is therefore a formulation, balanced to specific amino
  acids, not a heap of good ingredients. Its cost tracks the grain
  and oilseed markets directly, which is why the price of pork and
  chicken follows the price of feed within months.
ROOT: the body / an animal that cannot make a required part must
  eat it, so the diet's quality is set by the scarcest part rather
  than by the total protein.
THREAD: food (grain and bean gaps being different gaps), farming (a
  crop limited by its scarcest nutrient), money (a cost structure
  tied to one input's market).
ASKED-AS: pig chicken feed ration protein lysine grain price soya monogastric formulated balanced diet

ESSENCE: water is the feed that decides all the others. An animal
  short of water eats less within hours, and everything else — growth,
  milk, laying, condition — falls with the intake. So the first
  question about a shed of animals doing poorly on good feed is
  always about the trough: how much, how fast, how clean, and how
  many can drink at once.
ROOT: the body / eating requires water to process what is eaten, so
  intake of dry matter is capped by water available.
THREAD: farming (irrigation deciding what fertiliser can do),
  engineering (a system limited by its narrowest supply), people
  (a canteen judged by its queue rather than its menu).
ASKED-AS: water trough flow drinkers intake milk drop dirty blocked animals drinking access queue

ESSENCE: animals refuse water long before it is dangerous. Water
  fouled with dung, warm and green in summer, tasting of a treatment
  or a new tank, or too high in salts, is water they will drink less
  of — and drinking less is the injury, not the contamination
  itself. The trough is therefore checked by eye and nose every day,
  and any change to it is a change to the diet.
ROOT: the living world / an animal's caution about what it swallows
  is a defence, so palatability is a real physiological control and
  not fussiness.
THREAD: medicine (a medicine no patient will take being no
  medicine), food (a ration rejected by taste), computing (a safety
  system switched off because it was annoying).
ASKED-AS: water dirty green algae trough cleaning refusing drink medicating taste salty tank summer

ESSENCE: an animal's fat cover, felt with the hand over the back and
  the tail head, is the single most useful number in husbandry —
  because it reports the balance between what has gone in and what
  has been demanded over weeks, in a way that weight alone does not.
  Two cows of the same weight can be one too thin and one too fat,
  and the difference decides whether either gets in calf.
ROOT: evidence / a measurement that integrates over time tells you
  about management, while a snapshot tells you only about today.
THREAD: medicine (a long-run marker against a single reading),
  money (a balance sheet against a day's takings), the body (body
  composition mattering more than the scales).
ASKED-AS: body condition score thin fat cows ewes feeling backbone tail head scoring calving fertility

ESSENCE: whether an animal is short of copper, selenium, cobalt or
  iodine is usually a question about the ground it stands on. The
  mineral goes soil, plant, animal — so deficiencies are regional and
  they are the same fields every year, which is why local knowledge
  beats a general supplement and why the same problem in the next
  valley may not exist at all.
ROOT: geology / the rock beneath decides the soil's chemistry, and
  the plant can only offer what it could take up.
THREAD: farming (a nutrient locked in soil while the bag says
  plenty), public health (goitre and dental fluorosis mapping onto
  geology), food (a regional diet's characteristic gap).
ASKED-AS: copper selenium cobalt deficiency soil supplement bolus blood test region grass minerals licks

ESSENCE: for several trace elements the gap between too little and
  too much is narrow, and sheep are famously intolerant of copper
  that cattle need. So a mineral lick, a bolus, an injection and a
  supplemented feed can add up to a lethal dose while each was
  correct on its own — and the poisoning appears suddenly, weeks
  later, in an animal that looked well.
ROOT: chemistry / a dose makes the medicine or the poison, and where
  the two doses are close, the margin for error is the whole issue.
THREAD: pharmacy (a narrow therapeutic index requiring monitoring),
  food (a vitamin toxic in excess), farming (a fertiliser that locks
  another nutrient).
ASKED-AS: copper poisoning sheep cattle minerals bolus overdose lick toxic liver sudden deaths supplement

ESSENCE: feed is most of the cost of keeping any animal — commonly
  two thirds or more — so anything that changes how much they eat
  changes the whole business. That is why lameness, bad air, a slow
  water trough, a bully at the feed face and a badly designed barrier
  are all financial matters and not only welfare matters: they all
  reduce intake.
ROOT: money / where one input dominates the cost, everything that
  affects its use is a lever on the result.
THREAD: engineering (a plant judged by its yield on the main
  feedstock), medicine (nutrition deciding recovery), money (the
  largest line in a budget deserving most attention).
ASKED-AS: feed cost profit intake eating less trough space bullying ration barrier margin dry matter

ESSENCE: the breeding date is the beginning of everything. Feed
  demand, labour, housing, cash flow, sale dates and the vet's visits
  are all counted forward from it, so a herd whose calving or
  lambing has slipped by six weeks has every other part of the year
  in the wrong place — and getting it back costs a whole season
  because the only way to move a birth is to skip a cycle.
ROOT: the living world / the reproductive cycle has a fixed length
  that no management changes, so the calendar is the animal's and
  not the farmer's.
THREAD: farming (a crop's whole plan back-calculated from a date),
  logistics (a schedule built backwards from a sailing), money (a
  cash flow determined by when the income lands).
ASKED-AS: calving lambing block spread late breeding date season planning cycle slipped calendar farm

ESSENCE: many farm animals breed by day length rather than by
  temperature or feeding — sheep and goats as the days shorten,
  horses as they lengthen — so the season is set by the sun and not
  by the shepherd. Feeding harder in the wrong month does not bring
  it forward; only light, or the hormones that imitate light, will.
ROOT: astronomy / day length is the one environmental signal that is
  exactly repeatable every year, so it is the signal a body can
  safely trust.
THREAD: plants (flowering triggered by night length), the living
  world (migration timed by the sun), the human journey (birth
  seasonality still faintly visible in people).
ASKED-AS: sheep tupping season autumn day length breeding out of season horses spring light artificial

ESSENCE: almost all of the growth in a pregnancy happens in the last
  third, and so does almost all of the demand. A dam fed as though
  she were carrying nothing until the final weeks arrives at birth
  with a small weak offspring, poor colostrum and no reserves for
  milk — and none of those three can be corrected afterwards.
ROOT: the living world / a fetus grows fastest when it is largest,
  so the mother's requirement rises steeply at the end rather than
  evenly across the term.
THREAD: the human journey (nutrition in pregnancy affecting a whole
  life), building (a foundation decided before anything visible),
  farming (a crop's yield set at flowering).
ASKED-AS: pregnant ewe cow feeding last weeks scanning twins birth weight colostrum weak lambs nutrition

ESSENCE: hard births are mostly made months earlier. A dam too fat
  lays fat inside the pelvis and lacks the strength to push; a sire
  chosen for size gives a calf too big for a first-time mother; a
  young female bred before she is grown has a pelvis that is not
  finished. The difficult birth at three in the morning was a
  decision taken in the summer.
ROOT: engineering / a fit between two dimensions is settled when the
  dimensions are chosen, not when they meet.
THREAD: medicine (obstructed labour and the pelvis it was born
  with), building (a component ordered to the wrong dimension),
  design (a tolerance problem discovered at assembly).
ASKED-AS: difficult calving heifer fat easy calving bull selection birth weight assisting pelvis young breeding

ESSENCE: the commonest mistake in a birthing shed is helping too
  soon. The passage opens on its own timetable and pulling before it
  has opened tears the mother and damages the offspring, while
  waiting past the point where progress has stopped kills both. The
  skill is not in the pulling, it is in knowing the normal length of
  each stage and watching for progress rather than for time.
ROOT: the body / a birth is a sequence of stages with their own
  durations, so the useful question is whether it is advancing, not
  how long it has been.
THREAD: medicine (the same argument in human obstetrics), craft (a
  material that must be allowed to reach a state), management (an
  intervention made before a process has had time to work).
ASKED-AS: calving lambing when to help pulling too early progress stages waiting vet ring dilation

ESSENCE: a newborn farm animal is born with no immunity at all — its
  mother's antibodies never crossed the placenta — so it must drink
  a large amount of colostrum in the first hours, while its gut can
  still take whole antibodies through the wall. That door begins
  closing within six hours and is shut by about a day, and nothing
  reopens it.
ROOT: the body / the newborn gut is briefly permeable so that
  immunity can be handed over, and that permeability is a hazard
  which is closed as soon as it has served.
THREAD: medicine (a treatment window that closes), law (a right lost
  by missing a deadline), computing (a boot-time key exchange that
  cannot be repeated).
ASKED-AS: colostrum first feed calf lamb hours antibodies tubing quantity failure passive transfer late

ESSENCE: the antibodies a mother gives protect the young and also
  block the vaccine, because they neutralise the vaccine exactly as
  they would neutralise the disease. So there is a period during
  which the young are protected and cannot be immunised, followed by
  a gap in which the borrowed protection has faded and their own has
  not yet been built — and that gap is when they get ill.
ROOT: the body / a passive defence cannot tell a vaccine from an
  infection, so protection and the ability to learn are in
  opposition.
THREAD: public health (infant vaccination schedules built around the
  same fact), the mind (a strongly held belief blocking new
  learning), computing (a cache serving stale answers and preventing
  a refresh).
ASKED-AS: vaccinating calves young maternal antibody timing weeks age booster failed protection gap schedule

ESSENCE: weaning is not one stress, it is four arriving together —
  a change of diet, the loss of the mother, mixing with strangers,
  and often a move or a transport at the same time. Each on its own
  is manageable; together they produce the pneumonia and the scour
  that the shed is famous for. The whole craft is to separate them
  by a week or two apiece.
ROOT: the body / stresses combine, and an animal handling several at
  once has to spend on all of them from one budget.
THREAD: the human journey (life events clustering and illness
  following), engineering (loads combining on one member), management
  (a reorganisation, a new system and a deadline in one quarter).
ASKED-AS: weaning stress calves pigs mixing pneumonia scour castration vaccination transport same day spreading

ESSENCE: nearly all the worms are outside the animal. The great
  majority of a parasite population at any time is eggs and larvae on
  the pasture, waiting — so dosing every animal in the herd and
  turning them back onto the same field treats a small fraction of
  the problem and re-infects them by teatime. Parasite control is
  grazing management with a drug as a tool, not the other way round.
ROOT: the living world / a population lives where its numbers are,
  and killing the visible minority leaves the reservoir untouched.
THREAD: farming (rotation aimed at a pest's life cycle), public
  health (treating people while the water supply stays contaminated),
  cleaning (wiping a surface that is being re-contaminated).
ASKED-AS: worms drenching sheep pasture reinfection grazing rotation eggs larvae dosing clean fields wormer

ESSENCE: every animal dosed is a lottery run for resistance: the
  worms that survive are the ones the drug could not kill, and if
  the whole flock is dosed and moved to clean ground, those
  survivors are the only worms breeding there. The population that
  results is descended entirely from the resistant. Leaving some
  animals untreated is what keeps a supply of ordinary worms in the
  mix and keeps the drug working.
ROOT: heredity / selection acts on whoever survives, so a treatment
  applied to everyone is the strongest possible selection for
  resisting it.
THREAD: medicine (antibiotic stewardship on the identical logic),
  farming (a pesticide breeding its own defeat), strategy (a
  defence that trains the attacker).
ASKED-AS: wormer not working resistance drenching whole flock refugia leaving untreated rotating drugs testing faecal

ESSENCE: within any group, a few animals carry most of the parasite
  burden and most carry very little. So treating the ones that need
  it — identified by growth, by condition, by the paleness of the
  eye membrane, by a dung sample — protects those animals, saves
  most of the drug, and leaves the untreated majority preserving the
  drug's future usefulness. The same decision serves the animal and
  the flock.
ROOT: the living world / burdens in a population are distributed
  very unevenly, so uniform treatment is mostly wasted and
  occasionally insufficient.
THREAD: medicine (targeting treatment rather than treating a
  population), logistics (a few lines carrying most of the value),
  teaching (help directed where it changes something).
ASKED-AS: worm burden few animals selective treatment eye colour weighing dung sample flock testing targeted

ESSENCE: the fly, the tick, the louse and the mite run on
  temperature and season, so external parasites are a calendar
  problem. They arrive at a predictable time, they build over weeks
  and they spread the diseases they carry as they go — which means
  the treatment is timed against their emergence and not against the
  moment the animals are visibly bothered, by which point the season
  is half over.
ROOT: the living world / a cold-blooded parasite's life cycle runs
  at a rate set by warmth, so its year is set by the weather.
THREAD: farming (spraying timed against an insect's emergence),
  public health (mosquito control aimed at standing water), weather
  (a season as a biological calendar).
ASKED-AS: flies ticks lice summer season pour on treatment timing maggots strike sheep breeding sites

ESSENCE: a vaccine is not a fence around an animal. It usually
  reduces the severity of disease and the amount shed rather than
  preventing infection, it takes two to four weeks and often a
  second dose to work, and it protects against the strains it was
  made from. So it must be given before the risk period, to a healthy
  animal, on a plan — never as a response to an outbreak already
  under way.
ROOT: the body / immunity is learned, and learning takes time that
  the disease does not allow once it has arrived.
THREAD: public health (a seasonal campaign that must precede the
  season), computing (a patch applied before rather than after),
  building (a defence installed ahead of the flood).
ASKED-AS: vaccination programme timing before outbreak booster weeks working failed protection sick animals plan schedule

ESSENCE: the cheapest medicine on any farm is the gate. Most new
  disease arrives by a countable set of routes — bought-in animals,
  shared equipment, vehicles, visitors' boots, neighbouring stock
  over a fence, water, feed, vermin and birds — and each of those can
  be closed by an ordinary practical measure. It is unglamorous, it
  is never credited, and it outperforms everything in the medicine
  cabinet.
ROOT: chance+risk / keeping a hazard out is cheaper than managing it
  once inside, and the routes in are few enough to be listed.
THREAD: public health (border and hospital infection control),
  computing (perimeter hygiene against an infected machine inside),
  farming (clean seed and clean tools).
ASKED-AS: biosecurity boots visitors buying in disease fence neighbours vehicles closed herd preventing outbreak

ESSENCE: an animal that has just arrived is the single most likely
  source of a new disease, and it will look completely well while
  incubating one. So new arrivals are kept apart, watched, tested and
  treated for the known travellers before they ever touch the
  resident group — for weeks, not days, because that is how long the
  incubation of the diseases you are worried about actually is.
ROOT: the living world / an infection has a silent period, so
  appearance at arrival carries no information at all.
THREAD: public health (quarantine as the oldest measure there is),
  computing (a new device isolated until scanned), logistics (goods
  inspected before entering a store).
ASKED-AS: new animals quarantine isolation bought in weeks testing incubation separate group boots disease introduced

ESSENCE: many animals recover from a disease and go on carrying it
  for life, shedding it quietly at times of stress — around calving,
  at mixing, at a change of feed. So the healthy-looking animal in
  the corner can be the reservoir the whole herd is being reinfected
  from, and a herd problem that keeps coming back after treatment is
  usually one of these rather than a failure of the treatment.
ROOT: the living world / an organism that can persist in a host
  without killing it has found a better strategy than one that
  cannot.
THREAD: public health (asymptomatic carriage and the difficulty of
  eradication), computing (a dormant infection reactivating), people
  together (a problem that returns because its cause was never the
  one addressed).
ASKED-AS: keeps coming back disease carrier healthy looking shedding stress testing herd persistent eradication reinfection

ESSENCE: the animals that look ill are a small part of the cost. The
  larger part is the ones that never look ill and simply grow more
  slowly, give less milk, take longer to get in calf and go for
  slaughter a month late — a lung damaged by pneumonia at three
  months old, a mild lameness, a worm burden below the level that
  causes scour. It is invisible and it is most of the loss.
ROOT: money / a loss spread thinly over many animals is larger than
  a loss concentrated in a few, and far harder to see.
THREAD: medicine (a chronic condition costing more than an acute
  one), engineering (small continuous losses beating rare failures),
  money (a slow leak against a single theft).
ASKED-AS: growth rates poor not sick subclinical loss milk yield lameness worms performance hidden damage

ESSENCE: filling a building, emptying it completely, cleaning and
  drying it, and then filling it again breaks the chain of infection
  between one group and the next. Trickling new animals into a
  building that is never empty means the youngest always meet
  whatever the oldest are carrying, and the building's disease load
  climbs year on year until it must be depopulated anyway.
ROOT: the living world / an organism needs a continuous supply of
  hosts, so an interruption with a clean and dry period is a break
  it cannot cross.
THREAD: farming (rotation removing a pest's host for a season),
  public health (a ward closed and deep-cleaned), computing
  (rebuilding a machine from clean rather than repairing it).
ASKED-AS: all in all out continuous flow pens cleaning disinfecting empty drying batch pigs calves

ESSENCE: lameness is the commonest serious welfare failure in kept
  animals and it is nearly always made by the surfaces and by the
  standing. Long hours on concrete, poor cubicle design that
  discourages lying down, wet underfoot, sharp stones on a track,
  and too little time at rest — those five make most of it, and the
  foot is where they are paid for.
ROOT: the body / feet are loaded continuously, so any fault in the
  ground or in the time spent on it accumulates without pause.
THREAD: the human journey (foot problems from a job spent standing),
  building (a floor deciding the health of everyone on it), sports
  (surfaces causing most chronic injury).
ASKED-AS: lame cows limping feet trimming tracks concrete cubicles lying times scoring hooves stones

ESSENCE: a hoof grows continuously and wears continuously, and
  whether the animal is comfortable depends on those two rates
  matching. Soft wet ground and no work means growth outruns wear and
  the foot deforms; hard abrasive ground means wear outruns growth
  and the sole thins to the quick. Trimming is only ever a
  correction to a mismatch that the ground has caused.
ROOT: the body / a growing structure in constant contact reaches a
  shape set by the balance of growth against wear.
THREAD: the body (fingernails and teeth shaped by use), engineering
  (a wear surface designed against its duty), horses (shoeing
  existing entirely because of this mismatch).
ASKED-AS: hoof trimming overgrown feet horn wear ground wet hard sole thin schedule farrier

ESSENCE: the udder is an opening straight into the body, held a foot
  above whatever the animal lies on and opened twice a day by a
  machine that goes from animal to animal. So mastitis is not
  fundamentally a disease of cows, it is a disease of the bedding,
  the milking routine and the machine — and it is treated in the
  parlour and the shed long before it is treated with a tube.
ROOT: the living world / infection follows contact and opportunity,
  so a route in that is opened regularly is the route that matters.
THREAD: medicine (catheter and line infections following the same
  logic), cleaning (a route of contamination rather than a dirty
  object), engineering (a machine as a vector between users).
ASKED-AS: mastitis cows cell count milking machine liners bedding teat dipping treatment recurring parlour hygiene

ESSENCE: prey animals hide pain, because in the wild the one that
  limps is the one that is taken. So an animal in real pain may
  stand still, eat a little, and make no noise at all — and the
  absence of obvious signs is not evidence of comfort. This single
  fact is why pain relief was under-given in farm and equine
  practice for a century.
ROOT: the living world / a signal that attracts predators is
  selected against, so the display of weakness is suppressed
  precisely when the weakness is greatest.
THREAD: the mind (people concealing distress in some settings),
  medicine (pain under-treated in patients who cannot report it),

ESSENCE: what a painful animal changes is its behaviour, and the

  stands with its weight elsewhere, it eats in shorter bouts, it

ESSENCE: every animal has a distance at which it becomes
  uncomfortable and moves away, and a point on its shoulder in front
  of which you make it back up and behind which you make it go
  forward. Working with those two facts moves animals quietly with no
  noise, no stick and no running — and working against them produces
  everything people blame on stubbornness.

ESSENCE: animals will not walk into a dark place, will balk at a
  shadow, a puddle, a drain, a flapping coat or a change from light
  to dark floor, and they much prefer to go back the way they came
  and to follow one another. A race that curves so they cannot see
  the end, with solid sides and even light, moves stock faster than
  any amount of shouting — and shouting itself is one of the worst
  offenders, because high-pitched noise frightens them more than
  movement does.
ROOT: the living world / an animal built to detect danger treats
  every discontinuity as a possible threat, so the design of the
  space is the design of the behaviour.
THREAD: architecture (crowd flow designed by sightlines and
  lighting), the mind (a small obstacle stopping a process), teaching

ESSENCE: calm handling is not only kindness, it is a number. Animals
  handled roughly eat less, gain less, take longer to get in calf,
  bruise more and are harder to handle next time; the same animals
  handled quietly by people they know come to the gate rather than
  away from it. Here the welfare argument and the production
  argument are the same argument, and they do not need reconciling.
ROOT: the body / fear costs energy and suppresses growth, digestion
  and reproduction, all through the same hormones.
THREAD: people together (a workplace's turnover following how people
  are treated), teaching (fear reducing what is learned), sport (a

ESSENCE: transport harms animals mostly at its ends and not in the
  middle. Gathering, fasting, mixing with strangers, loading up a
  ramp, and unloading into a strange pen do most of the damage; the
  hours on a smooth road do comparatively little. So journey time is
  the wrong thing to argue about first — the number of loadings and
  the mixing are the things that matter.
ROOT: the body / stress comes from novelty, handling and conflict,
  and a moving vehicle supplies less of all three than a loading
  yard does.
THREAD: logistics (handovers dominating a journey's time and
  damage), the human journey (the airport being worse than the
  flight), medicine (a patient transfer as the risky moment).

ESSENCE: humane slaughter is a technical matter with one
  requirement: the animal must be made instantly unconscious, and
  must not recover before it dies. Everything in the design — the
  stunner, the restraint, the operator's training, the checking of
  signs, the backup device — exists to make that one event reliable.
  The killing is not the welfare question; the stun is.
ROOT: right+wrong / where death is accepted, the moral weight falls
  entirely on whether there was experience of it.
THREAD: medicine (anaesthesia judged by unconsciousness and its
  monitoring), law (a procedure specified because outcomes cannot be
  checked afterwards), engineering (a critical function with a
  backup and a verification step).

ESSENCE: stress before slaughter is written into the meat. An animal
  exhausted over hours uses up the muscle sugar that should have
  turned to acid after death, and the meat stays dark, firm, dry and
  spoils quickly; an animal frightened in the last minutes produces
  the opposite fault, pale and watery. Both are visible in the
  chiller, and both are records of what happened to the animal.
ROOT: chemistry / muscle after death continues the chemistry it was
  running while alive, so its condition at the end determines the
  product.
THREAD: cooking (why a cut behaves as it does), evidence (a physical
  record of a process nobody witnessed), sport (physiology showing in
  the outcome).

ESSENCE: diseases cross between animals and people, and they cross
  at the points of closest contact — at birthing, in handling dung,
  in drinking unpasteurised milk, in a bite or a scratch, in the
  live market and the slaughter hall. The route is nearly always
  mundane and the protection is nearly always mundane too: gloves,
  hand washing, keeping food out of animal areas, and pregnant women
  away from lambing.
ROOT: the living world / an organism that can use two hosts will,
  and the crossing happens wherever the two hosts touch.
THREAD: public health (most new human diseases arriving from
  animals), cleaning (hand washing as the single largest measure),

ESSENCE: bacteria that learn to survive an antibiotic in an animal's
  gut do not stay in the animal. They leave on the meat, in the
  slurry that goes on the land, on the boots and hands of the people
  who work there — and the gene that made them resistant can be
  handed sideways to bacteria that infect people. Using these drugs
  routinely, to prevent rather than to treat, is the practice that
  drives it hardest.
ROOT: heredity / resistance genes move between bacterial species,
  so a reservoir built anywhere is a reservoir available everywhere.
THREAD: medicine (hospital stewardship on the same logic), farming
  (a pesticide breeding its own defeat), governance (a cost imposed
  on people outside the transaction).

ESSENCE: an animal treated with a drug carries traces of it in its
  meat, milk or eggs for a known period afterwards, so every
  treatment starts a clock during which that animal's produce must be
  kept out of the food supply. The period belongs to the drug, the
  dose and the species, and missing it contaminates a whole bulk
  tank or a whole batch.
ROOT: chemistry / a substance leaves a body at a rate set by its own
  chemistry, so the wait is a property of the drug and not a matter
  of judgement.
THREAD: pharmacy (a drug's half-life setting dosing intervals), law
  (a rule that only works with records), food (one contaminated
  batch spoiling a whole tank).

ESSENCE: the commonest failure in ending an animal's life is doing

ESSENCE: nothing in husbandry can be judged without records,

ESSENCE: the primary diagnostic instrument on any farm is a person

  inspection, and the early quiet signs stop being seen at all.

ESSENCE: pushing hard on production traits drags the fitness traits
  the other way, because they are connected. The very high-yielding
  dairy cow is harder to get in calf, more prone to lameness and
  metabolic disease, and lasts fewer lactations; the fastest-growing
  broiler has more leg problems. It is not a moral failure of
  breeders, it is a correlation, and it has to be bred against
  deliberately.

  selecting on one moves others whether or not anyone selected them.
THREAD: farming (a high-yield crop that needs the whole package),
  engineering (a design optimised on one measure failing on others),

ESSENCE: on a farm the value of the individual sets the ceiling on

THREAD: public health (population measures against individual

ESSENCE: deciding which animals leave is the strongest and cheapest
  health tool on a farm, and it is used least because it feels like

  genetics that produced them, all in one decision.

ESSENCE: almost everything a farm vet treats was caused by the

  on, the bed it lies in, the feed it was moved onto, the group it

  the one that walks the buildings and reads the records, and the
  treatment is only what has to be done while the cause is put
  right.
ROOT: farming / an animal is a system in an environment, so its
  health is mostly a property of the environment.
THREAD: public health (housing, water and air determining health far
